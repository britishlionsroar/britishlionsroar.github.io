Higher Taxes, Tighter Regulations, Fewer Landlords: Who Pays the Price?

Labour promised a new era for Britain's renters. But with rental properties disappearing from the market and rents forecast to rise twice as fast as house prices, tenants could find themselves paying for the reforms intended to protect them.

By Steve English • Independent British writer and editor • 27 November 2026

Britain's renters face another financial squeeze.

New research from property specialists Hamptons forecasts that rents on newly agreed tenancies will rise by 14.2% between 2026 and 2029, almost twice the predicted increase in house prices.

The number of homes available to rent has fallen by more than a quarter compared with 2016. In London, the figure is 39%.

Against this backdrop, the Government is increasing taxation on landlords while introducing the biggest overhaul of private tenancy legislation in decades.

The landlord tax squeeze

From April 2027, landlords face the following property income tax rates:

  • Basic rate: 20% rising to 24%.
  • Higher rate: 40% rising to 44%.
  • Additional rate: 45% rising to 49%.

The basic rate is increasing by a fifth.

These changes follow years of additional taxation and restrictions imposed under successive Conservative and Labour governments, including restrictions on mortgage interest tax relief and increased stamp duty charges.

But who ultimately bears that cost?

The Government's own watchdog has warned about rising rents

The Office for Budget Responsibility addressed precisely this issue in its October 2026 Economic and Fiscal Outlook.

Its assessment identifies the risk that declining financial returns will discourage new landlords from entering the market and encourage existing landlords to leave.

With fewer properties available, remaining landlords may be better positioned to pass increased costs on to tenants.

This is not merely an objection raised by landlord associations. It is a risk identified by Britain's independent fiscal watchdog.

Stronger rights, but fewer homes?

Labour's Renters’ Rights Act introduced major changes on 1 May 2026.

Section 21 no-fault evictions have been abolished, fixed-term assured tenancies replaced with periodic arrangements, and new rules introduced governing rent increases and possession proceedings.

Greater security for tenants is a legitimate objective. Nobody should have to live in dangerous accommodation or face unlawful eviction.

But stronger tenancy rights cannot, by themselves, produce more homes.

If providing rental accommodation becomes less financially attractive, the Government must also consider the consequences for people who cannot afford to buy.

Another customer enters the rental market

While private tenants compete for accommodation, Home Office contractors are also sourcing residential properties for asylum accommodation.

Contractors such as Serco advertise long-term leasing arrangements, continuous rental payments, property management and maintenance services to landlords.

More than 72,000 asylum seekers are currently living in dispersed accommodation, principally houses and flats.

For landlords facing rising costs and changing tenancy regulations, government-backed accommodation contracts may represent an attractive alternative to conventional private tenancies.

The precise number of properties leased under these arrangements remains an important question.

The people caught in the middle

Private landlords provide accommodation for millions of people who cannot afford to purchase their own homes and who may have little prospect of obtaining social housing.

England already has more than 1.3 million households on social housing waiting lists.

When a rental property leaves the market, its former occupants still need somewhere to live. A sale may benefit a prospective homeowner, but it does not necessarily help a family unable to obtain a mortgage.

The Government faces a difficult balance: protecting tenants without discouraging the investment needed to provide their homes.

The success of its reforms should ultimately be measured not simply by the rights contained in legislation, but by whether ordinary people can find and afford somewhere to live.
