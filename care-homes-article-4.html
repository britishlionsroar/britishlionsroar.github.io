The Reform Solution

Three decades of political failure, £12 billion in future taxpayer liabilities, and an £18.5 billion annual case for change. Here is the five-year plan that could actually rescue British social care — and why only one party is pointing in the right direction.

The care home visa route for new overseas applicants was formally closed on 22 July 2025. It is the most significant structural change to the sector in years. It is also, on its own, completely insufficient.

Closing the immigration tap does not re-open the wage conversation. It does not remove private equity from the ownership of one in eight care home beds. It does not redirect the £1.5 billion that leaks out of the sector annually in debt servicing, management fees, and offshore profit extraction. It does not address the 130,000 British workers who left the sector since 2021 because the pay was not worth the work. And it does not prevent the sector from continuing to use the same financial model that created the crisis in the first place, just with a smaller and more expensive pool of domestic labour to exploit.

What is needed is not a tap adjustment. It is a structural replacement.

The first step is understanding what the government's current plan actually is, because it is important to be honest about how far short it falls.

Labour's response to three decades of care sector failure is, at its core, two things. The first is an independent commission chaired by Baroness Casey, tasked with designing a National Care Service. That commission will report to the Prime Minister and make recommendations. It is not expected to produce its conclusions until 2028. The second is a Fair Pay Agreement established under the Employment Rights Act, creating a negotiating body to set sector-wide minimum wages and conditions. That body is also not expected to produce binding outcomes until around 2028.

Between now and 2028, the structural ownership model stays exactly as it is. Private equity continues to extract profit. Offshore structures continue to receive debt servicing. Council fee caps continue to suppress wages. The only change is that the care sector can no longer import workers from abroad to fill the gap those wages create — meaning it will either have to compete on pay for domestic workers, or quietly collapse in areas where it cannot.

This is not a plan. It is a managed drift toward a crisis that will arrive whether or not a commission is still deliberating.

“For over three decades social care has been no stranger to manifesto promises. There have been numerous commissions and independent reviews. The search for political will to implement reform remains elusive.”
— The Health Foundation, June 2024

The mechanism that could actually work is not complicated. It has three legislative components, sequenced over five years, each of which creates the conditions for the next.

The first is a British residency and living wage mandate. Parliament passes legislation stating that within five years, all social care facilities in the UK must be wholly owned by British residents — meaning all investors and shareholders, not just operators. Simultaneously, the legislation mandates a sector-wide real Living Wage as the minimum for all care workers, with no exemptions for any ownership structure.

The economic effect of this announcement is immediate and decisive. Private equity firms hold their care home assets because those assets generate a return. The moment British residency is required for ownership and wages are mandated at a level that eliminates their profit margin, the return disappears. Care homes become distressed assets on private equity balance sheets. The firms cannot sell to other international investors because that would also violate the residency law. The pool of buyers collapses to domestic entities — principally local councils and housing associations.

Private equity, facing debt-laden care homes generating no return that they cannot sell at market value to their usual buyers, opts for the fastest exit available. The fire sale begins.

The second legislative component prevents the fire sale from becoming an asset-stripping exercise. This is the loophole that must be closed before the residency mandate takes effect, or the care estate will be demolished.

If a private equity firm cannot sell a care home as a going concern, its next move is to evict the residents, close the facility, and sell the land to a residential property developer. The land beneath a care home in most English towns and cities is worth considerably more as a housing development site than as a care facility. Without legal protection, the sector's physical estate — built and operated over decades — would be stripped out from under the residents who depend on it.

The counter-measure is straightforward: amend planning law so that any land or building registered as operating a care facility cannot be granted planning permission for any other use. This single change destroys the alternative use value of the land. If the building cannot become flats, the only buyers are those who intend to continue operating it as a care facility. That leaves local councils as the only realistic purchasers in the market.

Why the Planning Law Component Is Non-Negotiable
  • Between 2011 and 2023, 98% of the 816 care homes closed by the CQC were for-profit providers — closures driven by the inability to sustain profitability under regulatory pressure
  • When private equity exits a business, the standard financial move is to realise the underlying asset value. For care homes, that means the land
  • Without a planning restriction locking care home land to care use, the residency mandate risks accelerating closures rather than enabling council purchases
  • The planning restriction must be enacted simultaneously with, or before, the residency mandate — not after the fire sale has already begun
Source: CQC closure data cited in The Canary / The Lowdown, November 2025

The third component is the funding mechanism for the council buyout. A dedicated, ring-fenced National Care Acquisition Fund, financed by central government, provides local authorities with the capital to execute Compulsory Purchase Orders at government-assessed distressed prices. These are not market prices — they are the prices that reflect the actual value of assets whose alternative use has been legally eliminated and whose current use is unprofitable under the new wage mandate. Rock bottom prices, paid by councils using public funds, acquiring the physical care estate for permanent public ownership.

The existing NHS Better Care Fund mechanism — which pools NHS and council budgets for integrated care — provides a ready-made expansion vehicle. As Article 3 of this series established, the NHS loses over £2 billion per year to delayed hospital discharges caused by social care capacity failures. Every care home brought into council ownership, staffed properly, and paid a living wage reduces that delayed discharge burden. The savings can be recycled directly into the acquisition fund, creating a self-financing snowball effect: more care homes purchased means more patients discharged means more NHS savings means more acquisition capital.

The Five-Year Managed Buyout — Year by Year
Year 1
Enact the British Residency and Living Wage Mandate. Simultaneously amend planning law to lock care home land to care use. Announce the National Care Acquisition Fund. Private equity begins pricing its exit.
Year 2
Fire sale accelerates as profit margins are eliminated under the wage mandate. Councils begin Compulsory Purchase Order processes for the first tranche of distressed-price homes. NHS savings from improved discharge rates begin flowing into the acquisition fund.
Year 3
First large-scale council-owned homes operational with fully domestic workforces on living wages. NHS discharge improvements produce measurable savings. Domestic recruitment campaigns active. The acquisition fund self-finances the next tranche of purchases.
Year 4
Majority of the care estate in council or non-profit ownership. Offshore structures eliminated from the sector. Frontline staff absorbed into local government frameworks with contracted hours, sick pay, and public sector pensions.
Year 5
100% council-owned, fully domestic care estate operational. Living wage sector-wide. Zero-hour contracts gone. NHS bed-blocking structurally resolved. The care home crisis ends — not deferred to a commission.

No government has attempted this. The question of whether any government will is, fundamentally, a question about political will — and political will is a function of the parties that exist and the platforms they stand on.

Labour's 2024 manifesto committed to a National Care Service but provided no funding mechanism, no ownership reform, and no timeline tighter than a decade. The Casey Commission is the vehicle for Labour's ambitions and it will not report until 2028. In the meantime, private equity stays, wages stay low, and the sector continues to depend on whatever domestic labour it can attract at rates that drove 130,000 British workers away in five years.

The Conservatives, across fourteen years in power, commissioned multiple reviews, delayed the Dilnot care cost cap repeatedly, and used the visa system to allow private operators to import cheap labour rather than reform wages. Their 2024 manifesto offered no structural change to ownership and no serious workforce plan.

Reform UK's 2024 manifesto committed to a Royal Commission of Inquiry into the social care system and to freezing non-essential immigration. The manifesto stated directly that strict limits on immigration are the only way to relieve pressure on public services and increase wages, and called for the social care system to be simplified through a single funding stream instead of the split between NHS and local authorities. These are not fully worked-out legislative blueprints. But they are the correct diagnosis, applied at the correct scale. Reform UK is the only party that has explicitly connected immigration policy to wage suppression in the care sector and called for structural simplification rather than further managed complexity.

“That is the outcome Labour set in motion from 1997 onwards, under Tony Blair, when mass immigration was deliberately embraced as an economic strategy — and it is the outcome the Conservatives later chose to continue.”

The care home crisis is not an isolated policy failure. It is a symptom of a political consensus that ran from 1990 to the present day, shared across every government of every colour, that the cheapest available labour — wherever it came from — was an acceptable substitute for properly funded, properly paid domestic public services. That consensus was wrong. It is demonstrably, calculably, financially wrong, as the three preceding articles in this series have shown in detail.

Reversing it requires a party willing to say so clearly, fund the reversal honestly, and use the legislative tools available to execute it without deferring to a commission that will report after the next election.

Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse the current model of privatised, import-dependent social care. That does not mean its proposals are complete, or that every element of the mechanism described in this article is explicit in its manifesto. It means that the direction of travel — tight immigration, domestic wages, structural care reform, simplified public funding — points toward the solution rather than away from it.

Britons won't die out, but they'll become a minority in their own country, with no control over its future. The care home sector is a case study in exactly how that happens: one policy decision at a time, one low-wage imported workforce at a time, one private equity extraction at a time — until the institutions that were supposed to serve British people serve the financial interests of structures registered offshore.

The solution is available. The economics are settled. The legislative tools exist. What is missing is the political will to use them.

Reform UK — The Published Position on Care and Immigration

On social care: Commence a Royal Commission of Inquiry into the social care system. Simplify social care through a single funding stream instead of the split between NHS and local authorities. A national plan is critical for a sustainable social care system, with tax incentives, VAT breaks and reduced waste.

On immigration: Freeze non-essential immigration. Strict limits on immigration are the only way to relieve pressure on housing, public services, increase wages and protect culture, identity and values. Stop the illegal working scandal and impose significant penalties on companies and directors that breach these rules.

These positions, taken together and applied to the care sector, constitute the only policy platform from any major UK party that points structurally toward the solution described in this series. The mechanism for delivering that solution — the five-year managed buyout — requires the political will to follow the logic all the way to its conclusion.
