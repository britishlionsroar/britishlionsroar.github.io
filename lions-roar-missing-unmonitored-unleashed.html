Section 1: 53,000 People. Vanished Into Our Communities.

In October 2025, internal Home Office data recorded 53,298 individuals who had entered the United Kingdom illegally, been placed under immigration reporting conditions, and subsequently absconded. They stopped reporting. The Home Office lost track of them. They are in British communities. The government does not know where.

This figure was not volunteered to Parliament. It was concealed. Between January and November 2025, ministers answered three separate parliamentary questions about the scale of the absconder problem — and denied, on each occasion, that the information existed. The denial that "no central record" was held was made in September 2025. The document proving otherwise was dated October 2025.

On 9 December 2025, Rupert Lowe MP read the internal document into Hansard. It is now permanent parliamentary record. The three denials are on the record alongside it.

The PAC Chair, Sir Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, put it plainly in June 2026: control of the asylum system had been "all but lost."

Section 2: 736 Convicted Criminals. Disappeared Into Our Streets.

Within the 53,298 are 736 foreign national offenders. These are not people awaiting a first asylum decision. They were convicted in British courts. They served their sentences in British prisons. They were placed on deportation orders. Then they vanished.

Rupert Lowe MP named the categories from the floor of the House: rapists, murderers, paedophiles. The government has not published a breakdown by offence. What is confirmed is that all 736 met the legal threshold for deportation — a sentence of twelve months or more, or a specified serious offence. They are in British communities. They are unmonitored. There is no imminent prospect of removal.

The enforcement data explains why. In 2025: 8,971 arrests, 2,251 detained, 1,087 removed. Seven in every eight people arrested by immigration enforcement were not removed. Between July 2024 and December 2025, 12,322 arrests produced 1,726 total returns. The arrest press releases and the removal statistics tell two entirely different stories.

Section 3: Tens of Thousands of Terrorism Suspects. Living Freely Among Us. Completely Unmonitored.

Counter Terrorism Policing published its Mission Factsheet in February 2025. It is a public document. It uses a specific phrase: "tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest."

A Subject of Interest is not someone casually glanced at. It is a person formally assessed by trained counter-terrorism analysts as a terrorism concern and placed on the official register. "Closed" does not mean cleared. It means deprioritised — the case file has been shut because the resource to keep it open does not exist. The person remains in the community. The assessment of risk has not been withdrawn. The monitoring has.

At any given time, approximately 3,000 subjects of interest are under active investigation. Twenty officers are required for round-the-clock surveillance of a single individual. The arithmetic makes the rest inevitable: tens of thousands of formally assessed terrorism risks live in British communities with no active monitoring of any kind.

The consequence of this is not theoretical. Manchester Arena, 22 May 2017: Salman Abedi — closed subject of interest. Westminster Bridge, 22 March 2017: Khalid Masood — former subject of interest, file closed. London Bridge, 3 June 2017: Khuram Butt — closed subject of interest, appeared in a Channel 4 documentary while under investigation. All three attackers were known. All three were deprioritised. Fifty-six people were killed.

"Tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest." — Counter Terrorism Policing, Our Mission Factsheet, February 2025. A public document. Not a leak.

The official reviews described the triage problem as "intractable." This article argues otherwise: the size of the monitored population is itself a policy consequence. It is not a natural phenomenon. It is the predictable result of decades of immigration policy that has imported a population large enough to generate a counter-terrorism caseload that permanently exceeds the system's monitoring capacity.

Section 4: The Enforcement Fiction — Arrests Are Not Removals.

The government announced record enforcement activity. It did not announce that fewer than 1 in 8 arrests resulted in removal. It did not announce a 3% return rate on small boat arrivals. It did not announce that it had quietly stopped publishing the exit checks data that would have made all of this visible.

Between June 2024 and June 2025, 58,000 asylum claims were refused. Fewer than 11,000 people were removed — a 5:1 failure ratio. Of the approximately 166,000 people who crossed the Channel by small boat between 2018 and 2024, around 4,995 have been returned. Three per cent.

The government's communication strategy was consistent: lead with arrests, suppress returns. Arrest figures went into press releases. Return figures went into quarterly statistical bulletins that received a fraction of the media coverage. In his resignation speech on 22 June 2026, Keir Starmer claimed "a decrease in undocumented migration" without citing a source. The Home Office enforcement statistics had been updated four days earlier. He chose not to engage with them.

Section 5: What Comes Next

Two parliamentary committees reported in the same week of June 2026. The Public Accounts Committee on 5 June. The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee on 23 June. They examined different aspects of the same system. They reached the same conclusion: the government does not know who is in this country.

Exit checks data — the mechanism that would reveal how many people have overstayed — has been discontinued. The Lords committee demanded its immediate reinstatement. There is no shared evidence base between the Home Office, Ministry of Justice, Department for Work and Pensions, and local councils. The Lords called for a triennial migration plan — a basic instrument of governance that does not exist. Between 2019 and Q1 2025, 45,272 asylum cases were closed because the claimants disappeared from the process with no return recorded.

These are not the failures of a department overwhelmed by events beyond its control. They are the failures of a system that has not been required to account for itself, across multiple governments, over many years.
