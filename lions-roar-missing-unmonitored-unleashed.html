The Home Office knew the scale of it. They lied to Parliament about it three times. Then a whistleblower told the truth.

That claim deserves to be held up against the facts his own government produced — and against the facts his own government tried to hide.

This is not primarily a story about Starmer. It is a story about a system that has been breaking for decades, and a government that spent two years making it worse, lying about the scale of the damage, and hoping nobody would do the arithmetic. Two separate parliamentary committees did the arithmetic. A Home Office whistleblower did the arithmetic. This article is the arithmetic.

53,000 People. Vanished Into Our Communities.

As of October 2025, the Home Office's own internal data — labelled "absconder pool" and circulated regularly within the department — recorded 53,298 migrants who had breached their immigration bail or absconded from detention. These are not people whose cases are pending. These are people the state has lost. Their whereabouts are not estimated, not approximate. Unknown.

This figure was not published. It was not disclosed to Parliament. It was obtained by independent MP Rupert Lowe via a whistleblower and read into the Hansard record on 9 December 2025. When challenged to confirm or deny the figures in that same debate, the government minister responded: "We do not comment on unverified leaks."

They didn't say the numbers were wrong. They couldn't.

"The Home Office does not hold any central record of the requested information." — Home Office Minister, written parliamentary answer, 3 September 2025. That statement was false. The record existed. It was being shared within the department regularly.

The deception ran across three separate occasions. On 20 January 2025, asked what information the department holds on absconders, a minister replied that the data "could only be collated and verified at a disproportionate cost." False. On 3 September 2025, asked specifically about foreign national offenders who had absconded after deportation orders, a different minister stated the Home Office "does not hold any central record." False. On 24 November 2025, the Home Office Permanent Secretary was questioned directly at the Public Accounts Committee. No concise answer was given.

Three opportunities. Three failures to tell Parliament the truth. Meanwhile the data was sitting in a document being routinely shared inside the very department denying its existence.

The PAC report published on 5 June 2026 confirmed the systemic reality in the careful language of parliamentary committees: the Home Office "only knows where the 'vast majority' of failed asylum seekers are." The committee called this "shocking and unacceptable." In plain English: they have lost track of tens of thousands of people who entered this country illegally, placed in hotels in our towns, moved into flats in our streets — and they do not know where those people are now.

On 23 June 2026 — the day after Starmer resigned — the House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee published its own report on migration. It recommended, as a matter of urgency, that the Home Office restart publishing data on exit checks — data the government stopped publishing — to clarify how many migrants are actually in the UK and how many have overstayed their leave. A second parliamentary committee, one day later, independently confirming the same catastrophic absence of data.

Nobody knows how many people are here. Nobody knows how many have left. And the government stopped publishing the data that might have told us.

736 Convicted Criminals. Disappeared Into Our Streets.

Within that 53,298 total sits a confirmed subset of 736 foreign national offenders. These are not people who arrived and vanished before their cases were heard. These are people who were convicted of serious crimes in British courts, served time in British prisons, and were then released into immigration detention awaiting deportation. They did not wait. They walked out and disappeared into British communities.

Rupert Lowe, reading directly from the whistleblower document in the Commons chamber, put it plainly: "736 foreign criminals who have been released from prison and then absconded before deportation. Please think about that: 736 criminals — rapists, murderers, paedophiles. They were meant for deportation but have escaped into the community."

These individuals are fingerprinted, on record, known to the state. Convicted. Sentenced. Released. Gone. Living somewhere in Britain — near someone's wife, someone's daughter, someone's mother — and the Home Office has no idea where.

To understand how this is possible, look at what enforcement actually delivers. In 2025, immigration teams conducted 8,971 arrests in illegal working operations. Of those, 2,251 were detained under immigration powers. The number actually removed from the country: 1,087. Fewer than one in eight people arrested by immigration enforcement were ultimately removed. Over the longer operational window of July 2024 to December 2025, 12,322 arrests produced just 1,726 returns.

The government has been loudly publicising its arrest numbers as evidence of enforcement action. They have been considerably quieter about what happens after the arrests. Seven in eight people arrested by immigration enforcement are still in the country. The performance is the press release. The reality is the return rate.

Meanwhile, other countries are doing what Britain says it cannot. Germany is flying convicted Afghan criminals back to Kabul on charter flights. Pakistan has expelled hundreds of thousands of Afghan migrants. Iran deports Afghan nationals in the hundreds of thousands annually. A coalition of twenty European nations is currently negotiating directly with Taliban authorities in Brussels to establish a formal large-scale deportation framework. The EU has added Bangladesh, India, Egypt, Morocco, Colombia, Kosovo and Tunisia to its common list of safe countries of origin — accelerating refusals and returns for nationals of those countries. Bangladesh is one of the top six sources of asylum claims in the UK. It is not on the UK's safe countries list. Britain watches its European neighbours build returns infrastructure while its own enforcement teams arrest people and release seven in eight of them.

Tens of Thousands of Terrorism Suspects. Living Freely Among Us. Completely Unmonitored.

Separate to the missing migrants, separate to the absconded criminals, there is a third category. And it is the one that should make every person reading this stop and think about their own street, their own town, their own family.

Counter Terrorism Policing published a factsheet in February 2025. It states, in their own words: "We have to make difficult decisions every day about which investigations need to be prioritised — meaning there are tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest alongside current investigations."

A "closed subject of interest" is not someone who was investigated and found to pose no threat. It is someone who was assessed as a serious enough extremism or terrorism risk to be brought into the MI5 or Counter Terrorism Policing system in the first place — and was then deprioritised because there were not enough officers to maintain active surveillance. They were not exonerated. Their file was closed because newer, more immediately volatile threats pushed them down the queue. They remain in our communities. Unmonitored. Free to do as they wish.

MI5 has the resources to actively monitor approximately 3,000 individuals at any given time. The wider pool of individuals assessed as potential terrorism threats has been confirmed at over 40,000 since a major internal data recheck in 2020. Tens of thousands of people — flagged by the security services as extremism risks — are living in Britain right now with no active oversight whatsoever.

Nobody placed these people on that list for administrative convenience. You do not make it onto MI5's radar by accident. The threshold for becoming a Subject of Interest is serious. These are people someone in the security services assessed, at an official level, as representing a genuine risk of terrorism or extremist violence. Being deprioritised off the active list does not mean that assessment changed. It means the money ran out.

We already know what this looks like when it goes wrong.

Manchester Arena, 22 May 2017. Salman Abedi murdered 22 people and injured hundreds more, many of them children leaving a concert. He was a former Subject of Interest whose file had been closed. Post-attack reviews found MI5 had received intelligence about his activities that was "not fully appreciated" at the time. The intelligence existed. The capacity to act on it did not.

Westminster Bridge, 22 March 2017. Khalid Masood killed five people. He had previously been a Subject of Interest to MI5. His file was closed due to a lack of current intelligence.

London Bridge, 3 June 2017. Khuram Butt was one of three attackers who killed eight people. He was a closed Subject of Interest at the time — and had appeared in a Channel 4 documentary about jihadists while still under investigation. He was on television. His file was closed anyway.

Official post-attack reviews described this as an "intractable problem." Independent reviewers acknowledged the security services cannot be expected to prevent every attack when tracking this volume of individuals. That is true. But it is also a political choice. The number of people entering the system, driving the triage, forcing files to close, is a direct function of immigration policy. The more people who arrive through uncontrolled channels, the more names on the list, the more files that must be closed to make room, the more gaps through which the next Salman Abedi can walk. This is not bad luck. It is cause and effect.

The Enforcement Fiction: Arrests Are Not Removals

The PAC report of June 2026 confirmed what the individual statistics already showed: there is no functioning strategy, only reactive firefighting. In December 2025, 100,600 people were claiming asylum in the UK — more than double the figure in December 2019. The total work-in-progress caseload stood at 224,700 cases as of June 2024. The appeals backlog nearly doubled in one year, reaching 80,333 pending cases by end of 2025, with an average wait of 60 weeks. The government spent £4.9 billion on asylum in 2024–25, excluding legal aid and local council costs.

The PAC found no clear strategy. No end-to-end accountability. No shared data systems between departments. No evidence that lessons from past failures informed current decisions. A Home Office that "still cannot demonstrate that it fully understands the levers and controls available to it." The PAC Chair, Sir Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, concluded: "At the time of our inquiry, control of it had been all but lost."

The House of Lords committee, reporting the following day, went further on the data question. It found that "woefully inadequate data on migration" was obstructing the ability to make informed policy decisions, and that "in the absence of good data, misinformation on migration and settlement flourishes." Their solution was to call for exit checks data to be restarted urgently, for inter-departmental data sharing to be improved, and for a triennial migration plan. These are not solutions. These are the things a functioning government should already have been doing.

Starmer claimed in his resignation speech that he had delivered a decrease in undocumented migration. The PAC said control had been all but lost. The Lords said the data to even measure the situation has not been properly collected. Two parliamentary committees and one resignation speech. One of them is describing reality.
