53,000 People. Lost. While Ministers Told Parliament They Didn't Exist.

In October 2025, internal Home Office data recorded 53,298 individuals who had entered the United Kingdom illegally, been placed under immigration reporting conditions, and subsequently absconded. They stopped reporting. The Home Office lost track of them. They are in British communities. The government does not know where.

This figure was not volunteered to Parliament. It was concealed. Between January and November 2025, ministers answered three separate parliamentary questions about the scale of the absconder problem — and denied, on each occasion, that the information existed. The denial that "no central record" was held was made in September 2025. The document proving otherwise was dated October 2025.

On 9 December 2025, Rupert Lowe MP read the internal document into Hansard. It is now permanent parliamentary record. The three denials sit alongside it. The PAC Chair, Sir Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, put it plainly in June 2026: control of the asylum system had been "all but lost."

736 Convicted Criminals. Rapists, Murderers, Paedophiles. Disappeared Into Your Neighbourhood.

Within the 53,298 are 736 foreign national offenders. These are not people awaiting a first asylum decision. They were convicted in British courts. They served sentences in British prisons. They were placed on deportation orders. Then they vanished.

Rupert Lowe MP named the categories from the floor of the House: rapists, murderers, paedophiles. The government has not published a breakdown by offence. What is confirmed is that all 736 met the legal threshold for deportation — a sentence of twelve months or more, or a specified serious offence. They are in British communities. They are unmonitored. There is no imminent prospect of removal.

The enforcement data tells you why. In 2025: 8,971 arrests, 2,251 detained, 1,087 removed. Seven in every eight people arrested by immigration enforcement were not removed. Between July 2024 and December 2025, 12,322 arrests produced 1,726 total returns. The arrest press releases and the removal statistics tell two entirely different stories.

Tens of Thousands of Terrorism Suspects. Living Freely Among Us. Completely Unmonitored. By Official Admission.

Counter Terrorism Policing published its Mission Factsheet in February 2025. It is a public document. It uses a specific phrase: "tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest."

A Subject of Interest is not someone casually glanced at. It is a person formally assessed by trained counter-terrorism analysts as a terrorism concern and placed on the official register. "Closed" does not mean cleared. It means deprioritised — the case file has been shut because the resource to keep it open does not exist. The person is still in the community. The assessment of risk has not been withdrawn. The monitoring has.

At any given time, approximately 3,000 subjects of interest are under active investigation. Round-the-clock surveillance of a single individual requires up to twenty officers. The arithmetic produces the rest: tens of thousands of formally assessed terrorism risks living in British communities with no active monitoring of any kind.

Manchester Arena, 22 May 2017: Salman Abedi — closed subject of interest. Westminster Bridge, 22 March 2017: Khalid Masood — former subject of interest, file closed. London Bridge, 3 June 2017: Khuram Butt — closed subject of interest, appeared in a Channel 4 documentary about British jihadists while simultaneously under counter-terrorism investigation. All three were known. All three were deprioritised. Fifty-six people were killed.

"Tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest." — Counter Terrorism Policing, Our Mission Factsheet, February 2025. A public document. Not a leak. Their own words.

The Government Announced Record Arrests. It Did Not Announce That It Was Releasing Seven in Every Eight People It Arrested.

Throughout 2024 and 2025 the Home Office issued press releases announcing enforcement operations. Record arrests. Raids conducted. Businesses visited. The language conveyed a government in command of the situation.

There is a second number that never appeared in those press releases: the number of people actually removed. Between June 2024 and June 2025, 58,000 asylum claims were refused. Fewer than 11,000 people were removed — a five-to-one failure ratio. Of the approximately 166,000 people who crossed the Channel by small boat between 2018 and 2024, around 4,995 have been returned. Three per cent.

In his resignation speech on 22 June 2026, Keir Starmer claimed "a decrease in undocumented migration" without citing a source. The Home Office enforcement statistics had been updated four days earlier. He chose not to engage with them. The government also quietly stopped publishing exit checks data — the metric that would have made the true overstayer figures publicly visible. The House of Lords committee confirmed this on 23 June 2026 and demanded its immediate reinstatement.

Two Parliamentary Committees. Same Week. Same Conclusion: Nobody Knows Who Is In This Country.

The Public Accounts Committee reported on 5 June 2026. The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee reported on 23 June 2026. They examined the system independently. They reached the same verdict.

Exit checks data — the mechanism that would show how many people have overstayed — has been discontinued. The government cannot tell Parliament how many people are illegally present because it has stopped collecting the data that would reveal the figure. There is no shared evidence base between the Home Office, the Ministry of Justice, the Department for Work and Pensions, and local councils. Between 2019 and Q1 2025, 45,272 asylum cases were closed because the claimants disappeared from the process with no return recorded. The government has no triennial migration plan for a system costing £4.9 billion a year.

Public Accounts Committee, 5 June 2026: Control of the asylum system "all but lost." No shared evidence base across departments. 100,600 active claimants. 80,333 appeals pending. £4.9bn annual cost.

While Britain Removed 1,087 People All Year, Germany Was Flying Planes to Kabul.

When pressed on its removal record, the government reaches for the standard defences. Deportation is legally complex. International obligations constrain what can be done. The courts intervene.

Each of these defences contains a grain of truth. None explains what comparable nations — operating under the same international legal framework — are managing to do in the same period.

Germany resumed charter flight deportations to Afghanistan in August 2024, under the same European Convention on Human Rights framework cited by British ministers as a barrier. Twenty European nations formally invited Taliban officials to Brussels in May 2026 to negotiate a mass returns framework. The EU designated Bangladesh — one of the UK's top asylum source countries — a safe country of origin in December 2025. Pakistan expelled hundreds of thousands of Afghan migrants, including UNHCR-registered refugees. Britain has not designated Bangladesh a safe country. Britain was not at the Brussels talks. Britain removed 1,087 people in a full year.

The constraint is not the law. It is not diplomacy. It is not logistics. It is the political decision, maintained across multiple governments, not to pursue removal at the scale the situation requires.
