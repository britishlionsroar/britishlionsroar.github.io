Starmer Mark II finds a fellow U-turn specialist: Anas Sarwar fails upwards

Andy Burnham promised a break with Keir Starmer but already looks like Starmer Mark II. Now reports — still awaiting a formal GOV.UK appointment notice — say he plans to bring in Anas Sarwar: a man who condemned the House of Lords, promised never to join it, promised to remain at Holyrood, lost another election and appears set to be rewarded with a lawmaking job for life.

By The British Lions Roar · 22 July 2026

Anas Sarwar’s reported ascent to the House of Lords is wonderful news for the children of Scotland. It proves that no matter who you are or where you come from, if your father was a millionaire Labour MP, your family has poured a fortune into Labour politics and you spend long enough climbing the party ladder, you too may be handed a seat in Parliament for life.

Multiple outlets report that Sarwar is set to resign as Scottish Labour leader, enter the House of Lords and take a ministerial post in Andy Burnham’s expanded Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade. Some reports place him in a trade brief. At the time of publication, however, no formal GOV.UK appointment notice names Sarwar and the exact post remains unconfirmed.[1][2][20]

This comes after he led Scottish Labour to just 17 seats in May — its lowest Holyrood total since devolution, level with Reform UK — and after he personally lost his constituency contest before returning through Labour’s Glasgow regional list.[3]

In most walks of life, losing badly and walking away from your promises damages your prospects. In Labour politics, it can earn you a promotion.

Birds of a feather: Starmer Mark II finds his man

Keir Starmer left office with U-turns attached to his name like a permanent job title. Newspapers and broadcasters compiled running lists covering winter fuel payments, welfare cuts, digital identity plans, cancelled local elections and other policies announced with great certainty before being watered down or abandoned.[4]

Andy Burnham arrived promising a “circuit breaker” and a new kind of politics.[5] Yet during his journey back to Westminster he had already reversed himself on Brexit and on whether defence spending should sit outside Labour’s fiscal rules.[6] The promised break with Starmer is already looking less like a clean departure and more like Starmer Mark II: a new face, the same habit of taking a firm position until political pressure makes another direction more useful.

Before Burnham had even formally entered No. 10, Labour was forced to row back from briefings suggesting that his government would approve the Rosebank and Jackdaw North Sea developments.[7] Within days of taking office, he was also reviewing the early-release policy expanded under the previous Labour government after a public backlash over the offenders who could benefit.[8]

Now Burnham’s reported pick is another man whose political sat-nav seems permanently set to “make a U-turn when possible”. Birds of a feather, it seems, do not merely flock together. Under Labour, they can be elevated together.

Keir Starmer

Built a reputation for announcing firm positions and reversing them when political pressure arrived.

Andy Burnham

Changed direction on Brexit, borrowing rules and North Sea policy before or immediately around taking power.

Anas Sarwar

Opposed the Lords, rejected a peerage, promised to serve his MSP term and said he would remain leader — then left for the Lords.

Burnham promised to change Labour. His apparent Sarwar pick suggests something else: Starmer Mark II has found a fellow specialist in changing position whenever the next political opportunity appears.

Three promises, one peerage

In July 2022, Sarwar said the House of Lords had “no place in 21st-century politics”. He called it unacceptable for unelected representatives to wield such power and backed replacing it with an elected senate representing the nations and regions.[9]

During the final week of the 2026 Scottish election campaign, he was asked whether he would accept a peerage. He ruled it out and promised to serve a full term as an MSP.[10]

After Labour’s election defeat, he was asked whether he would remain leader. His answer was: “Absolutely. My job is to hold my party together.”[11]

Ten weeks later, he is leaving that party group without a leader, abandoning the full Holyrood term he promised to serve and joining the unelected chamber he said should not exist.

Opposed the Lords.

Rejected a peerage.

Promised to stay.

Lost the election.

Accepted the reward.

Politicians are allowed to change their minds. Voters are equally entitled to notice when the change of mind comes with a ministerial salary, Lords allowances and a seat in Parliament that does not require another election.

Born into Labour’s political family

Sarwar was born in Glasgow in 1983, qualified as a dentist and worked in the NHS before entering full-time politics. He did not arrive as an unknown activist fighting his way through an open field. His father, Mohammad Sarwar, was already a wealthy businessman and senior Labour politician who became Britain’s first Muslim MP in 1997.

When Mohammad Sarwar retired from Westminster, Anas Sarwar became Labour’s candidate in Glasgow Central and won the seat in 2010. In his maiden speech, he paid tribute to his predecessor — his own father — and described it as an honour to follow him into Parliament.[12]

That was not a literal inheritance. Voters still elected him. But pretending it was an ordinary route into politics would be ridiculous. He began with a famous political surname, an established Labour network, a wealthy family and access to campaign support unavailable to almost every young person Labour claims to represent.

Mohammad Sarwar was later considered for a peerage himself, but the nomination was blocked after concerns were raised with the honours watchdog about his tax affairs. That was not a finding of tax wrongdoing, but it prevented the father from entering the Lords. Sixteen years later, the son is taking the family seat on the red benches instead.[13]

The bank of the Sarwar family

The SNP says its analysis of Electoral Commission records found £296,555 in donations from Sarwar relatives and family-linked business interests to Sarwar, his campaigns or Scottish Labour since 2010.[14]

That figure is the SNP’s total from the public records, not a criminal allegation. Political donations from permissible donors are legal. The point is the contrast between Labour’s carefully manufactured image and the reality of Sarwar’s rise.

Labour presents itself as the party through which ordinary working people can reach power. Sarwar’s story is closer to the political equivalent of the bank of mum and dad: family money, family connections and a family seat at Westminster.

He also held a quarter share in the family wholesale company, valued during the 2017 Scottish Labour leadership contest at about £4.8 million. Under pressure over the business and the wages it paid, Sarwar placed the holding into a trust for his three children, saying he would never again be able to access the shares or their dividends.[15]

Wealth does not disqualify anybody from office. Nor does having political parents. But when a millionaire political dynasty receives hundreds of thousands of pounds in family backing and then lectures the country about privilege, equality and working-class opportunity, the public is allowed to laugh.

A career of being returned, rescued and rewarded

Sarwar served one term at Westminster from 2010 until the SNP removed him in 2015.[16] He returned to elected politics the following year as the first name on Labour’s Glasgow regional list and became an MSP.

He lost his first attempt to become Scottish Labour leader to Richard Leonard in 2017. Leonard resigned in 2021 after years of pressure and Sarwar won the second contest.

His first Holyrood election as leader produced 22 Labour seats in 2021, then a record-low 17 in 2026. In that latest election, he lost the constituency he contested and was again returned through the regional list.

Now, rather than remain and rebuild the Scottish party he promised to hold together, he is moving to the only major chamber in Britain where losing votes does not matter because voters are not invited.

What qualifies him to make laws for life?

There is no public examination for becoming a life peer. There is no minimum legislative achievement. There is no requirement to win the approval of the people whose laws you will help write.

The Prime Minister recommends the appointment. The successful nominee receives a title, a place in Parliament and the right to vote on legislation for the rest of his life.

Sarwar’s supporters will point to fifteen years across Westminster and Holyrood, his frontbench positions and his leadership experience. Those are real entries on a political CV.

His critics will point to something else: he followed his father into Westminster, lost that seat, returned through a party list, lost two Scottish elections as leader, broke explicit promises to voters and was then elevated by a new Prime Minister whose own path to power Sarwar helped clear.

That is why the appointment looks less like recognition of an outstanding record and more like the Labour establishment looking after one of its own.

Was there a Burnham deal?

Sarwar called for Keir Starmer to resign in February. Burnham is now Prime Minister. Sarwar is now receiving a peerage and a government post. The SNP says the sequence “reeks of a long-planned scheme”.

No evidence has emerged proving that Burnham secretly promised Sarwar a reward in exchange for putting pressure on Starmer. Reporting from inside Scottish Labour says the government offer developed only recently and surprised some of Sarwar’s closest allies.[17]

That does not make the appointment politically innocent. It makes it politically convenient.

Sarwar would gain an escape route from Holyrood after another disastrous election. Burnham would gain an experienced Labour operator, a visible Scottish appointment and a former internal critic bound by government collective responsibility.

Whether arranged months ago or improvised this week, the appearance is the same: the Scottish Labour leader who helped turn the pressure on Starmer now appears set to be rewarded by Starmer’s successor.

A bridge back to Labour’s lost Muslim vote?

There may also be a wider electoral calculation behind Burnham’s apparent choice.

Labour has been trying to rebuild support among Muslim voters since its position on Gaza caused a sharp collapse in what had long been one of the party’s most dependable voting blocs. Analysis after the 2024 general election found Labour’s Muslim vote had fallen by about 28 per cent, while the party had already begun targeted work to win that support back.[21][22]

Burnham has retained Shabana Mahmood as Home Secretary. She is the first Muslim woman to hold one of Britain’s four Great Offices of State.[23] His reported addition of Sarwar would place another nationally prominent Muslim Labour politician inside the government.

Sarwar is not simply the outgoing Scottish leader. He is a former deputy leader of Scottish Labour, the first Muslim to lead a major UK political party, and the son of Mohammad Sarwar, who became Britain’s first Muslim MP in 1997.[24][25]

No evidence yet shows that Burnham selected Sarwar specifically to recover Muslim votes. But the political symbolism is impossible to miss. Keeping Mahmood in one of the highest offices in the country while bringing Sarwar into government would allow Burnham to present Labour as once again offering visible Muslim representation at the top.

That may help him repair some of the damage Starmer caused. It also risks looking like the same old Labour calculation: treat British Muslims as a voting fold to be won back through prominent appointments while avoiding a more honest reckoning with why so many stopped voting Labour in the first place.

Islamophobia claims and ethnic politics

Sarwar has repeatedly campaigned against racism and Islamophobia and has reported receiving racist abuse. Nobody should excuse abuse directed at him because he is Muslim or of Pakistani heritage.

That does not place his own statements beyond criticism.

During his 2017 leadership campaign, Sarwar alleged that Labour councillor Davie McLachlan had told him Scotland would not elect a politician using an anti-Pakistani racial slur. McLachlan denied saying it. Labour later found no case to answer, while Sarwar attacked the disciplinary process and said he had not been allowed to present his evidence.[18]

The outcome did not prove Sarwar fabricated the allegation, but it left a serious accusation publicly unresolved.

In 2022, at an event marking the 75th anniversary of Pakistan’s independence, Sarwar said:

“We will only truly get real power, not if we just have more Pakistanis sitting in council chambers and parliaments, but actually having more Pakistanis and South Asians sitting in the corridors of power, making the decisions.”

In the wider speech, he spoke of leading political parties and governments and influencing what children are taught.[19]

Sarwar and Labour said the clip was being stripped of context and turned into a racial dog whistle. The context shows he was encouraging greater participation and institutional influence by Pakistanis and South Asians. It does not erase the words.

People are entitled to ask why a politician aspiring to govern all Scots was using explicitly ethnic language about placing one community in the corridors of power and influencing school curricula. The same question would be asked — far more aggressively — if a white politician made the equivalent appeal to a white ethnic audience.

Criticising that speech is not proof of hostility towards every Muslim or Pakistani. It is scrutiny of a politician appealing for power on ethnic lines.

Burnham’s “new politics” looks remarkably familiar

Burnham entered Downing Street promising honesty, collaboration, stronger standards and a break with the failed politics of the past.

His reported choice of Sarwar sends a rather different message.

A politician condemns an unelected institution. He promises never to join it. He promises voters he will serve a full term. He loses an election and promises to remain with his defeated party. Then, within weeks, reports say he is leaving for the institution he condemned and receiving a government job from the man who benefited from the fall of his former leader.

Starmer became known for U-turns. Burnham was performing them before reaching No. 10. Sarwar’s reported move would complete one of the cleanest political reversals imaginable.

Burnham has not broken with Labour’s old politics.

He appears ready to promote one of its finest practitioners.

The question is not whether Anas Sarwar is legally qualified to become a Lord. The system barely asks that question.

The question is why voters should continue believing anything Labour politicians promise when losing elections, abandoning commitments and reversing principles so often lead not to resignation, but promotion.

Labour calls it public service.

To everybody outside the Westminster club, it looks like failing upwards — with a title attached.
