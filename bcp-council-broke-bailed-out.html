Broke, Bailed Out and Paying the Bosses More Than the PM

Technically Insolvent, Still Handing Out Pay Rises: BCP's Lib Dem-Led Coalition

By Steve English • Independent British writer and editor • November 2026
  • BCP Council's own budget papers say it has been “technically insolvent” since 1 April 2025. It owes more than it has in the bank.
  • In that same year, three of its top officers were on pay above the Prime Minister's, and its two top directors each cost the taxpayer over £200,000 once pension is added.
  • The number of staff on £50,000-plus jumped from 581 to 732 in one year, during a so-called spending freeze.
  • Councillors' own allowances rose by £270,000. The Leader's top-up was raised by 26%.
  • The council's borrowing rose by £63 million in twelve months, to £434 million.
  • Residents got a 6.74% council tax rise, above the legal cap, and the whole country is paying to wipe out most of its special needs debt.
PURPOSE: To show, from BCP Council's own accounts and papers, who runs Bournemouth, Christchurch and Poole Council, how broke it really is, and how its leaders kept paying themselves and their top officials more while sending the bill to residents and national taxpayers.

Bournemouth, Christchurch and Poole Council is broke. It says so itself. When it set its 2025/26 budget, the council formally recognised that it was “technically insolvent” from 1 April 2025. In plain English: it owes more than everything it has saved.

So what did the people running it do in the year they declared themselves bust?

They put three of their top officers on pay above the Prime Minister's. They put 151 more staff on salaries of £50,000 or more. They raised their own councillor allowances by more than a quarter of a million pounds. They signed off a £1.75 million sweetener on staff pay. They handed one departing employee a payoff of up to £160,000. And then they went to the government, asked for emergency help, and put up council tax above the legal limit.

This is not a story about one or two fat salaries. It is a story about who is in charge, and what they chose to do with other people's money.

Who runs BCP? The Lib Dems

The Liberal Democrats are the largest party on BCP Council with 28 councillors. They run it in a coalition they call the “Three Towns Alliance”, with Christchurch Independents, the Poole People Party and the Bournemouth Independent Group.

The Leader of the Council is Liberal Democrat Millie Earl. Her deputy, and the man in charge of the council's finances, is Liberal Democrat Mike Cox.

Council officers are not allowed to join political parties. They don't set their own pay. The politicians do. Every senior salary in this article was signed off by a Lib Dem-led council. The same budget papers that admitted the council was insolvent also asked councillors to approve the pay policy for its top officers. When the new Chief Executive was appointed in 2025, on a salary of more than £200,000, it was Millie Earl who said she was “delighted to recommend” him.

How broke is broke?

The main hole in BCP's finances is special needs schooling. For years the council has spent far more on special educational needs (SEND) than the government gives it for that job, and it has run up a debt to cover the gap. A government rule lets councils keep that debt off their main books for now. Take the rule away and the council is underwater.

In 2019 that special needs debt was £3.6 million. By March 2025 it was £113 million. By March 2026 it was £180 million. On the council's own figures it is heading for £380 million by March 2028. Just the interest on it now costs the council £10.5 million a year.

Against that, the council's usable reserves (its rainy-day money) stood at £87 million in March 2025. It owes twice what it has saved. Even the money it set aside for specific jobs shrank by £8.2 million over the year.

And the borrowing keeps climbing. BCP's accounts show total borrowing of £371 million at the end of March 2025. Twelve months later it was £434 million. Its short-term loans, the kind you take out to cover the bills, went from £124 million to £188 million. That's a rise of more than half in one year.

The council's own auditors, Grant Thornton, put it bluntly. They said the council “is not financially sustainable” and doesn't have enough reserves to cope if the government removes the rule that keeps the special needs debt off the books. They warned that if it can't get the debt under control, it may have to issue a Section 114 notice. That is the council equivalent of declaring itself bankrupt.

The auditors say it is not financially sustainable. The council says it is technically insolvent. The bosses got pay rises anyway.

The bosses' bill

The Prime Minister's pay in 2025/26 was £169,344: an MP's salary of £93,904 plus £75,440 for running the country. BCP's draft accounts for the same year show three officers above that line.

Aidan Dunn came from Dorset Council, where his published salary was £150,628. Moving across the border to run a council that had already declared itself insolvent was worth roughly £60,000 a year to him.

Cathi Hadley's pay went up from £175,394 to £181,007. The Director of Finance, the person meant to be guarding the money, got a 10.5% rise, from £126,642 to £139,938. The directors of marketing and communications and of people and culture each got 5.4%, taking them to £118,218.

Add it all up and the council's senior officers cost £1.5 million in 2025/26, pension included.

On top of that, the accounts list 22 payoffs to departing staff costing £495,000. One of them was worth between £140,000 and £160,000.

And in the middle of all this, the coalition signed off an “enhanced” staff pay and reward deal from 1 December 2025. Its own papers put the extra cost at £1.75 million, and said further savings would have to be found to pay for it.

The spending freeze that wasn't

In October 2025 the council announced a freeze on non-essential spending and on filling vacancies. Belts tightened, apparently.

Look at what actually happened that year. Staff paid £50,000 or more went from 581 to 732, a jump of 26%. Outside schools, the number rose from 451 to 580. The £50,000 to £55,000 band alone grew from 170 people to 268. Six non-school staff were on £125,000 to £130,000. The year before, there were none.

Some freeze.

The councillors looked after themselves too

It wasn't only the officers. In July 2025, months after declaring the council insolvent, councillors approved a new allowances scheme for themselves.

The basic allowance every councillor gets went from £15,109 to £16,512, with that year's pay award added on top. The Leader's extra allowance went from £32,730 to £41,280, a rise of 26%. Cabinet members, including the deputy leader, went from £21,820 to £25,800, up 18%.

The council's own register of what was paid out shows the result. Total allowances and expenses for councillors and co-optees rose from £1,460,639 in 2024/25 to £1,730,639 in 2025/26. That's an extra £270,000, up 18.5%.

Millie Earl took £64,227.57. Mike Cox, the man holding the purse strings, took £43,230.68, up from £35,337.96 the year before. A 22% rise for the finance chief of an insolvent council.

Rewarded for failure

The best-paid director runs the department doing the most damage to the budget.

In 2025/26 the council overspent, and its own papers blame “continued growth in the number and cost of children's social care placements”. This year it is worse. At the first quarter of 2026/27, Children's Services was forecasting an £11.8 million overspend, 10% over budget, even after it had been given an extra £9 million.

The council's total forecast overspend for the year hit £27 million. Its own report called that “of a scale unseen at BCP Council” and “a real and significant threat” to the council's survival.

The director in charge of the biggest overspend is the council's highest-paid director, and her pay went up.

Then they came for your wallet

Councils in England can't normally raise council tax by more than 4.99% without a local vote. BCP went to the government asking for emergency help and got permission to go further. Councillors approved a 6.74% rise for 2026/27. A Band D household now pays £1,980.46 to BCP, an extra £10.42 a month. They also agreed £14 million of cuts and scrapped council tax discounts on beach huts. At the same meeting, Mike Cox admitted the council remained “technically insolvent”.

It doesn't stop at Bournemouth. The government has said it could cover 90% of BCP's historic special needs debt, around £165 million, on condition that the council's reform plan is approved. That money comes from taxpayers across the country. Every household in Britain is helping clear a debt run up in Bournemouth, Christchurch and Poole, while the people in charge there gave themselves and their top officials more.

And there's more to come. The council expects to be £38 million short next year. Its budget consultation already assumes another 4.99% council tax rise, and says that filling the whole gap through council tax would mean a rise of around 16%. Cox insists a 16% rise is “simply not true” and that the figure was used for “illustrative purposes”. Residents can decide for themselves how illustrative it feels.

Who pays, and who decides

Strip away the jargon and the story is simple. A Lib Dem-led coalition ran a council into the ground. It admitted it was insolvent. Then it raised top pay, raised staff numbers on big salaries, raised its own allowances, borrowed another £63 million, went cap in hand to Whitehall and put up council tax above the legal limit.

Nobody at the top went without. The people paying are the residents of Bournemouth, Christchurch and Poole, and every taxpayer in the country covering the bailout.

Reform UK has three councillors on BCP and sits in opposition. Every BCP seat is up for election in May 2027. That's when residents get their say on the people who spent their money like this.

Reform's answer to councils like this is simple: open the books. Its national contract with voters pledges to “Save £5 in every £100”, requiring every department to slash wasteful spending, cut bureaucracy and get better value from its contracts. In local government, that means a DOGE-style audit team of volunteer forensic accountants, data analysts, software engineers and public sector reform experts, combing through consultant and supplier contracts, capital spending, procurement, reserves, past audit warnings and whistle-blower concerns. Where Reform runs councils, that work is now official policy. Kent County Council's efficiency strategy commits it to cutting waste and keeping spending focused on the services residents rely on.

Bournemouth, Christchurch and Poole has had the other approach: borrow more, pay more, tax more. In May 2027, voters can choose which approach they want running their council.
