Production & Scale-Up Strategy

This is not a request to buy tooling now

If Anvil likes the concept, this sets out a realistic, staged route from CAD and prototype validation through to proven high-volume manufacturing and UK/European contract supply — without committing to expensive tooling before demand is proven.

The Production Question
A different question at scale

Electrasthetics began as a range that could be produced locally through 3D printing and sold in small quantities to individual campervan owners. If the range stays limited to a few personalised products, outsourced additive manufacturing may remain appropriate indefinitely. If it develops into a common mounting architecture supplied to professional converters, vehicle manufacturers, distributors and other industries, the shared parts must eventually be produced through processes designed for greater consistency, speed and volume.

The answer is not to abandon 3D printing and immediately purchase expensive injection-mould tooling. The answer is a controlled production ladder in which each manufacturing method is used where it makes commercial and technical sense.

Mould the common parts. Print the specialist parts. Extrude the continuous parts.

The Core Manufacturing Principle
Three product types, three production routes

Electrasthetics is unusually well suited to hybrid production because it consists of three genuinely different types of component.

Common Platform Components

Strongest injection-moulding candidates

  • Universal mounting backplates
  • Standard 1/2/3/4-module outer housings
  • Common closure components
  • Standard face frames
  • Common blanking inserts
  • Repeatable mounting clips and rails

Device-Specific Components

Remain 3D printed, even post-moulding

  • Internal adapter inserts
  • Mounting carriers
  • Device-specific bezels
  • Cable-routing adapters
  • Low-volume specialist housings
  • Personalised names, symbols, layouts

Continuous Flexible Components

Natural fit for profile extrusion

  • Consistent continuous lengths
  • Flexible hinges
  • Repeatable locking profiles
  • Supply on rolls or coils
  • Low cost per metre

Stage One
Design and engineering validation

The first objective is not maximum production speed — it's proving the architecture works. Early prototypes should validate real component fit, mounting depth, wiring/terminal clearance, cable bend radius, VHB mounting area, lower locating hooks, upper cantilever catches, removal and servicing, closure tolerances, repeated opening cycles, dimensional consistency, installation time, hot-cabin behaviour, vibration resistance and final appearance.

FDM Printing

Inexpensive dimensional trials, rapid design changes, fit checks, early backplates/inserts, installer feedback. Visible layer lines and direction-dependent strength mean it shouldn't automatically be treated as the final production standard.

MJF / SLS Nylon

Stronger functional prototypes, snap-fit testing, more isotropic mechanical properties, small commercial batches, complex geometry without support structures.

Resin Printing

Highly detailed appearance models, photography, trade presentations, surface styling checks. Standard brittle resins shouldn't validate long-life flexible clips unless an engineering resin is selected.

Stage complete only when:

The relevant third-party component has been physically fitted
The housing has been installed on a real panel
The closure has been opened and closed repeatedly
Adhesive/mechanical mounting has been tested
The product can be assembled and serviced as intended
CAD is judged through physical samples, not AI review alone
Stage Two
Low-volume commercial launch

Electrasthetics does not need injection moulding before it can reach customers. Outsourced additive manufacturing provides a low-risk launch route for initial direct retail, custom finishes, product-market testing, specialist controller housings, low-volume router housings, new insert designs, short converter trials, personalised orders and replacement parts — avoiding commitment to thousands of units before demand is known.

Launch production need not be performed by Anvil

Anvil wouldn't necessarily need to purchase printers, employ dedicated operators, maintain machines, calibrate materials or manage failed print runs. A contract additive-manufacturing supplier can quote price per part, batch pricing, material options, finishing, inspection, packaging and repeat-order lead time. The first production decision should compare internal printing, local contract printing, UK industrial additive manufacturing, and overseas contract printing — including labour, rejected parts, finishing, packaging and quality control, not merely filament cost.

Stage Three
Bridge production

There is a large gap between 3D printing and full steel-tool mass production. That gap can be filled by several lower-risk processes.

Aluminium-Tool Injection Moulding

Low-volume runs from pilot production through 10,000+ parts. Protolabs UK's own moulds start from around £995 for simple geometries — real Electrasthetics tooling would depend on size, slides, texture and snap-fit complexity. (Verified: this figure is stated directly on Protolabs' own UK site.)

Vacuum / PU Casting

Silicone moulds and cast polyurethane give small quantities of visually finished parts from a master pattern — suits presentation samples, premium low-volume housings, colour trials. Less suitable for validating exact long-term snap-arm behaviour, since material and process differ from injection moulding.

Reaction Injection Moulding

May suit larger housings/covers at moderate volume where high-pressure injection moulding isn't yet economical. Consider selectively, not assumed for the small shared modules.

The correct process depends on forecast quantity, part size, surface requirement, clip performance, number of variants, required lifespan, acceptable tooling cost and time to market. Manufacturing companies should be asked to recommend the most economical route rather than being told in advance which process must be used.

Stage Four
Proven high-volume production

Conventional injection moulding becomes attractive once a shared component has stable CAD, tested snap geometry, proven demand, repeat orders, forecast volumes sufficient to recover tooling, and limited likelihood of dimensional redesign. Strong candidates: universal backplates, standard outer covers, common 1/2/3/4-module shells, repeatable front frames, standard blanking pieces, high-volume heater-controller housings.

Offers

Low unit cost at scale, consistent dimensions, controlled surface texture, rapid cycle time, repeatable clip behaviour, integrated logos/markings, production-ready packaging and assembly.

Commits To

DFM engineering, mould construction, first-off trials, alterations, texture/finish approval, tool maintenance, minimum production runs, storage, material purchasing, inventory.

Injection moulding should be applied to proven shared parts, not automatically to every Electrasthetics SKU.

The best early production design may not be the design with the fewest individual parts — it may be the one with the fewest expensive mould tools.

Designing to Reduce Tooling Cost
A tool-count reduction review, before CAD is frozen

Separate tooling might otherwise be required for single/double/triple/four-way housings, downlight housing, heater-controller housing, router housing, Wema housing, corner pieces, end caps and specialist trims. Before production CAD is frozen, the architecture should be reviewed against:

Can one common outer shell accept several internal inserts?
Can the visible cover stay identical while only the hidden carrier changes?
Can specialist devices fit a standard shell through printed adapters?
Can 2/3/4-way products share repeated centre sections?
Can left- and right-hand parts be made identical?
Can the same catch geometry be reused across the range?
Can one backplate accept several cover depths?
Can decorative bezels be separate from structural bodies?
Can one mould produce more than one small component?
Can interchangeable mould inserts create variants without a fully separate tool?
Material Strategy for Rigid Housings
The requirement selects the polymer — not the other way round

No polymer should be locked in purely because it's widely used in automotive interiors. Selection must be driven by hot closed-vehicle temperatures, snap-arm strain, long-term creep, repeated opening, impact, vibration, UV exposure, cleaning chemicals, electrical safety, flame behaviour, mould finish, cost, recyclability and bonding to adhesive tape.

ABS

Widely available, good surface finish, economical, easy to colour/texture. Questions: selected grade's heat performance, UV stability, long-term clip fatigue, flame rating, cleaner/oil resistance. Shouldn't be automatically selected for stressed catches without testing.

Impact-Modified ABS

May offer improved toughness for housings and closures while retaining many of ABS's finishing advantages.

PC/ABS

Credible where higher heat resistance and toughness are needed — premium structural housings, snap-fit parts, vehicle-interior products. Suitability still depends on grade, catch strain, wall thickness and moulding design.

Polypropylene

Attractive for repeated flexing, living-hinge behaviour, chemical resistance, lower density. But low surface energy — adhesive bonding may need specialised tape/treatment/primer — and creep must be considered.

PBT, Polyamide & Specialist

May suit particular inserts, catches or electrical components, but may be unnecessary or too costly for the complete visible housing.

Final choice should follow: material shortlist → sample production → clip cycling → hot-soak testing → adhesive testing → chemical testing → cost comparison → moulder DFM review.

Adhesive Mounting & VHB Integration
The concealed backplate needs engineering validation equal to the housing

3M describes VHB tapes for transportation as suitable for exposure to vibration, moisture, UV, heat and cold, and supports precision die-cut pads through converter partners. However, "3M VHB" is a product family, not one universal tape — selection depends on housing polymer, wall finish, timber veneer, laminate, painted panel, powder-coated metal, surface energy, installation temperature, tape thickness, required removability, dynamic load, humidity and service temperature. 3M itself advises application testing and provides selection tools based on substrate and conditions.

Production Options

  • Die-cut tape pads on individual liners
  • Continuous tape in a recessed backplate channel
  • Pre-applied tape by the moulder
  • Application by a secondary assembly partner
  • Consumer-applied replacement pads
  • Optional screw fixing for unsuitable surfaces

Required Tests

  • Clean painted plywood, laminate, vinyl-wrapped panels, GRP, aluminium, sealed timber, textured plastic
  • Warm and cold installation
  • Sustained vertical load, vibration, humidity
  • Removal without panel destruction

The product should never claim universal adhesive compatibility without specifying approved surfaces and preparation.

Decorative Finish Strategy
Tiered by volume, not fixed at launch

Electrasthetics requires more than technically correct plastic — appearance is part of the value.

Low-Volume & Launch Finishes

Printed colour, sanding and coating, automotive paint, vinyl/architectural film, hydrographic transfer, printed decorative skins, separately applied metallic bezels — preserves choice without every finish needing its own moulding batch.

Coloured Masterbatch

Pigment mixed through the polymer pre-moulding. Consistent base colour, scratch damage less conspicuous than paint. Limitations: metallic pigments may show flow/weld lines, gate location affects appearance, colour minimums may apply. Real moulded samples must be reviewed.

Mould Texture

Fine grain, matte, leather-style, satin, patterned surfaces — likely one of the most cost-effective ways to give standard housings a premium, durable appearance.

In-Mould Decoration / Labelling

Decorative film bonded during moulding — wood grain, brushed metal, printed graphics, logos. Most economical on proven, repeated covers rather than the entire launch range.

Hydrographic / Film-Applied

Short-run premium editions, personalised interiors, detailed timber/metal effects. Requires testing for edge lifting, abrasion, cleaning, heat, UV, clear-coat durability.

Flexible Trunking Production
Not a very long 3D print — custom profile extrusion

A custom extruder creates tooling for one continuous cross-section and produces the profile in long lengths — supply on coils, consistent wall thickness, repeatable closure, integrated flexible hinges, colour matching, lower cost per metre, continuous adhesive channels. TP Extrusions produces rigid and flexible custom profiles in materials including TPE and silicone, and can provide dual-hardness extrusions, with design guidance noting co-extrusion can join rigid sections with flexible hinges, lips or flaps.

Firmer Sections

  • Mounting back
  • Cable enclosure
  • Locking tooth
  • Shape retention
  • Straight visual edges

Softer Sections

  • Hinge
  • Sealing lip
  • Corner flexibility
  • Repeated opening
  • Grip

Likely a dual-durometer or co-extruded profile — final material candidates: TPE, TPU, flexible PVC, silicone, or another extruder-recommended compound, selected after review of hinge radius, required softness, closure force, roll diameter, hot-cabin behaviour, adhesive compatibility, flame requirements, and colour/surface finish.

Quotation Pack
What to send manufacturers for quotation

A manufacturer can't give a meaningful quote from concept images alone. The pack should contain:

Product Overview

Purpose, target markets, installation environment, shared architecture, concealed-fixing requirement, service access, finish expectations.

CAD & Drawings

STEP files, dimensioned drawings, wall thickness, draft, clip geometry, tolerances, insert relationships, assembly direction, cosmetic faces.

Material Requirements

Stated as performance needs, not a forced resin — hot vehicle interior, vibration, impact, repeated catch use, VHB suitability, premium finish, flame target, UV exposure, chemical exposure.

Quantity Scenarios

Prototypes, 100 / 500 / 1,000 / 5,000 / 10,000 units, annual call-off quantities.

Tooling Questions

DFM charge, prototype/aluminium/steel tool, single or multi-cavity, interchangeable inserts, tool life, ownership, storage, maintenance, alteration cost, lead time, approval process.

Secondary Operations

Texturing, colour matching, metallic masterbatch, decoration, painting, printing, film application, VHB application, assembly, inspection, packaging, barcoding, fulfilment.

The Most Credible Production Plan
Five phases
01
Validate

Commission professional CAD, print functional prototypes, validate each physical component, test the shared closure, install in real vans, time installation, refine dimensions.

02
Launch

Use outsourced additive manufacturing for launch, sell direct and to selected converter partners, collect real demand data, retain made-to-order finishes and specialist inserts.

03
Quote

Request DFM and bridge-tool quotations for the common backplate and most popular standard housing; request extrusion feasibility/tooling quotations for trunking; test production VHB pads; compare UK and offshore options.

04
Scale Selectively

Injection-mould only proven common components; continue printing device-specific inserts and niche products; extrude proven continuous profiles; introduce moulded-through colour and selected production finishes.

05
Expand

Add multi-cavity or steel tooling when volume warrants it; develop converter and manufacturer call-off supply; establish European distribution; consider licensed production only after the platform is commercially proven.

Not a choice between 3D printing and mass production. A hybrid model, used where each method actually makes sense.

Electrasthetics doesn't need to abandon the flexibility that made the concept possible in order to scale. The commercial strength lies in combining additive manufacturing for development and low-volume variants, injection moulding for repeated common components, extrusion for continuous trunking, die-cut adhesive systems for fast installation, and specialist decorative processes for premium finishes.

The immediate next step is not purchasing machinery. It is completing professional CAD, building physical prototypes, validating the shared architecture, and obtaining comparative quotations from manufacturing partners. Only then can Anvil decide, using real figures, which products should remain printed, which should be moulded, and which justify dedicated production tooling.

Prototype flexibly. Validate physically. Mould selectively. Scale only what the market proves.
