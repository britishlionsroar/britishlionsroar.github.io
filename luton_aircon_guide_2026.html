Right then — what’s this guide actually about?

Quick heads up before you dive in: This guide covers native 12V DC rooftop air conditioners only. These run straight from your leisure battery bank — no inverter needed, no shore power, proper off-grid stuff. They’re the only sensible choice if you’re doing serious wild camping in a Luton.

This guide does not cover 230V mains-powered rooftop units like the Mestic RTA-2500L, Dometic FreshJet, or Truma Aventa. Those are campsite units and a very different conversation. I’ll cover those separately.

Your battery — what the numbers actually mean

Most of you already have a leisure battery. Before the tables below mean anything useful, it’s worth understanding what your battery can actually deliver — because the number on the label isn’t what you can use.

Same number on the label — very different story in real life

AGM and lead-acid — the 50% rule

Traditional AGM and sealed lead-acid batteries can only be safely discharged to 50% of their rated capacity. Push beyond that and you permanently damage them. A 110Ah AGM gives you about 55Ah of real-world usable power. Running a 12V air conditioner from an AGM bank is going to be short, painful, and hard on the battery.

LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) — the game changer

Lithium batteries can be safely discharged to 80–90% of their rated capacity. A 280Ah LiFePO4 — like the Fogstar Drift Pro that many group members run — gives you around 224–252Ah of actual usable power. That’s why lithium has become the go-to for serious off-grid loads.

The simplest way to understand runtime — watt-hours (Wh)

Forget amps for a moment. The most useful number for working out aircon runtime is watt-hours (Wh), usually printed on your battery label. Divide your usable Wh by the Wh-per-hour figure for your chosen unit and you get runtime in hours. One simple division. The tables are built around this — green means comfortable, amber means tight, red means don’t bother.

What most UK Luton self-builders are running

  • Budget / starter: 100–110Ah AGM. About 55Ah usable. Fine for lights, fridge, phone charging. Not viable for aircon.
  • Standard modern build: 200–230Ah LiFePO4. About 180–210Ah usable. Minimum realistic starting point for short-duration aircon.
  • Serious off-grid / aircon build: 300–460Ah LiFePO4 in parallel. About 270–400Ah usable. What you actually want for comfortable overnight cooling.
Solar and B2B charging reality check: Drain 200Ah overnight running aircon and a 100–200W solar setup in typical UK weather won’t replenish it next day. During a genuine heatwave with clear skies you’ll do better, but don’t count on solar alone. A decent B2B charger from your alternator — at least 40–60A — is what actually saves you. Replenishing 200Ah at 50A takes roughly 4 hours of driving.

What difference does solar actually make?

Solar reduces how much your battery works during daylight hours. But it contributes nothing overnight — exactly when you need aircon most. These tables use honest heatwave output figures, not the rated numbers printed on the panels.

Why your panels won’t hit rated output in a heatwave: Panels lose about 0.3–0.5% efficiency per degree above 25°C. At 35°C ambient, panel surface temperature hits 55–65°C, cutting real output by 10–15%. A 400W array with a good MPPT realistically delivers around 260–280W at midday peak on a clear heatwave day — not 400W.

The cheap stuff on eBay and Amazon — let’s be honest

I know some of you are looking at units for £400–£900 and wondering if I’m just steering you toward the expensive stuff. I’m not. Here’s the straight picture.

Refrigerant fraud — this one really gets me:

Several cheap imported units use refrigerants that are either banned in the UK or being phased out under F-Gas regulations due to extremely high global warming potential. Selling them pre-charged is illegal. So sellers ship them dry — no refrigerant — and bury instructions in the small print telling you to charge the system yourself. A current regulatory loophole allows owners to re-gas their own systems, and these sellers exploit it deliberately.

You’re buying a system with a banned or soon-to-be-banned refrigerant, sold by someone who has found a legal workaround. Finding a UK F-Gas certified engineer who will touch one of these units will be very difficult. When it needs work, it becomes your liability.

This is completely different from cheap Chinese diesel heaters, which have proved themselves reliable over years of community use. The moment refrigerant gas is involved, that comparison breaks down entirely.
  • No genuine UK warranty. Shipping a 30kg unit to China at your expense is warranty on paper only.
  • No UK spare parts. When a control board, fan motor, or compressor fails, there is nowhere in the UK to source a replacement. Unit becomes scrap.
  • Fire risk. Unbranded 12V units drawing 40–60A sustained, installed by self-builders, with no independent UK electrical certification and potentially substandard internal protection circuitry.
  • No independent performance verification. BTU and watt figures are manufacturer claims with no testing behind them. Real output is frequently much lower than stated.
  • No UK technical support. A generic overseas email at 11pm with an error code is not support.
  • No reliability data. Without long-term UK owner data in UK conditions, there’s no way to know how these hold up over years of van life use.

Before you spend a penny — passive cooling first

Every unit on this list is a significant cost and a permanent roof modification. Make sure your passive cooling is already sorted before you commit. In a well-insulated Luton with good ventilation and the right habits, a 30°C day can be genuinely comfortable without active cooling.

  • Insulation: The single most impactful thing. Keeps heat out during the day, keeps cooled air in once the temperature is down. A poorly insulated Luton will defeat any aircon unit.
  • Reflective and thermal covers: External reflective windscreen and window covers block solar heat before it enters the van. Far more effective than trying to cool a space that’s been baking all day.
  • MaxxFan Deluxe units: Two in extract mode on a Luton move a serious amount of air. On nights when ambient temperature drops below 20°C, fans alone are often enough. Front intake, rear extract.
  • Parking: Shade is free. Trees, the side of a building, anything. Shaded parking drops interior temperature by 5–8°C. Orient the living end away from afternoon sun.
  • Timing: Vent aggressively in the cooler morning hours. Close everything once outside exceeds interior temperature. Open again in the evening when outside drops below inside. Simple but it works.

This doesn’t replace aircon in a genuine 35°C heatwave — but it dramatically reduces how hard any unit has to work, giving you longer battery runtime, less unit wear, and a much quieter night.
