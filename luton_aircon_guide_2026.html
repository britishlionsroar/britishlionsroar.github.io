Right then — what’s this guide actually about?

Quick heads up before you dive in: This guide covers native 12V DC rooftop air conditioners only. These are units that plug straight into your leisure battery bank — no inverter needed, no shore power, proper off-grid stuff. They’re the only sensible choice if you’re doing serious wild camping in a Luton.

This guide does not cover 230V mains-powered rooftop units like the Mestic RTA-2500L, Dometic FreshJet, or Truma Aventa. Those are campsite units and a very different conversation. I’ll cover those separately another time.

I’ve compared four units: the Dometic CoolAir RTX 2000, Mestic RTA-2000, Indel B Plein-Aircon, and Nomadic Cooling X2. Everything here is sourced from manufacturer datasheets and live UK retailer listings at the time of writing. Prices include VAT unless I say otherwise. Always double-check before you order — prices in this category move around.
Important — read this before you use the battery and solar tables: Every figure in those tables assumes the battery shown is only running the air conditioning unit. It doesn’t include your fridge, lighting, water pump, USB charging, diesel heater, or anything else. You’ll need either spare capacity on top of your current bank, or a dedicated extra battery, to cover aircon on top of your existing daily usage.

Your battery — what the numbers actually mean

Most of you already have a leisure battery. Before the tables below mean anything useful to you, it’s worth understanding what your battery can actually deliver — because the number printed on the label isn’t what you can use.

Same rated capacity, very different real-world story

AGM and lead-acid — the 50% rule

Traditional AGM and sealed lead-acid batteries can only be safely discharged to 50% of their rated capacity. Push beyond that and you start damaging them permanently. So a 110Ah AGM gives you about 55Ah of real-world usable power. Running a 12V air conditioner from an AGM bank is going to be short, painful, and hard on the battery.

LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) — the game changer

Lithium batteries can be safely discharged to 80–90% of their rated capacity without damage. A 280Ah LiFePO4 — like the Fogstar Drift Pro that a lot of group members run — gives you around 224–252Ah of actual usable power. That’s why lithium has become the go-to for anyone wanting to run serious loads off-grid.

The simplest way to understand your battery’s power — Wh

Forget amps for a moment. The most useful number for working out how long an aircon unit will run is watt-hours (Wh). It’s usually printed on the battery label. Divide your usable Wh by the Wh-per-hour figure for your chosen unit, and you get your runtime in hours. No complicated maths — just one simple division. The tables below are built around this. Green means you’re fine. Amber means it’s tight. Red means don’t bother.

What most UK Luton self-builders are running

  • Budget / starter build: 100–110Ah AGM. About 55Ah usable. Fine for lights, a fridge, and phone charging. Not really viable for air conditioning.
  • Standard modern build: 200–230Ah LiFePO4 (Fogstar Drift, Roamer, etc). About 180–210Ah usable. The minimum realistic starting point for short-duration aircon use.
  • Serious off-grid / aircon build: 300–460Ah LiFePO4 in parallel. About 270–400Ah usable. What you actually want for a comfortable overnight cooling session without emptying your entire bank.
Solar and B2B charging — the reality: If you drain 200Ah overnight running aircon, a 100–200W solar setup in typical UK weather won’t replenish that the next day. During a proper heatwave with clear skies you’ll do better, but don’t count on solar alone to keep up with overnight cooling. A decent B2B (Battery-to-Battery) charger from your alternator — at least 40–60A — is what actually saves you. Replenishing 200Ah at 50A takes roughly 4 hours of driving.

What difference does solar actually make?

Solar reduces what your battery has to do — but only during daylight hours

Solar panels reduce how much your battery has to work during the day. But they don’t eliminate the draw, and they contribute nothing overnight. These tables show what you can realistically expect from a UK solar array during a heatwave — and it’s important to use honest figures, not the rated output printed on the panels.

Why your panels won’t deliver their rated output in a heatwave: Solar panels lose about 0.3–0.5% efficiency per degree above 25°C (the standard test temperature). In a UK heatwave at 35°C ambient, panel surface temperature can hit 55–65°C, cutting real output by 10–15%. A 400W array with a good MPPT controller realistically delivers around 260–280W at midday on a clear heatwave day. Morning and evening it’s much less. The cheap stuff on eBay and Amazon — let’s talk about it

Right. I know some of you are looking at units for £400–£900 on marketplace sites and wondering if I’m just steering you toward the expensive stuff. I’m not. Here’s the honest picture.

Refrigerant fraud — this is the one that really gets me:

Several cheap imported units use refrigerants that are either banned in the UK or being phased out under F-Gas regulations because of their extremely high global warming potential. Selling these units pre-charged is illegal. So what do sellers do? They ship the units dry — without any refrigerant charge — and bury small-print instructions telling you to charge the system yourself. There’s a current regulatory loophole that allows owners to re-gas their own systems, and these sellers exploit it deliberately.

In plain English: you’re buying a system with a banned or soon-to-be-banned refrigerant, sold by someone who has found a technical workaround. Practically speaking, finding a UK F-Gas certified engineer who will touch one of these units — let alone service or re-gas it — will be very difficult. When it needs work, it becomes your problem.

This is completely different from cheap Chinese diesel heaters, which have genuinely proved themselves reliable over years of community use. The moment gas refrigerant is involved, the comparison breaks down entirely.

Beyond the refrigerant issue, the consistent problems with cheap imports include:

  • No genuine UK warranty. A warranty that requires you to ship a 30kg unit to China at your expense is not a practical warranty. It’s a warranty on paper only.
  • No UK spare parts. When a control board, fan motor, or compressor fails, there is nowhere in the UK to source a replacement. The unit becomes scrap.
  • Fire risk. Unbranded 12V compressor units drawing 40–60A sustained, installed by self-builders, with no independent UK electrical certification and potentially substandard internal protection circuitry. I don’t need to spell that out.
  • No independent performance verification. The BTU and watt figures are manufacturer claims with zero independent testing behind them. Real-world cooling output is frequently much lower than stated.
  • No UK technical support. A generic overseas email when you’re parked in a field at 11pm with an error code on the display and no English manual is not support.
  • No reliability data. Without long-term UK owner data in UK conditions, there’s no basis for assessing how these units perform over years of van life use. Some may be fine. Many won’t be. There’s no way to tell from a listing.

If cost is a real barrier and you’re seriously considering a cheaper imported unit, drop me a message in the group or reach out directly. I’m happy to research specific models with you and help you identify the ones to avoid and the ones that at least have some decent owner history behind them. You won’t get judged for asking.

But I want to be straight with you — these are not like cheap Chinese diesel heaters. Those have earned their reputation through years of real-world use across the community. Refrigerant gas changes the risk profile completely. If you go down this route, go in with your eyes fully open.

Before you spend a penny — passive cooling first

Sort these out first — they massively reduce how hard any aircon unit has to work

Every unit on this list is a significant cost and a permanent roof modification. Before you commit, make sure your passive cooling game is already sorted. In a well-insulated Luton with good ventilation and the right habits, a 30°C day is genuinely comfortable without any active cooling at all.

  • Insulation: The most impactful thing you can do. Proper build insulation keeps heat out during the day and keeps cooled air in once you’ve brought the temperature down. A poorly insulated van will defeat any aircon unit.
  • Reflective and thermal covers: External reflective windscreen covers and window covers block solar heat before it gets in. Far more effective than trying to cool a space that’s been roasting all day.
  • MaxxFan Deluxe units: Two of these running in extract mode on a Luton move a serious amount of air. On nights when ambient temperature drops below 20°C, fans alone are often enough. Front intake, rear extract, cross ventilation through the living space.
  • Parking: Shade is free. A tree, the side of a building, anything. Shaded parking drops interior temperature by 5–8°C compared to full sun. Orient the van so the living end faces away from the afternoon sun.
  • Timing: Vent aggressively in the cooler morning hours. Close everything once outside temperature exceeds interior temperature. Open again in the evening when outside drops below inside. Simple but it works.

None of this replaces aircon in a genuine 35°C heatwave. But it dramatically reduces how hard any unit has to work — longer battery runtime, less wear on the unit, and a much quieter night.
