Will the Real Tory Please Stand Up?

Rupert Lowe called the Conservatives vipers, took a committee seat they gave him, and admitted he softened his attacks in return. Weeks after ruling out any deal with Reform, he offered Farage a public pact — on his own terms.

  • The Conservatives gave Lowe one of their own Public Accounts Committee places on 21 October 2025, saying he shared their values on cutting waste.
  • Lowe has admitted an "agreement" under which he stopped calling Conservative MPs "Lib Dem Tories."
  • The Telegraph reports, citing two sources, that Lowe privately agreed not to attack Kemi Badenoch's party during committee proceedings in exchange for the seat. The Conservatives deny it.
  • Lowe ruled out any collaboration between Restore Britain and Reform UK on 13 June 2026.
  • On 2 August 2026 he publicly offered Reform a pact, including a demand to exclude former Conservative ministers from any future cabinet — while holding a seat the Conservatives gave him.
  • Within roughly 24 hours of the offer, Lowe was publicly accusing Farage of "outright lying."
PURPOSE: This article is part of The British Lions Roar's ongoing programme of political commentary, news analysis, and investigative opinion journalism. It sets out the documented public record of Rupert Lowe's statements and conduct, and identifies where the two appear to conflict. Readers are encouraged to follow every source link and judge the record for themselves.

Rupert Lowe has spent months telling voters that Britain's political parties cannot be trusted.

He has described the Conservatives as "vipers," dismissed them as little more than a left-wing party in disguise and attacked their MPs as "Lib Dem Tories."

He has repeatedly presented himself as the principled outsider: the man who would never compromise, never cooperate with the failed political establishment and never put personal advancement before the country.

But Rupert Lowe's public performance and his conduct behind the scenes appear to tell two very different stories.

The Secret Conservative Arrangement

Lowe joined the Commons Public Accounts Committee on 21 October 2025 after the Conservatives handed him one of the committee places allocated to their own party.

At the time, the Conservatives publicly explained that they had given Lowe the position because his business experience and views on cutting government waste aligned with their own.

But The Telegraph has now reported, citing two separate sources, that there was more to the arrangement.

According to the report, Lowe privately agreed that he would not openly attack Kemi Badenoch's Conservatives during committee proceedings if the party nominated him for the valuable Public Accounts Committee position.

The Conservatives have denied that any such secret deal existed.

However, Lowe himself had already admitted on GB News that there was an "agreement."

Discussing his relationship with the Conservatives, Lowe said that, as part of his agreement with Kemi Badenoch, he had undertaken to stop calling Conservative MPs "Lib Dem Tories."

So, while Lowe publicly presented the Conservatives as dishonest vipers and encouraged voters to reject them, behind the scenes he was accepting one of their parliamentary committee positions and agreeing to moderate his language towards them.

His rhetoric softened where it benefited Rupert Lowe.

Attacking Reform Instead

Lowe did not simply ease off the Conservatives.

He redirected his fire towards Reform UK and Nigel Farage.

In March 2025, Lowe described Reform's leadership as "rotten and deceitful" and declared that Nigel Farage must never become prime minister.

He continued repeating that judgement after the Crown Prosecution Service decided not to charge him over allegations referred by Reform.

He accused Reform figures of smears, dishonesty and conduct that made them unfit to lead or even to serve as MPs.

Lowe then built Restore Britain into a direct competitor to Reform.

He rejected cooperation between the two parties and stated as recently as 13 June 2026 that there would be no collaboration between Restore and Reform.

The hostility continued into July.

On 7 July, Lowe launched another personal attack on Farage, saying that everything Farage did was about one person: Nigel Farage.

Restore initially announced that it would stand in the Clacton by-election, directly opposing Farage.

Even where Restore later decided not to contest Clacton, Lowe continued publicly criticising Farage, Reform MPs and the party's conduct.

There was no public reconciliation.

No shared statement.

No admission that months of attacks might have been excessive.

No visible attempt to rebuild trust.

Then Came the "Pact"

On 2 August 2026, only weeks after ruling out collaboration and continuing his attacks, Lowe suddenly released a professionally produced public video offering to work with Reform.

He did not begin with private negotiations.

He did not request a discreet meeting with Farage to resolve their differences.

He did not announce that the two men had spoken, reconciled or reached common ground.

Instead, he went directly to the cameras.

Lowe placed himself at the centre of the announcement and publicly presented Reform with a list of conditions.

These included mass deportations, sweeping immigration restrictions, a referendum on capital punishment, large welfare reductions and a rule excluding former Conservative ministers from any future cabinet.

That last demand was particularly remarkable.

Lowe was demanding that Reform exclude former Conservative ministers while he had himself accepted a committee position from the Conservatives and admitted moderating his attacks as part of an agreement with them.

Apparently, working with Conservatives is unacceptable when somebody else does it — but perfectly acceptable when it advances Rupert Lowe's position in Parliament.

An Offer or an Ultimatum?

This was presented as an olive branch, but it bore little resemblance to genuine reconciliation.

A serious attempt to repair such a bitter political relationship would normally begin with private contact, a meeting and some attempt to establish trust.

Instead, Lowe made his approach publicly, surrounded it with demands and ensured that he controlled the first headlines.

Farage said on 3 August that there had been no negotiation and distinguished between being open to a conversation and entering formal talks.

Lowe then accused Farage of "outright lying" about the contact between them.

Within roughly 24 hours of publicly asking Farage to put personalities and past disagreements aside, Lowe was once again attacking him in public.

Yet Lowe continued insisting that his offer remained open.

This was not a carefully prepared reconciliation.

It looked far more like an attempt to attach Restore Britain to Reform's much larger electoral operation while allowing Lowe to retain his own party, his own public platform and his own place in the limelight.

Restore had just demonstrated that it could take enough votes from Reform to damage it — but not enough to replace it.

In the Greater Manchester mayoral election, Reform received approximately 21 per cent while Restore received 8.7 per cent.

Restore could split the vote. Reform remained the considerably larger political vehicle.

Days later, Lowe offered cooperation — but only publicly and on his own declared terms.

The Pattern

The issue is not simply that politicians sometimes change their minds.

The issue is the repeated difference between what Rupert Lowe tells voters publicly and what he appears prepared to do when his own influence is at stake.

He attacked the Conservatives as vipers while cooperating with them behind the scenes.

He called their MPs "Lib Dem Tories," then admitted agreeing to stop when the Conservatives assisted his parliamentary advancement.

He said Nigel Farage must never become prime minister.

He declared there would be no collaboration between Restore and Reform.

He built his party by attacking Reform relentlessly and encouraging its voters to defect.

Then, after Restore demonstrated that it could split the Reform vote but could not match Reform's support, Lowe suddenly offered a pact — on camera, in public and accompanied by his own list of demands.

When Farage did not immediately respond as Lowe wished, Lowe returned to accusing him of lying.

That is why trust matters.

A political agreement cannot survive without it.

Why should Reform trust a man who publicly attacks those with whom he is privately negotiating?

Why should Conservative voters trust someone who calls their party poisonous while accepting its assistance?

Why should Restore supporters trust a leader who told them there would be no collaboration with Reform, only to reverse himself weeks later?

And why should anybody believe that Rupert Lowe's latest position will last any longer than the previous one?

He claims to expose the vipers in British politics.

But judging by the difference between his public attacks and his private dealings, perhaps Rupert Lowe should take a closer look in the mirror.

Will the real Tory please stand up?
