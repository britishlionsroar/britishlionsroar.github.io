The Swords of Mathematics

The government uses economic multipliers to justify foreign aid, mass immigration, and public subsidy. Apply the same methodology to replacing 250,000 foreign care workers with British ones — and the case for doing nothing collapses completely.
The British Lions Roar — Investigative Opinion Journalism — June 2026
  • Each migrant care worker household costs the taxpayer a lifetime net fiscal sum of £103,000 — 117,000 are due to settle by 2030, producing a liability exceeding £12 billion
  • Replacing 250,000 foreign care roles with British workers at the Living Wage generates £925 million in new annual tax revenue and saves £875 million per year in welfare payments
  • Delayed hospital discharges caused by care home understaffing cost the NHS over £2 billion per year — at the official NHS unit cost of £562 per blocked bed day
  • Every pound paid to a domestic worker generates £2.36 of local economic activity. Foreign workers on low wages remit a significant portion overseas, removing it from the UK economy entirely
  • The total systemic return from a fully domestic, properly paid care workforce exceeds £30 billion in combined savings and economic stimulus
This is the third in a four-part series on the destruction and possible rescue of Britain's adult social care sector. Articles 1 and 2 established how private equity and foreign recruitment broke the system. This article applies the government's own economic tools — multipliers, lifetime fiscal calculations, welfare offsets, and NHS bed-cost data — to calculate what a British workforce in a publicly owned care sector would actually mean for the public finances.
This article is written in plain English, following George Orwell's rule: if you can say something simply, do so. It combines investigative reporting with policy analysis and opinion. Satire is used where appropriate to test ideas by applying them consistently. Facts are sourced; opinions are labelled; institutions are not indulged.

Every time the government wants to justify spending money on something — foreign aid, mass immigration, an LGBT cultural event, a regeneration scheme in a marginal constituency — it reaches for the same toolkit. It talks about economic multipliers. It talks about lifetime fiscal contributions. It talks about gross value added, induced spend, and preventative savings. It builds a model that shows £1 in produces £3 out, declares the investment a net positive for the public finances, and proceeds.

The same tools exist for every policy question. They do not get applied selectively unless someone has decided in advance what conclusion they want to reach.

So let us apply them honestly. Let us take the government's own macroeconomic methodology and run it across a scenario in which the 250,000 care roles currently filled by foreign workers are transitioned to British workers paid the real Living Wage. Not as a fantasy. As a calculation.

The numbers are, to put it plainly, embarrassing for everyone who has spent three decades insisting that the current model is the only affordable one.

The starting point is the avoided cost. The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee, as reported in Article 2 of this series, established that a migrant care worker settling in the UK with an adult dependant costs the taxpayer a lifetime net fiscal sum of £103,000. This is not a tabloid figure. It is the Migration Advisory Committee's own estimate, based on the gap between what low-wage workers contribute in tax and what they consume in public services over their lifetime in the UK.

Between 2026 and 2030, 117,000 care workers are expected to receive settlement rights, accompanied by 79,000 adult dependants. That produces a direct, calculable long-term liability to the public finances of over £12 billion in avoided infrastructure costs — NHS consumption, housing pressure, school places, and local authority service demand.

If those 117,000 roles had been filled by British workers already living here — people already using the NHS, whose children are already in schools, whose housing costs are already factored into local authority budgets — that £12 billion liability does not exist. The people are already here. The infrastructure cost is already being met. The incremental public service demand from hiring them into care rather than retail is zero.

That is before a single multiplier is applied. That is purely the avoided cost of the immigration model. And it is £12 billion.

“In September 2025, an average of 13,032 patients a day remained in hospital despite being medically fit for discharge. At the official NHS unit cost of £562 per bed day, this equates to over £2 billion annually in costs directly attributable to social care capacity failures.”
— NHS England discharge delay data; analysis by The Lowdown, October 2025

The second stream of savings is the NHS. This one has now been formally costed by NHS England for the first time, following years of estimates from think tanks and health economists.

In September 2025, NHS England published its own figure for the cost of delayed hospital discharges for that month: £220 million. The official unit cost of a blocked bed day is £562, derived from NHS 2023/25 cost data uplifted for inflation. Across a full year, with an average of 13,032 patients per day remaining in hospital despite being medically fit to leave, the total annual cost to the NHS exceeds £2 billion.

The primary cause of those delayed discharges, as established in parliamentary and NHS analysis, is the absence of available social care capacity to receive patients. Care homes and home care packages are understaffed. Patients cannot be discharged because there is nowhere suitable to send them. They remain in hospital beds at £562 per night while the ward fills up around them and the waiting list for the bed they are in grows longer.

A fully staffed, publicly owned care sector with a stable domestic workforce eliminates the staffing gap that causes delayed discharge. Not partially. Structurally. The vacancies that cause the blockage go away. The patients flow out of hospital on time. The £2 billion annual cost to the NHS drops.

That saving does not require any new money. It requires the existing money to stop being wasted.

The third stream is welfare and tax. This is the calculation the government applies to justify immigration on economic grounds, so it is only fair to run it in reverse.

Transitioning 250,000 care roles to British workers who are currently unemployed or economically inactive and are being disincentivised by sub-living-wage care pay produces two simultaneous fiscal effects.

If half those positions — 125,000 — are filled by people currently claiming Universal Credit, the state stops paying out an average of £6,000 to £8,000 per year per person in standard benefits. The annual welfare saving is approximately £875 million.

Simultaneously, moving 250,000 workers onto the real Living Wage at an average of around £25,000 per year full-time converts each of them into a net tax contributor. Each worker generates roughly £2,300 in Income Tax and £1,400 in National Insurance contributions annually. Across 250,000 workers, that is £925 million in new tax revenue flowing directly to the Treasury every year — revenue that does not exist under the current model, where low wages mean minimal fiscal contribution.

Combined, the welfare saving and the new tax yield produce a £1.8 billion annual improvement in the public finances.

The fourth stream is the multiplier effect, and this is the one the government uses most enthusiastically when it wants to justify spending decisions it has already made.

Standard macroeconomic multipliers for low-income domestic workers show that for every £1 paid in wages, approximately £2.36 of economic activity is generated in the local economy. Low earners spend almost all of what they earn, and they spend it close to home: food, rent, local services, local shops, local pubs. That spending generates VAT, business rates, and further employment in the businesses they patronise.

Foreign workers on low wages behave differently in one material respect. A significant proportion of their earnings are remitted overseas to support families in their country of origin. That money leaves the UK economy entirely. It generates no domestic VAT. It supports no British business. It is, from the perspective of the UK's economic multiplier, a dead loss.

A domestic workforce of 250,000 care workers paid the real Living Wage generates an aggregate annual salary pool of approximately £6.25 billion. Applying the £2.36 multiplier produces £14.75 billion of economic activity circulating in UK communities, generating substantial VAT and business tax returns to the state.

These numbers are built from the same data sources and the same methodological tools the government uses when it wants to make a positive case for any other policy. The difference is that no minister has chosen to run this particular calculation, because the conclusion is politically inconvenient.

The conclusion is that the current model — private equity ownership, suppressed wages, imported labour — is not economically rational. It is a subsidy. Private equity operators extract profit and export debt servicing to offshore structures. The British taxpayer then funds the NHS bed-blocking that results from their chronic understaffing, the welfare system that supports the British workers their low wages drove out, the immigration infrastructure for the workers who replaced them, and the long-term public service costs of those workers settling in the UK. The profit goes offshore. The costs stay here.

“The crisis in social care is not a lack of available money in the wider government system. It is entirely a question of political priorities and how the Treasury chooses to allocate the existing national budget.”

There is one further dimension that the pure fiscal calculation does not capture, and it matters.

The case for a domestic care workforce is not only about money. It is about language, culture, continuity, and the dignity of elderly people in the final years of their lives. A care worker who shares a cultural background, speaks English fluently as a first language, and lives in the same community as the person they are caring for is not a fungible unit of labour. They are better able to understand what the person is saying, to recognise changes in behaviour or condition that indicate deterioration, to communicate effectively with families, and to build the kind of continuous relationship that good dementia care requires.

The 70% of care home residents who live with severe dementia or memory problems are not served well by a revolving door of workers on tied visas from different countries, managed by a corporate structure optimised for debt servicing rather than care quality. This is not a point about nationality. It is a point about stability, linguistic capacity, and the conditions under which good care is actually possible.

The economic case for British workers in a publicly owned care system is, as demonstrated above, overwhelming. The human case is stronger still.
