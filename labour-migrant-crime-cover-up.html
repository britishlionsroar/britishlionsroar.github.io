Labour Lawyers Up to Hide Eight Years of Migrant Crime Figures From the People Who Pay Them

The information watchdog ruled the public is entitled to know. Labour lost, appealed, voted down publication in Parliament, and today refused a further year. You are paying for the lawyers.

By Steve English, Independent British writer and editor · 16 November 2026

  • The Ministry of Justice has refused to release 2025 conviction figures by nationality, on top of the 2018 to 2024 figures it is already fighting in a tribunal. That is eight years of data kept from the public.
  • The Information Commissioner threw out both of the Ministry's excuses, cost and privacy, and ordered the figures released by 22 July 2026. Labour appealed instead.
  • Every refusal, every appeal and every vote in this story happened on Labour's watch. Two Labour Justice Secretaries have kept the fight going.
  • The Ministry said finding the figures would take ten working days. It chose a court battle instead, paid for by the same taxpayers it is keeping in the dark.
PURPOSE: To set out, from the official record, how the Labour government has used public money and its Commons majority to stop the British public seeing anonymised conviction figures by nationality, and to ask the question ministers will not answer: what are they hiding?

Today the Ministry of Justice told the British public it will not hand over a single year's worth of conviction figures broken down by nationality. The year is 2025. An official from the Ministry's Analysis Directorate gave the reason:

"On balance the interests of the data subjects to safeguard personal information outweigh the legitimate interest in this case."Ministry of Justice, Analysis Directorate, November 2026 [Express]

The "data subjects" are convicted foreign offenders.

Read that again. Not the rights of victims. Not the rights of the public who fund every court, every prison and every civil servant in that building. The rights of foreign offenders.

This is not a one-off. The 2025 refusal is the latest move in a fight Labour has been running for seventeen months, in which it has already lost once, and in which it is now spending your money in a tribunal to stop you seeing the figures for 2018 to 2024. Add 2025, and that is eight years of migrant crime data the government has decided you are not allowed to see.

Nigel Farage, leader of Reform UK, called it an "outrageous cover up" and asked the only question that matters: "What are they hiding?" [Spectator]

It started on Labour's watch, not the Tories'

Labour will want you to think this is an old problem it inherited. It is not. Here is the timeline, taken from the Information Commissioner's own decision notice. [ICO]

DateWhat happened
22 June 2025The Centre for Migration Control asks the Ministry of Justice for conviction totals for 2018 to 2024, broken down by nationality and offence, including violence, sexual offences, weapons, drugs and robbery.
30 June 2025The Ministry refuses.
10 July 2025The Ministry upholds its own refusal on internal review.
23 July 2025The complaint goes to the Information Commissioner.
21 October 2025Labour MPs vote down an amendment that would have required sentencing data, including nationality, to be published.
22 June 2026The Information Commissioner rules against the Ministry and orders the figures released within 30 days.
July 2026Instead of complying, the Ministry appeals to the First-tier Tribunal.
16 November 2026The Ministry refuses the 2025 figures too.

The request was made a year into Labour's government. Labour's Ministry of Justice refused it. Labour's Ministry of Justice upheld the refusal. Labour ministers lodged the appeal. There is no Conservative to blame for any line in that table.

And here is the irony. Two days after the request went in, Labour justice minister Alex Davies-Jones told a Commons committee: "I recognise the importance of transparency when publishing data on foreign national offenders." [Hansard] Her department was refusing that very data six days later.

They lost, so they lawyered up

The Ministry gave two excuses. The watchdog threw out both.

Excuse one: it costs too much. Freedom of Information law lets a government department refuse a request if the work would cost more than £600, worked out at £25 an hour. That is 24 hours of work. The Ministry claimed the job would take ten working days. The Commissioner found the Ministry had "failed to demonstrate" its cost claim, because it never gave a proper evidenced breakdown of the work. [ICO decision]

Excuse two: privacy. The Ministry argued that the figures could identify individual criminals. But the requester had already asked for any figure below five to be blanked out, precisely so nobody could be identified. [Hungarian Conservative] The Commissioner agreed. Totals of five or more, it ruled, are not personal data, because "the data represents groups rather than individuals". [ICO decision]

The ruling was plain: disclose the figures within 30 days. That deadline was 22 July 2026.

The Ministry, then led by David Lammy, appealed to the First-tier Tribunal instead. [Brussels Signal] When Alex Norris took over as Justice Secretary, he could have dropped it. He kept it going. [Brussels Signal] Two Justice Secretaries, one policy: the public does not get to see.

Your money, your data, their lawyers

Do the sums on the Ministry's own claim. Ten working days at a seven-and-a-half-hour civil service day is 75 hours. At the government's own Freedom of Information rate of £25 an hour, that is £1,875.

That is what Labour says it would cost to give the British public the figures. Instead, it chose to take an independent regulator to a tribunal. Government lawyers do not work for free. Every hour of Government Legal Department time preparing that appeal, every hour of counsel, every hour of officials drafting witness statements, is paid for by the taxpayer.

So the people of this country are paying for the lawyers whose job is to stop the people of this country seeing anonymised figures about crime in their own country. Ministers and civil servants are public servants. We are their employers. Picture any business in Britain where the staff hired lawyers, on the company's money, to stop the owners reading the company's own books. Nobody would call that privacy. They would call it a cover-up.

Meanwhile, the people asking for the figures have had to raise money from the public through a crowdfunder to fight the appeal. [Crowdfunder] The public pays twice: once through their taxes for the government's lawyers, and again out of their own pockets to fight them.

They voted against it in Parliament too

This is not just a department digging in. Labour MPs had the chance to settle the matter in law and voted it down.

On 21 October 2025, during the Sentencing Bill, MPs voted on New Clause 9. It would have required courts to pass sentencing information, including the offender's nationality and immigration status, to HM Courts and Tribunals Service within 24 hours of sentence, for publication. It was defeated by 318 votes to 104. [TheyWorkForYou]

So Labour has blocked transparency in the Commons and in the courts. That is not an accident. That is a policy.

Whose rights?

The Ministry says the interests of "the data subjects" outweigh the public's. But the watchdog has already settled this. Totals by nationality, with small numbers blanked out, identify nobody. Nobody is asking for names, addresses or court files. The request is for how many people of each nationality were convicted of each type of crime.

If the figures identify no one, then the only thing being protected is the government's own story about immigration and crime. The public has a right to judge that story for itself, with the facts in front of it. That right has been put below the comfort of convicted criminals and the convenience of ministers.

Even the Office for National Statistics has said it holds no breakdown of crime by migrant status and that offender and conviction data is the Ministry of Justice's responsibility. [ONS FOI] The Ministry of Justice holds the figures, and it is the department fighting to keep them locked away.

Promises and a cover-up

Robert Jenrick, Reform UK's economy spokesman, summed it up: "The cover-up continues. Successive Tory and Labour justice secretaries have refused to publish this basic information." [Brussels Signal]

He is right that the Tories never published it either. The difference is that Labour has been told by the independent regulator that the public is entitled to the figures, and has chosen to fight that ruling with public money.

There are only two possibilities. Either the figures are harmless, in which case there is no reason on earth to spend public money hiding them. Or they are not harmless, in which case the British public had a right to know years ago.

Publish the figures. All eight years. Then let the people who pay for this government decide for themselves.

— Steve English
