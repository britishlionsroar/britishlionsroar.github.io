736 Convicted Criminals — Rapists, Murderers and Paedophiles Disappeared Into Your Community

They were convicted in British courts. They served their sentences. They were awaiting deportation. Then they vanished. Seven in every eight people arrested by immigration enforcement are still here. While Britain arrests and releases, Germany deports, Pakistan expels hundreds of thousands, and twenty European nations negotiate directly with the Taliban.

What This Article Establishes

  • 736 foreign national offenders — convicted criminals — are among the 53,298 who have absconded from immigration oversight.
  • These are not people awaiting a first decision. They were convicted in British courts, sentenced, and placed on deportation orders. They disappeared before the order could be executed.
  • In 2025, immigration enforcement made 8,971 arrests. Only 1,087 people were removed — fewer than 1 in 8.
  • Between July 2024 and December 2025, 12,322 arrests produced 1,726 total returns. The arrest press releases and the return statistics tell two entirely different stories.
  • Every other comparable nation is removing people at scale. Britain is not. This is a political choice, not a legal or logistical impossibility.

These are not abstract policy failures. These are named categories of convicted criminal — people judged by British courts to have committed serious offences against British victims — who are living in British communities with no monitoring, no restrictions, and no prospect of removal under the current system.

Who the 736 Are

The 736 figure emerged from the same whistleblower document read into Hansard by Rupert Lowe MP on 9 December 2025. The document was drawn from internal Home Office data as of October 2025. Within the total absconder pool of 53,298, 736 were categorised as foreign national offenders — individuals who had been convicted of criminal offences in United Kingdom courts.

It is necessary to be precise about what that means, because the category is specific and the implications are severe.

A foreign national offender in UK immigration law is not someone accused of a crime. It is not someone under investigation. It is someone who has been tried, convicted, and sentenced by a British court. They have served, or partially served, their sentence in a British prison. Upon release or near the end of their sentence, they were placed on a deportation order — a legal instrument requiring their removal from the United Kingdom.

These 736 people did not comply with the deportation order. They did not present themselves for removal. They are not in immigration detention. They are not subject to any known monitoring. They have disappeared into British communities, and the Home Office does not know where they are.

736 Convicted foreign national offenders — absconded before deportation, October 2025

The crimes for which foreign national offenders are deported from the United Kingdom span the full spectrum of serious criminal offending. Under UK law, automatic deportation applies to foreign nationals sentenced to 12 months or more in prison. The categories represented in the deportable FNO population include sexual offences — including offences against children — violent offences including grievous bodily harm and murder, drug trafficking, and fraud at scale.

The government has not published a breakdown of the 736 by offence category. It has not stated how many are convicted of sexual offences, how many of violence, how many of offences against children. That information has not been made available to Parliament or to the public. What is known is that all 736 met the legal threshold for deportation — a threshold that requires a sentence of 12 months or more, or commission of a specified serious offence regardless of sentence length.

"Rapists, murderers, paedophiles — all of these people are in that figure. They were convicted. They should have been deported. They are now living somewhere in this country." — Rupert Lowe MP, Hansard, 9 December 2025

The Enforcement Pipeline: What the Numbers Actually Show

The government has made much of its immigration enforcement activity. Press releases have announced record arrests. Ministers have cited enforcement figures as evidence of a system working. The Home Office enforcement data — updated as recently as 18 June 2026 — tells a different story when read in full rather than selectively.

Full Year 2025 — The Real Numbers

Arrests: 8,971

Detained: 2,251

Removed: 1,087

Of every 8 people arrested by immigration enforcement in 2025, fewer than 1 was removed from the United Kingdom. The other 7-plus were arrested and then released back into the community — either because detention capacity was unavailable, because legal challenges were lodged, or because the removal infrastructure could not process them.

July 2024 to December 2025 — The Cumulative Picture

Total arrests: 12,322

Total returns: 1,726

Across eighteen months of the current government's enforcement programme, 10,596 people were arrested by immigration enforcement and not removed. They were encountered, processed, and returned to the community. Many will have subsequently absconded. Some will already be in the 53,298 figure.

The ratio of arrests to removals is not a bureaucratic footnote. It is the operational reality of the UK's immigration enforcement system. Arrest without removal is not enforcement. It is contact. It records the person's presence and then returns them to the population the system is supposed to be managing.

8,971 Arrested
1,087 Removed
7,884 Still here

The broader removal picture is no more reassuring. Between June 2024 and June 2025, 58,000 asylum claims were refused. Fewer than 11,000 people were removed — a ratio of approximately 5 refusals for every 1 removal. Of the 166,000 people who arrived by small boat between 2018 and 2024, approximately 4,995 have been returned — a return rate of 3 per cent.

Three per cent. Of every 100 people who crossed the Channel illegally in the six years to 2024, 97 remain in the United Kingdom.

The Press Release vs the Reality

The government's communication strategy around immigration enforcement has been consistent: lead with arrests, bury returns. Every enforcement operation produces a press release announcing the number of arrests made. The return statistics — the metric that actually measures whether the system is working — appear in quarterly statistical bulletins that receive a fraction of the media attention.

This is not accidental. A government that publicises 8,971 arrests without contextualising them against 1,087 removals is presenting a deliberately incomplete picture. The arrest figure conveys activity. The removal figure conveys outcome. The gap between the two — 7,884 people encountered and not removed in a single year — is the operational failure the press releases are designed to obscure.

Keir Starmer, in his resignation speech on 22 June 2026, claimed "a decrease in undocumented migration." He did not cite a source for that claim. He did not reference the return rate. He did not explain what had happened to the 53,298 absconders, the 736 convicted criminals among them, or the 7 in 8 people arrested by his government's enforcement operations who were not removed.

"Every decision I have taken has been about putting the country I love first." — Keir Starmer, resignation statement, 22 June 2026. The Home Office enforcement data was updated four days earlier.

What Every Other Country Is Managing to Do

The standard government defence of its removal record rests on the claim that deportation is legally complex, internationally constrained, and practically difficult. That defence does not survive contact with the evidence of what other comparable nations are doing in the same period.

United Kingdom Arrests people and releases 7 in 8 of them. Has not designated Bangladesh — one of its top asylum source countries — as a safe country of origin. 3% return rate on small boat arrivals since 2018. 1,087 removed in full year 2025

Bangladesh is among the top source countries for asylum claims in the United Kingdom. The European Union designated Bangladesh a safe country of origin in December 2025, with the regulation entering force in June 2026. This designation enables EU member states to process Bangladeshi claims on an accelerated basis and to remove refused claimants rapidly. The United Kingdom has not made the same designation.

Twenty European nations — facing the same international legal framework, the same European Convention on Human Rights, the same practical constraints cited by British ministers — negotiated formally with Taliban representatives in Brussels in May 2026 to establish a returns framework for Afghan nationals. Britain was not among them.

The legal constraints cited to explain Britain's removal failure are real. They are also navigable, as every other comparable nation has demonstrated. The constraint is not legal. It is not logistical. It is political.

736 People. No Monitoring. No Known Whereabouts.

Return to the 736. These are not people caught in a processing backlog. They are not people awaiting a first asylum decision. They are convicted criminals — judged by British courts to have committed offences serious enough to warrant both imprisonment and deportation — who have actively evaded removal and are now living somewhere in the United Kingdom without any known address, without monitoring, and without any prospect of removal under current operational capacity.

The government does not know where they are. It has confirmed this through its own inability to answer parliamentary questions on the subject. It has confirmed it through the whistleblower document that had to be read into Hansard because no minister would provide the information voluntarily. And it has confirmed it through the PAC report, which found the asylum system's control to have been "all but lost."

British victims of foreign national offenders — people whose attackers were convicted, imprisoned, and placed on deportation orders — are living in a country where those attackers are also living, unmonitored, with no imminent prospect of removal. That is the operational consequence of a 3% return rate, a 1-in-8 removal ratio, and a government that chose to announce its arrest figures and conceal its removal figures.

Reform UK Policy Position

Reform UK's published policy platform calls for the immediate detention of all foreign national offenders pending removal, with no exceptions for those who have served their sentences and are subject to active deportation orders.

Reform UK has called for the designation of Bangladesh, Pakistan, India, and other major source countries as safe countries of origin, enabling accelerated removal consistent with the approach already adopted by EU member states.

Reform UK has proposed emergency charter flight programmes for convicted foreign nationals, modelled on the German programme currently operating to Kabul and other destinations.

Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse current demographic trends.

That is the outcome Labour set in motion from 1997 onwards, under Tony Blair, when mass immigration was deliberately embraced as an economic strategy — and it is the outcome the Conservatives later chose to continue.
