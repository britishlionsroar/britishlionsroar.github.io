Bev Craig Calls Herself a Champion for Women. Her Record as Council Leader Tells a Different Story.

Labour's Bev Craig wants to be Greater Manchester's first female mayor. Her record on women's safety, grooming gang investigations, and single-sex spaces tells a different story.

This article examines the public record of Bev Craig, Labour's candidate for the Greater Manchester mayoralty, against the standard she set for herself: a champion for women and girls in public life.

This article is written in plain English, following George Orwell's rule: if you can say something simply, do so. It combines investigative reporting with policy analysis and opinion. Satire is used where appropriate to test ideas by applying them consistently. Facts are sourced; opinions are labelled; institutions are not indulged.

The Redacted Pages

In July 2025, His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services published its findings on Greater Manchester Police's handling of child sexual exploitation investigations. The headline was cautiously positive — significant improvements, better systems, stronger leadership. But buried in the detail was a finding that should follow Bev Craig through every hustings, every interview, and every campaign appearance between now and polling day.

Material provided by Manchester City Council — the council Bev Craig has led since December 2021 — took many months to arrive at GMP and was, in the inspectors' own words, “so heavily redacted that some pages contained only a few words.”

These were not bureaucratic forms. This was intelligence needed by detectives running live grooming gang investigations. Operations Green Jacket and Bernese — both named in the report — were specifically identified as having been particularly affected by the delays and redactions.

On the Record — HMICFRS, July 2025
“Material provided by Manchester City Council took many months to arrive and was so heavily redacted that some pages contained only a few words. Operations Green Jacket and Bernese have been particularly affected by delays caused by these issues.”

At the time of the inspection, those investigations involved 714 victims and survivors, and 1,099 suspects.

Pages with only a few words on them.

Manchester City Council's own chief executive, Tom Stannard, was eventually forced to concede that the council had, on occasions, been “too cautious around redactions.” That is the language of an institution that got caught. It is not the language of one that was trying its hardest to bring child rapists to justice.

Bev Craig has not faced that reckoning. She has called grooming “abhorrent.” She has noted that some of the institutional failures predate her leadership. Both things can be true — and the redactions can still have happened on her watch, during live investigations, affecting real victims who are still waiting for justice.

She now wants to run Greater Manchester.

Who Is Bev Craig?

Bev Craig was born in Belfast and moved to Manchester around the age of eighteen. She has spent her entire adult life in Labour politics in the city, rising through the council ranks to become, in December 2021, Manchester City Council's first female leader and its first openly LGBT leader. She received an OBE in 2026. The mainstream press profile is warm, the biographical arc compelling — working class roots, social housing background, barrier-breaker.

She is, by every measure of the modern progressive Labour playbook, the perfect candidate.

In her first weeks as council leader she made a point of pledging to prioritise women's safety in public spaces. She noted, correctly, that urban planning had historically been male-led and that women paid the price in spaces that felt unsafe. It was a good line. It positioned her well. It also turns out to have been one of the more hollow commitments in recent Manchester political history.

“Within weeks of pledging to prioritise women's safety, her council passed a motion declaring trans women are women — unanimously, without debate on the implications, and without any Equality Impact Assessment.”

Within weeks of that pledge, in February 2022, her council passed a motion — unanimously, without debate on the implications, and without any Equality Impact Assessment — declaring that “trans men are men, trans women are women, non-binary people are non-binary and trans rights are human rights.” The motion committed the council to embedding that position across its services, its employment practices, and its public facilities.

No one asked what it meant for women-only swimming sessions. No one asked what it meant for domestic abuse services, homeless hostels, or changing rooms. No one apparently thought to ask at all.

That is the real Bev Craig behind the polished profile. A politician whose instinct, when the culture war comes to a council vote, is to move fast, move unanimously, and worry about the women affected later. Or not at all.

She wants to bring that instinct to the mayoralty of Greater Manchester.

The Trans Rights Motion

The February 2022 motion was not a symbolic gesture buried in committee. It was passed at a full council meeting, proposed, seconded, and carried without a single dissenting vote. It committed Manchester City Council to affirming that trans women are women across every service the council delivers — leisure centres, housing, social care, schools, domestic abuse provision, and beyond.

No Equality Impact Assessment was conducted before the vote. None was commissioned immediately after it. When residents began asking questions — through FOI requests, through scrutiny committees, through direct correspondence with councillors and the city solicitor — they were met with delay, deflection, and eventually a collection of generic EIAs that predated the motion entirely and addressed none of the specific questions being asked.

FOI Evidence — WhatDoTheyKnow
Residents submitted FOI requests for Equality Impact Assessments covering women-only leisure services following the 2022 motion. The council's responses provided EIAs that were generic to specific leisure facilities and, in the council's own admission, predated the Trans Rights Are Human Rights motion entirely. No EIA specifically addressing the motion's implications for single-sex services was ever produced or provided.

The Women's Rights Network and Labour Women's Declaration spent more than two years trying to get straight answers from Manchester City Council about what the motion meant in practice for single-sex services. What did it mean for women-only swimming sessions? For female domestic abuse refuges? For care homes where vulnerable women have a legal right to intimate personal care from a person of the same sex? The council's responses were, to borrow a word that has become something of a theme in this article, heavily redacted in all but name — technically responsive, substantively empty.

The legal picture has since shifted decisively. The Supreme Court ruled in 2025 that the definition of woman in the Equality Act refers to biological sex. That ruling did not create new law — it clarified what the law already said. Which means that when Manchester City Council passed its 2022 motion and embedded it across council services without an impact assessment, it was almost certainly operating outside the law it was bound to uphold.

Bev Craig has never acknowledged this. She has never ordered a formal review of the motion in light of the Supreme Court ruling. The council's own website still carries its commitment to being a “trans and non-binary inclusive workplace,” still states that “trans women are women,” and still frames this as a matter of progressive values rather than legal compliance.

This is the woman who, in 2021, pledged to make women's safety her priority.

She meant a different kind of women. Apparently.

The Culture On Her Watch

In 2025, The Mill — Manchester's independent investigative outlet — reported that a senior Manchester City Council executive had behaved in a manner described as “sleazy” at council events. The allegations against James Binks, then assistant chief executive, included inappropriately touching and grinding against junior female members of staff.

Bev Craig's response, when the story broke publicly, was to email staff saying she was “shocked and repulsed.”

Shocked. Repulsed. And apparently unaware until a journalist told her.

That is the detail worth sitting with. Not that it happened — organisations are not immune from the behaviour of individuals within them — but that it required an independent news outlet to surface it before the council's own leadership reacted. The women affected were junior staff. The man involved was a senior executive. The leader of the council, the woman who had built her brand on being a champion for women in public life, learned about it from The Mill.

There followed a large all-hands meeting, an address from Craig and the chief executive, expressions of concern and commitment to culture change. The standard choreography of institutional damage limitation. Whether anything substantive changed beneath the choreography is a question the council has not been required to answer publicly.

What the episode illustrates is a pattern. On grooming gang intelligence — reactive, once forced. On the trans rights motion's implications for women — reactive, once legally cornered. On the conduct of a senior executive towards junior female staff — reactive, once a journalist published.

“Leadership is not just what you say when the cameras are on. It is what you build, what you notice, and what you act on before someone else forces your hand.”

She has described herself as a feminist. She may well believe it. But feminism as a personal identity and feminism as institutional practice are two very different things. Manchester City Council under her leadership has repeatedly demonstrated which version she actually runs.

The Sanctuary City and the Streets

In June 2023, Bev Craig put her name to a letter from Greater Manchester's combined authority leadership to the Home Secretary. The letter called on the government to step back from the Illegal Migration Bill, which the signatories argued would “create further vulnerability by denying asylum-seekers and victims of Modern Slavery routes to support and protection.”

It was a principled stand, her supporters would say. A compassionate one.

Manchester is, formally, a City of Sanctuary. That designation reflects a genuine tradition of welcome in the city and Craig wears it as a badge of honour. In interviews and public statements she has consistently framed Greater Manchester as a place that stands against the hostile environment, that believes in human dignity, that will not turn its back on people fleeing persecution.

What the letters and the motions and the sanctuary designations do not address is what happens on the ground when policy meets reality.

The reality in Manchester is this. At points during Craig's leadership, up to fifty percent of rough sleepers in parts of the city had come directly from asylum accommodation — people granted leave to remain and then given twenty-eight days to vacate their Home Office housing, with nowhere to go and no recourse to public funds. They arrived on Manchester's streets not because of the hostile environment but because of the move-on cliff edge that follows a successful asylum claim. Craig's own council opened emergency respite facilities — former educational buildings fitted with bunk beds — to absorb some of the overflow.

The sanctuary city filled up. The bunk beds went in. And Bev Craig wrote to the Home Office to complain that the system was broken.

She was not wrong that the system is broken. But there is a particular kind of political positioning on display here that deserves scrutiny. A leader who advocates loudly for more asylum seekers to be brought into her city, who opposes every measure designed to reduce the flow, and who then writes letters of complaint when the human consequences of that position land on Manchester's streets and in Manchester's emergency shelters — that leader is not managing a crisis. She is performing concern about the consequences of her own politics.

Meanwhile, in the same city, working-class communities in Rochdale, Oldham, and Manchester itself spent decades being failed by institutions that prioritised community cohesion over child protection. The grooming gang scandal did not happen in a vacuum. It happened in a political culture where raising certain questions was actively discouraged, where the instinct of local Labour leadership was to manage optics rather than confront uncomfortable realities.

Bev Craig is the product and the continuation of that culture. The sanctuary motions, the trans rights declarations, the Illegal Migration Bill letters — they are not separate issues. They are expressions of the same institutional instinct: to perform progressive values loudly and publicly, and to deal with the consequences quietly, slowly, and only when forced.

The Mayoralty Stakes

The Greater Manchester mayoralty is not a ceremonial position. It carries real powers over policing, transport, housing, and public health across a city region of nearly three million people. The mayor chairs the Combined Authority. The mayor sets the strategic direction for Greater Manchester Police. The mayor is, in practical terms, the most powerful directly elected figure outside Westminster.

Greater Manchester Police is still running fifty-nine live multi-victim, multi-offender child sexual exploitation investigations. Seven hundred and fourteen victims. One thousand and ninety-nine suspects. These are not historical cases being reviewed at a comfortable distance. These are live investigations, with detectives working them now, requiring intelligence from partner agencies now, including from Manchester City Council now.

The council that sent police pages they could barely read.

Bev Craig's pitch for the mayoralty is continuity. She is Andy Burnham's chosen successor, the woman described in some circles as the brains behind his administration. She has been deputy mayor in all but name throughout his tenure. What she is offering Greater Manchester is more of the same — the same political culture, the same institutional instincts, the same progressive positioning over practical accountability.

Consider what that continuity means in practice. The mayor of Greater Manchester will oversee a police force still pursuing over a thousand suspects in live child sexual exploitation cases. That mayor will depend on intelligence sharing from the very council that obstructed those investigations with heavily redacted, months-delayed files. That mayor will set the tone for whether Greater Manchester's institutions finally face the reckoning they have avoided, or whether the machine finds another gear of managed optics and performative concern.

The women of Greater Manchester — the victims still waiting for justice, the women who lost single-sex spaces without anyone bothering to ask them, the junior council staff who needed a journalist to speak for them — deserve a mayor who leads before being forced to. Who acts before being caught. Who treats accountability as a first instinct rather than a last resort.

On the evidence of her record, Bev Craig is not that mayor.

She will have her lines ready. She always does.

Greater Manchester voters will have to decide whether lines are enough.

Reform UK — A Note on the National Picture

The failures documented in this article — institutional timidity on grooming gangs, the erosion of women's single-sex spaces, mass immigration policy disconnected from its local consequences — are not unique to Greater Manchester. They are the product of decades of Labour and Conservative governance at both local and national level. Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse these institutional failures. That is the outcome Labour set in motion from 1997 onwards, under Tony Blair, when mass immigration was deliberately embraced as an economic strategy — and it is the outcome the Conservatives later chose to continue.

The institutional culture described in this article — performative progressivism, reactive accountability, optics over outcomes — is not a conspiracy. It does not require bad faith in every individual. It requires only that the incentives of political life consistently reward the appearance of values over their practice. That is what Greater Manchester has had. That is what Bev Craig is offering to continue.
