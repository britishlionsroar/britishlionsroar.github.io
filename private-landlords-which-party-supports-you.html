PRIVATE LANDLORDS: WHICH PARTY SUPPORTS YOU?

How Reform UK, Labour, the Conservatives and Greens would affect your tax, costs, control and future as a landlord

Britain's private landlords have spent the past decade absorbing one change after another: mortgage-interest restrictions, higher purchase taxes, new tenancy rules, energy-efficiency requirements and increasingly complicated tax administration.

So rather than asking which party is supposedly “pro-landlord” or “anti-landlord”, there is a more useful question:

What would each party's actual policies mean if you own and rent out property?

That distinction matters because plenty of claims circulating online mix together current policy, old manifestos, proposals under consultation and things politicians have never actually promised.

Here is where the four parties currently stand.

REFORM UK

Reform has a documented proposal to reverse one of the major tax changes affecting individual landlords.

Its 2024 Contract explicitly promised to “Scrap section 24 for Landlords”, restoring landlords' ability to deduct finance costs and mortgage interest when calculating taxable rental income. That commitment is genuine, although it should be described as a 2024 commitment unless and until Reform formally reaffirms it in its developing 2026 programme.

There is a much more recent commitment on tenancy regulation. In February 2026, Richard Tice announced plans for a “Great Repeal Bill”, including scrapping Labour's Renters' Rights Act regime.

However, there is an important distinction. Repealing the Renters' Rights Act is documented; precisely what Reform would put in its place is less clear. We therefore should not automatically turn that into claims about the exact future form of Section 21, Section 8 or tenancy contracts until Reform publishes those details.

Reform has also put forward potentially substantial incentives for property investment on qualifying brownfield developments. At its September 2026 conference, Tice floated ten-year exemptions from Stamp Duty Land Tax, Capital Gains Tax and income tax on rental income for qualifying newly built homes. These are currently proposals/consultation, not settled policy.

That distinction matters because Reform's old £750,000 SDLT threshold and £2 million inheritance-tax threshold belonged to its 2024 tax programme and should not simply be recycled as firm current 2026 promises.

Reform's position on Making Tax Digital is also relevant to landlords. At the party's small-business launch on 10 June 2026, Nigel Farage criticised the requirement for sole traders to interact with the online tax system four times a year and said Reform would “get rid of that provision.” Contemporary reporting described the policy as restoring annual returns and making MTD optional. This concerns quarterly reporting/updates, not paying income tax four times per year.

One claim we cannot yet substantiate is that Reform has specifically promised to abolish the 2030 EPC-C requirement for private landlords. Reform has extensive anti-Net-Zero policies, but that is not sufficient evidence to turn a possible consequence into a specific landlord-policy promise.

LABOUR

With Labour, much of the comparison concerns law rather than promises.

Since 1 May 2026, the Renters' Rights Act regime has fundamentally changed private renting in England. Section 21 no-fault eviction has gone, most private assured tenancies have become periodic tenancies, and landlords wishing to recover their properties must use statutory possession grounds.

That does not mean a landlord can never recover a property. Grounds remain for circumstances including selling the property, landlord or family occupation, serious rent arrears and antisocial behaviour. The important difference is that possession now requires an applicable statutory ground rather than the former Section 21 route.

The Act also changes rules covering rent increases, rent in advance, rental bidding and other aspects of managing tenancies, while expanding enforcement mechanisms.

On taxation, Labour has not reversed Section 24. From 6 April 2027, individual property income in England, Wales and Northern Ireland will have separate rates of 22%, 42% and 47%. The residential finance-cost tax reducer consequently moves from 20% to 22%, but that should not be confused with restoring mortgage interest as an ordinary deductible business expense: the underlying property-income tax rates themselves are increasing.

Labour is also pursuing a requirement for privately rented homes in England to reach EPC C or equivalent by 1 October 2030, subject to exemptions and a £10,000 per-property expenditure cap under the announced framework.

CONSERVATIVES

The Conservative position is more complicated than either Labour's current legislation or Reform's repeal proposals.

First, Section 24 was introduced under a Conservative government. George Osborne announced the restriction on residential mortgage-interest relief in 2015. We have identified no current Conservative commitment to repeal or materially alter it.

Likewise, although Conservatives have criticised Labour's Renters' Rights reforms and their consequences, we have identified no current formal Conservative commitment to repeal the Renters' Rights Act or restore Section 21.

That fits with their previous position. At the 2024 election the Conservatives themselves supported ultimately abolishing Section 21, although they wanted court reforms in place before doing so.

There is a major current Conservative property-tax promise: Kemi Badenoch has pledged that a future Conservative government would abolish Stamp Duty on primary residences. For landlords, however, the words “primary residences” matter. We have not established a corresponding commitment abolishing SDLT on additional buy-to-let purchases.

So it would be misleading to take a substantial Conservative homebuyer tax proposal and automatically present it as a landlord tax cut.

GREEN PARTY

The Greens' position is substantially different again.

The party's adopted policy calls for the “effective abolition of Private Landlordism” over time. Its proposals include ending buy-to-let mortgages, additional taxation of landlord income, rent controls and mechanisms intended to transfer more privately rented homes into tenant or public ownership.

There is no specific Green policy we have identified that mentions Section 24 itself, so it would be wrong to claim that the party has explicitly pledged either to retain or alter it.

Instead, its documented programme approaches landlord taxation from another direction, including proposals for National Insurance on private rental income and broader changes to property taxation.

Its adopted landlord programme also proposes tenant first-right-to-buy when a landlord sells, with total rent previously paid deducted from the purchase price, followed by specified council purchase rights. Green policy additionally proposes ending buy-to-let mortgages and rent controls.

Green retrofit material proposes stringent energy-performance requirements for the private rented sector, including EPC C and then EPC B targets, alongside mechanisms for landlord-funded improvements.

For a landlord, therefore, the relevant Green comparison is not simply “what would they do with Section 24?” Their proposed changes to the structure and scale of private landlordism itself are potentially much more consequential.

SO WHAT ACTUALLY SEPARATES THE FOUR?

Section 24: Reform's 2024 Contract explicitly promised abolition. The Conservatives introduced the restriction. Labour retains it. We have identified no specific Green Section 24 proposal.

Renters' Rights Act: Labour introduced and is implementing it. Reform explicitly proposes repeal. We have found no equivalent current Conservative repeal commitment. Green policy generally seeks stronger tenant protection and goes further in areas such as rent controls.

Section 21: gone under Labour since 1 May 2026. Reform proposes repealing the legislation that abolished it, although the precise replacement regime still needs to be specified. Conservatives have not committed to restoring it. Greens support stronger security for tenants.

Landlord taxation: Labour has legislated higher dedicated property-income rates from April 2027. Reform's historic Section 24 proposal would reduce the tax burden on mortgaged individual landlords, while its new brownfield proposals could provide substantial targeted tax exemptions if adopted. Green proposals would introduce additional forms of taxation on landlord/property income.

Buying property: Labour's existing additional-property SDLT regime remains. The Conservatives' proposed abolition applies to primary residences rather than established BTL purchases. Reform's old £750,000 threshold should not be presented as a current firm commitment, while its new brownfield tax package remains a proposal/consultation.

Regulation and control: Labour's direction is toward greater regulation of the landlord-tenant relationship. Reform proposes significant deregulation through repeal. Conservatives have criticised elements of Labour's approach without so far promising wholesale reversal. Green policy envisages substantially greater intervention in rents, property finance and the size of the private rented sector.

THE IMPORTANT CAVEAT

This is not an assessment of which party has the “best” housing policy overall. Tenants, landlords, taxpayers, first-time buyers and government can have competing interests.

But viewed specifically through the finances, rights and operating conditions of an individual private landlord, the documented policies are markedly different — and those differences can be explained without pretending that every old manifesto promise remains current.

That's what this series will examine.

Claims deliberately NOT made

The following were investigated but are not treated as established current policy in the article:

1. Reform will definitely restore Section 21 exactly as it existed before May 2026.

2. Reform has specifically pledged to abolish the 2030 PRS EPC-C requirement.

3. Reform's old £750,000 SDLT threshold is a firm current 2026 commitment.

4. Reform's old £2m IHT threshold is a firm current 2026 commitment.

5. Conservatives currently promise to repeal the Renters' Rights Act.

6. Conservatives currently promise to restore Section 21.

7. Conservatives currently promise to repeal Section 24.

8. Green Party explicitly supports retaining Section 24 — no specific Section 24 position was identified.

9. MTD requires landlords to pay tax quarterly. The relevant requirement concerns digital records/quarterly updates, not quarterly income-tax payment.
