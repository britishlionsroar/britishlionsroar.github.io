FURTHER RIGHT THAN REFORM — SO WHY IS FARAGE STILL THE OBSESSION?

Restore sits to Reform’s right and has attracted documented involvement from people with extreme-right backgrounds. Yet Labour, the Conservatives and national politics remain fixated on Farage. Follow the numbers and there is an obvious reason.
Editorial note: This is an opinion investigation based on published reporting, polling and public records. Where the evidence establishes a fact, we say so. Where we draw an inference, we say so. Readers can make up their own minds.

Listen to British politics for a day and you could be forgiven for thinking Nigel Farage and Reform UK occupy every spare room in Westminster.

Labour attacks Reform. Conservatives attack Reform. Farage dominates interviews, speeches and political rows. The label “far right” is repeatedly thrown into arguments about Reform.

Meanwhile Rupert Lowe has built Restore Britain to Reform’s right.

And Restore has attracted exactly the sort of political company that ought, on the face of it, to make Britain’s professional anti-far-right industry sit bolt upright.

Former BNP figures and activists from harder-right organisations have supported, campaigned around or attempted to influence Restore. National newspapers have reported people with neo-Nazi, white-supremacist and ethno-nationalist backgrounds becoming involved around the project.

So why does Farage remain the obsession?

Perhaps because one party threatens the established parties’ power — and the other currently does not.

RESTORE ISN’T JUST BEING CALLED “TO REFORM’S RIGHT” BY ITS ENEMIES

YouGov itself describes Restore as a party that “positions itself to the right of Reform UK”. Its July voter study found Restore drawing a base primarily made up of around 10% of people who voted Reform at the 2024 general election.

Restore’s policies and rhetoric have consequently attracted people for whom Reform is apparently not hard enough.

Reporting in 2026 has documented former BNP figures and people connected with organisations considerably further to the right becoming involved with, supporting or campaigning around Restore. Separate investigations have reported an organised effort by hard-right activists to gain influence within the party.

The Mail on Sunday put Restore activists attending a “white supremacy summit” on its front page in June. The following day the Daily Mail ran another attack describing Restore as a new home for neo-Nazis. Other national outlets have investigated similar connections.

That qualification matters: an extremist supporting a party does not prove that the party endorses the extremist. Lowe has publicly said he detests neo-Nazis and has rejected ethno-nationalism.

But the existence of those networks around Restore is not something Reform supporters invented on Facebook. It has been the subject of national newspaper investigations.

REFORM DRAWS A DIFFERENT LINE

Reform UK has an explicit prohibition covering former membership of the BNP and a list of other extremist organisations. Candidates exposed as having concealed historic BNP links have been removed or expelled.

That does not settle the endless argument over ideological labels. “Far right” is itself a contested political description, and different journalists and academics use it differently.

But it creates an obvious contrast.

Restore is positioned to Reform’s right and has attracted documented involvement from figures whose political histories go well beyond Reform’s own membership rules.

Yet the party Labour and the Conservatives obsess over is Reform.

THE SIMPLE EXPLANATION: FOLLOW THE ELECTORAL THREAT

We do not need a secret Westminster meeting to explain that.

YouGov’s latest Westminster voting-intention poll puts Labour on 23%, the Conservatives on 21% and Reform UK on 21%.

Restore is on 4%.

Reform has MPs, large numbers of councillors, local-government representation, national campaign infrastructure, substantial fundraising and polling support placing it alongside the two parties that have dominated British government for generations.

Restore has nothing remotely comparable at present.

So which party would Labour and the Conservatives rationally spend their time trying to damage?

The one capable of taking their voters, their councillors, their constituencies and potentially their route to government.

That does not prove a conspiracy to protect Lowe. In fact, parts of the national press have subjected Restore to extremely hostile scrutiny.

But it explains why Reform occupies a completely different political universe from Restore when Westminster calculates threats.

RESTORE DOESN’T NEED TO WIN BRITAIN TO MATTER.
IT ONLY NEEDS TO TAKE ENOUGH VOTES FROM REFORM IN THE RIGHT SEATS.

THE SPOILER PROBLEM

Here is where Restore’s small size stops being irrelevant.

YouGov found substantial overlap between the two parties’ electorates. Restore was drawing around one in ten people who had voted Reform in 2024.

We have already seen the problem in an actual contest. Before the Makerfield by-election, constituency polling reported by The Independent put Labour on 46%, Reform on 41% and Restore on roughly 7%. The newspaper explicitly identified the split on the right as potentially decisive.

The eventual numbers differed, as election results often do from pre-election polling, but the arithmetic remains brutally simple under first past the post.

A party polling 4%, 5% or 7% nationally does not have to win a seat to change who does.

If enough of those voters would otherwise have supported Reform, the beneficiary can be Labour or the Conservatives without Restore electing anybody.

That does not tell us Lowe’s motive.

It tells us the electoral effect that everybody involved can see.

AND THEN THERE IS LOWE’S RELATIONSHIP WITH THE CONSERVATIVES

This is where the picture becomes considerably more awkward.

On 21 October 2025 Lowe was given a place on the House of Commons Public Accounts Committee from the Conservative allocation. Lowe acknowledged an “agreement” under which he stopped referring to Conservative MPs as “Lib Dem Tories”.

The Telegraph subsequently reported, citing two sources, that Lowe had privately agreed not to attack Kemi Badenoch’s party during committee proceedings in exchange for the seat. The Conservatives denied that account.

So the documented committee appointment and Lowe’s acknowledged agreement should not be confused with the newspaper’s disputed allegation about precisely what was exchanged.

But the chronology becomes harder to ignore when placed beside Lowe’s subsequent behaviour towards Reform.

THE POISON OLIVE BRANCH

On 13 June 2026 Lowe ruled out collaboration between Restore and Reform.

Then, on 2 August, he suddenly appeared publicly offering Reform what was presented as an “olive branch”.

Except it was not preceded by private negotiations with Reform UK. There had been no quiet approach followed by a meeting to discover whether the two parties could find common ground.

Instead Lowe went public first — with a video and a list of demands Reform would have to satisfy.

That is an extraordinary way to begin a negotiation if the overriding objective is genuinely to reach a deal.

Our view is that it looked considerably more like political grandstanding: make the offer publicly, attach conditions, then retain the ability to say that you extended the olive branch if the other side refuses your terms.

One of those conditions was particularly striking. Lowe demanded that former Conservative ministers be excluded from any future Reform cabinet — while Lowe himself occupied a parliamentary committee place that had come from the Conservative allocation.

And within roughly 24 hours of the supposed reconciliation attempt, Lowe was publicly accusing Farage of “outright lying”.

Readers can decide whether that looked like a serious attempt to stop splitting the vote or political theatre that was almost designed to fail.

We examined that episode and Lowe’s wider Conservative connections in detail in our earlier investigation: Why Rupert Lowe Is the Real Tory.

SO WHY IS FARAGE STILL THE OBSESSION?

Perhaps the answer is staring us in the face.

Restore currently poses little direct electoral threat to Labour or the Conservatives.

Reform does.

Restore is polling at 4%. Reform is polling at 21% alongside the Conservatives and only two points behind Labour in the latest YouGov figures.

Restore has attracted people from political currents considerably further to the right than Reform. That has produced some serious newspaper investigations — so claims of a total media blackout would be wrong.

But Restore is not presently threatening to replace Labour or the Conservatives as a governing force.

Reform is competing with them nationally.

And here is the uncomfortable part:

Restore can be too small to frighten the establishment while still being large enough to damage Reform.

Those two things can be true simultaneously.

WHO BENEFITS?

We cannot tell you that Rupert Lowe created Restore in order to help Labour or the Conservatives. We have not found evidence proving that, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

We do not need to know what is inside Lowe’s head to examine what happens at the ballot box.

If Restore wins a constituency, Restore benefits.

If Restore takes enough Reform-leaning voters to change first place without winning itself, somebody else benefits.

Under first past the post, there is no prize for ideological purity and no second-place coalition of votes. There is simply the candidate with the largest pile.

That leaves voters with a question rather more important than whether Westminster commentators use the correct ideological label for Rupert Lowe.

If Restore remains a low-single-digit party drawing disproportionately from Reform’s electorate, who actually gains from every constituency in which both stand?

Perhaps that is why Westminster is not remotely as frightened of Restore as it is of Reform.
