There Are Increasingly Fewer Davids

Across Britain, more and more people are questioning the political consensus they have been told for years they must accept.

There are increasingly fewer Davids.

Across Britain, more and more people are questioning the political consensus they have been told for years they must accept.

And Britain is far from alone.

In France, Marine Le Pen's National Rally is preparing for the next presidential election.

In Germany, the AfD has risen to challenge the country's traditional political establishment.

Across Europe, parties campaigning on immigration, borders, national sovereignty and putting their own citizens first have grown — and in several countries they are already participating in or supporting governments.

And here in Britain, Reform UK is attempting to break 102 years of Labour and Conservative domination.

The labels differ from country to country.

The grievances often don't.

Mass immigration.

Illegal immigration.

Borders.

Crime.

Integration.

Pressure on housing and public services.

National identity.

And political establishments that too often seem more concerned about what ordinary people are allowed to say about these problems than actually solving them.

Now watch how fiercely the political establishment fights to preserve the status quo.

Because Britain's David has a story to tell...

At First, David Did Not Understand

At first, David did not understand.

He watched the evening news, paid his taxes, voted responsibly and believed what respectable people told him.

Britain needed mass immigration because the economy needed workers.

Diversity was our strength.

Public services were under pressure because they simply needed more funding.

Disorder in schools was caused by inequality.

Crime statistics required “context”.

And anyone who connected these things too quickly was probably far right.

Then his children started coming home from school saying they could barely hear the teacher.

Half the lesson was spent restoring order.

Teachers changed constantly.

Some simply disappeared for weeks.

David complained once, politely.

The school explained that the situation was “challenging” and that it was developing an “inclusive response”.

David went home reassured.

There were professionals dealing with it.

His neighbourhood changed too.

Shops closed.

Others appeared.

Groups of men occupied the same corners every evening.

His wife stopped taking one particular street after dark.

David noticed all of this.

But David had also learned that noticing patterns was dangerous.

So every event remained an isolated event.

One closed shop meant nothing.

One assault meant nothing.

Ten incidents meant ten unrelated incidents.

Only a racist would connect the dots.

Then his daughter was sexually assaulted by a man who had recently arrived in Britain.

David was devastated.

But almost immediately, he began policing his own thoughts.

The attacker was an individual.

Of course.

One must never generalise.

The television explained that exploiting such crimes politically was irresponsible.

Politicians warned against “stigmatisation”.

David found himself nodding through tears.

What had happened to his daughter was monstrous.

But the truly frightening possibility, apparently, was that somebody somewhere might vote Reform UK because of it.

Nothing changed.

His son's school became worse.

His daughter stopped going out alone.

His wife wanted to move.

The police were exhausted.

The teachers were exhausted.

The NHS was exhausted.

David was exhausted.

Yet every explanation he had been given required him to believe that each deterioration was temporary, accidental, exaggerated — or caused by something entirely unrelated to the deterioration immediately beside it.

And whenever somebody suggested that perhaps Britain had made serious mistakes —

about mass immigration,

about illegal immigration,

about integration,

about education,

about policing,

about punishment,

about our borders,

about putting the interests of British citizens first —

David knew the approved response.

“Careful. That sounds far right.”

So David remained reasonable.

He remained moderate.

He remained informed.

He kept voting Labour.

Then Conservative.

Then Labour.

Then Conservative.

And he remained completely incapable of drawing a conclusion from his own life.

Until one day David finally asked himself a very simple question:

If voting for the same two parties keeps producing the same results, why am I still voting for them?

Perhaps the problem wasn't that David had suddenly become “far right”.

Perhaps David had simply stopped being frightened of the label.

Perhaps he had finally started believing his own eyes.

At first, David did not understand.

Now he does.
“Insanity is Hoping over and over and expecting different results”
