A Fool’s Guide to Cryptocurrency

A FOOL’S GUIDE TO CRYPTOCURRENCY

What it is, why people use it, why governments regulate it — and what Reform UK is actually proposing

Cryptocurrency can sound horribly complicated.

Crypto. Blockchain. Digital wallets. Mining. Exchanges. Decentralisation.

For most people, the terminology is enough to make them switch off before anybody has explained what the thing actually is.

So forget the jargon.

Let’s start with something everybody understands.

So what actually is cryptocurrency?

You know the money in your bank account?

Most of it is already digital. It isn’t sitting in your local bank as a pile of notes with your name on it. It’s mainly a record on computers saying how much money you have.

You get paid, the number goes up. You buy something, the number goes down. You send £20 to your mate, the banking system changes its records to show that £20 has moved from you to them.

So digital money isn’t new. We already use it every day.

Cryptocurrency is another way of owning and transferring something of value digitally.

The big difference is that you don’t need an ordinary bank sitting in the middle.

With the money in your bank account, the banking system keeps the records and controls the movement of the money.

With cryptocurrency, a network of independent computers connected through the internet keeps the records and checks transactions instead.

That means people can own and transfer cryptocurrency directly, without needing a bank to keep the master record or process every payment.

That’s the basic idea.

Cryptocurrency is digital value that can be owned and transferred without needing an ordinary bank in the middle.

If there’s no bank, who keeps the records?

Cryptocurrency still needs a record.

Otherwise you could spend the same £50-worth of cryptocurrency ten times and nobody would know.

The difference is that there isn’t one bank keeping that record.

Instead, lots of independent computers connected through the internet keep copies of it.

When cryptocurrency is sent from one person to another, the network checks the records to make sure the sender actually has it. Once the transaction has been checked, the record is updated.

Because lots of computers have copies of the same record, one person can’t simply change their copy and announce:

“Actually, I’ve got £1 million.”

The other copies wouldn’t agree.

And this is where that annoying word “blockchain” comes in

You’ve probably heard people talking about “the blockchain” as though you need a degree in computer science to understand it.

You don’t.

It’s basically the shared record we’ve just been talking about.

It records transactions in order and keeps building as new transactions are added.

There’s considerably more technology behind it, obviously.

But you don’t need to understand how an engine works to understand what a car does.

For our purposes:

Banking: banks keep the financial records.

Cryptocurrency: computers on a network keep a shared record.

That shared record is what people mean when they talk about a blockchain.

But if cryptocurrency is just numbers on computers, why is it worth actual money?

Turn the question around.

Why is a £20 note worth £20?

The paper certainly isn’t worth £20.

It has value because people accept it as having value. Shops accept it. Employers pay us in pounds. Other people accept pounds from us.

Cryptocurrency gets its value differently, but the idea of people agreeing that something has value isn’t new.

People buy cryptocurrencies because they believe they are useful, scarce, desirable or that somebody else will want them in future.

Different cryptocurrencies work differently.

Some have become widely used.

Some have hardly any use at all.

And some are complete rubbish.

Anyone can create a cryptocurrency. That doesn’t magically make it valuable any more than writing “£1 MILLION” on a piece of paper makes you a millionaire.

Ultimately, a cryptocurrency has a market value because people are willing to exchange ordinary money, goods or services for it.

And the old rule still applies:

How many people want it, and how much is available?

If demand rises, the price can rise.

If demand disappears, the price can collapse.

Which brings us to something important.

Cryptocurrency is not risk-free.

Its price can rise dramatically.

It can also fall dramatically.

So how do you actually buy cryptocurrency?

This is considerably less exciting than it sounds.

Most people use a company that buys and sells cryptocurrency.

You create an account, put ordinary money into it — pounds, for example — and use those pounds to buy cryptocurrency.

You don’t normally have to buy one whole unit. You can buy a tiny fraction.

You can then leave your cryptocurrency with the company you bought it through or move it somewhere you control yourself.

That brings us to another bit of jargon.

What’s a “crypto wallet”?

Despite the name, it isn’t really a digital purse containing little electronic coins.

Think of it as the thing that gives you access to and control over your cryptocurrency.

If you control it yourself, you don’t need a bank to give you access to your money.

That provides freedom.

But it also gives you responsibility.

If something goes wrong with your bank account, there’s a bank to contact.

If you take complete control of cryptocurrency yourself and lose the information needed to access it, there may be nobody to telephone and no password-reset button capable of getting it back.

So “no bank in the middle” has an upside and a downside.

You don’t need the bank.

But you don’t necessarily have the bank there to rescue you either.

So why would anybody want cryptocurrency?

There isn’t one answer.

Different people use it for different reasons.

1. Sending value without needing a bank

Cryptocurrency can allow two people to transfer value directly.

You don’t necessarily need both people to have accounts with banks that cooperate with one another.

That can be particularly useful when money is moving internationally.

2. More control over your own assets

With some cryptocurrencies, you can hold them yourself rather than having a bank or another company hold them for you.

For supporters, that is one of cryptocurrency’s biggest attractions.

3. It works internationally

Cryptocurrency isn’t confined to the banking system of one country.

Someone in Britain can transfer cryptocurrency to somebody on the other side of the world using the same network.

That doesn’t mean there are never costs, delays or legal requirements.

It means the underlying system itself isn’t dependent upon an international chain of ordinary banks.

4. Investment

Some people simply buy cryptocurrency because they think its value will increase.

Sometimes they make money.

Sometimes they lose money.

Calling something innovative doesn’t make it a guaranteed good investment.

5. New businesses and technology

There is now an enormous international industry building services around digital assets.

That means companies, software developers, financial businesses, jobs, investment and tax revenue.

And this is where the political argument really begins.

Why do governments regulate cryptocurrency?

For much the same reason they regulate ordinary financial businesses.

People can be defrauded.

Companies can collapse.

Money can be stolen.

Criminals can move money.

Businesses can mislead customers.

And people can lose enormous amounts of money.

So the serious argument isn’t really:

REGULATION or NO REGULATION.

It’s:

WHAT regulation do we actually need?

A rule that helps prevent criminals laundering millions of pounds is one thing.

A rule that creates six months of paperwork and enormous legal bills for a legitimate British technology company without providing much additional protection is something else.

The argument about “deregulation” is really an argument about which rules are necessary and which rules simply make doing business more difficult.

What about money laundering?

This is probably the criticism you’ll hear most often.

“If you deregulate cryptocurrency, criminals will use it to launder money.”

There is a genuine issue underneath that argument.

Criminals do use cryptocurrency.

But criminals also use:

  • cash; - bank accounts; - companies; - property; - valuable goods; and - international money transfers.

The existence of criminal use doesn’t tell us whether the legitimate use should be banned or smothered in unnecessary regulation.

The important question is:

Can we target criminal activity while allowing legitimate people and businesses to use the technology?

And this is where an important fact about Reform UK’s proposal gets lost.

Reform did NOT propose abolishing anti-money-laundering safeguards

Reform UK’s published Cryptoassets and Digital Finance Bill proposed a two-year trial in which selected financial businesses could operate with temporary relief from some regulations.

But the proposal specifically said that the trial would continue to maintain the main safeguards against money laundering and terrorist financing.

So “lighter regulation” and “no money-laundering rules” are not the same thing.

That distinction matters.

The existing UK system already requires crypto businesses carrying out certain services in Britain to register with the Financial Conduct Authority and comply with the UK’s Money Laundering Regulations.

Those requirements exist today.

And Britain is actually moving towards more extensive regulation of cryptocurrency businesses.

A much broader system of FCA regulation is due to start on 25 October 2027.

So the real political disagreement is not whether cryptocurrency should exist in a completely lawless financial Wild West.

It is about how heavily it should be regulated and which rules actually provide useful protection.

But isn’t cryptocurrency anonymous?

Not necessarily.

This is another common misunderstanding.

Remember that shared record we talked about earlier?

For many cryptocurrencies, transactions are recorded on a record that can be publicly examined.

What can be difficult is establishing which human being owns a particular crypto address.

Think of seeing account number 123456 making a payment to account 987654 without immediately knowing the names of the people behind those numbers.

Once investigators can connect an address to a particular person, previous transactions recorded against that address may provide useful information.

Different cryptocurrencies provide different levels of privacy, so there is no sensible blanket statement that “crypto is anonymous.”

It can make identifying somebody difficult.

That is not the same thing as saying every transaction is invisible.

So what does Reform UK actually want to do?

Reform UK published a Cryptoassets and Digital Finance Bill in 2025.

It proposed several major changes.

1. Reduce Capital Gains Tax on cryptocurrency to 10%

Capital Gains Tax is basically tax on the profit you make when something you own increases in value and you sell or otherwise dispose of it.

Reform proposed a single 10% rate for qualifying cryptocurrency gains.

Why?

Reform’s argument was straightforward:

Make Britain financially attractive to people and businesses involved in cryptocurrency and more of them will base their activities here.

That could mean more businesses, investment and taxable activity taking place in Britain rather than elsewhere.

What’s the criticism?

Critics argue that cryptocurrency investors would receive preferential tax treatment compared with people investing in other assets.

There is also no guarantee that reducing the tax rate would generate enough additional activity to compensate for the lower rate.

That is an economic argument.

It is not a money-laundering argument.

2. Give businesses room to experiment with fewer rules

This is the part normally described as deregulation.

Reform proposed a two-year trial supervised by the FCA and Treasury.

Approved businesses would temporarily be freed from selected regulations while they tested new financial services.

Why?

Imagine somebody develops a genuinely useful new financial service.

Before finding out whether customers even want it, they may have to spend enormous amounts of money on lawyers, compliance staff and regulatory approval.

Large international companies can afford that.

A small British startup may not be able to.

The idea is therefore:

Give legitimate businesses controlled room to experiment before burying them in the full rulebook.

And importantly, Reform’s proposal explicitly said that the main safeguards against money laundering and terrorist financing would remain.

That is very different from:

“Reform wants an unregulated cryptocurrency free-for-all.”

3. Stop banks discriminating against lawful cryptocurrency users

Reform proposed protections against banks closing accounts or refusing services simply because somebody lawfully uses cryptocurrency.

Again, this wouldn’t mean a bank couldn’t act where it suspected fraud, money laundering or another crime.

The principle is:

Using a legal financial technology shouldn’t by itself be enough to deny somebody ordinary banking services.

4. Allow taxes to be paid using cryptocurrency

Reform proposed allowing people to pay certain taxes using approved cryptocurrencies.

That doesn’t mean government employees would suddenly be paid their salaries in random internet coins.

It means that if somebody owes the Government money, an approved cryptocurrency could potentially become another way of paying it.

The Government could convert it into pounds.

Supporters see that as encouraging Britain’s digital-finance industry.

Critics can reasonably question whether the additional system is necessary and how government should manage price movements and administrative costs.

5. Create a national cryptocurrency reserve

This is one of the more controversial proposals.

Reform proposed creating a government Bitcoin reserve.

This is where Bitcoin specifically enters the story.

Governments already hold different types of financial reserves.

The idea would be to hold some Bitcoin as another national asset.

Supporters argue that if Bitcoin becomes increasingly important and valuable internationally, Britain would already own some.

Critics point to its considerable price volatility and question why taxpayers should be exposed to that risk.

Again, that’s a legitimate argument to have.

So why does Reform want Britain to be “crypto-friendly”?

Because cryptocurrency and digital finance are already a large international industry.

The question for governments is partly where those businesses, investors and jobs will go.

Countries compete for financial businesses in much the same way they compete for car manufacturers, technology companies and other industries.

Taxes matter.

Regulation matters.

The cost of doing business matters.

Legal certainty matters.

If Britain’s rules are extremely expensive or difficult, companies can potentially establish themselves somewhere else.

But there is another side.

If Britain’s rules are too weak, consumers can be exposed to scams, badly managed companies and financial crime.

So there is a balance.

Too much regulation can drive legitimate business away.

Too little regulation can allow bad businesses and criminals in.

Finding that balance is the actual debate.

Does “deregulation” mean removing all the rules?

No.

And this is probably the single most useful thing to remember when this argument comes up.

Deregulation can mean removing, simplifying or changing particular regulations.

It does not automatically mean abolishing every law governing an industry.

You could remove ten unnecessary regulations while keeping twenty important ones.

You could simplify the process legitimate businesses follow while increasing penalties for criminals.

You could make it easier to open a legitimate cryptocurrency business while continuing to require checks designed to prevent money laundering.

Whether a particular piece of deregulation is sensible depends upon which regulation is being changed.

Simply shouting “DEREGULATION!” doesn’t answer that question.

So what’s the sensible answer when somebody says:

“Reform wants to deregulate crypto and that will help money launderers”?

Try this:

“Which regulation do you mean?”

Because that’s the important question.

Reform’s published proposal did advocate lighter regulation and temporary exemptions from selected rules.

But it also explicitly said the core safeguards against money laundering and terrorist financing would remain during its proposed regulatory trial.

And cryptocurrency businesses operating in Britain are already subject to UK anti-money-laundering requirements.

So somebody can reasonably disagree with Reform’s proposed level of regulation.

They can argue the tax rate is too low.

They can oppose a national Bitcoin reserve.

They can argue that the proposed regulatory experiment removes particular protections they believe should remain.

Those are perfectly legitimate political arguments.

But:

“Deregulation means criminals can launder money”

doesn’t explain which regulation is supposedly being removed or whether that regulation has anything to do with money laundering in the first place.

THE REALLY SHORT VERSION

If you’ve got this far and your brain hurts, remember five things.

1. Your money is already mostly digital.

The number you see in your banking app is a computer record.

2. Cryptocurrency is another way of owning and transferring value digitally.

The major difference is that it can work without an ordinary bank keeping the central record.

3. Cryptocurrency can be used by criminals.

So can cash, banks, companies and property. That’s why sensible anti-crime rules matter.

4. “Deregulation” does not mean “no laws.”

It means changing or removing particular regulations. You need to know which regulation before deciding whether removing it is sensible.

5. Reform’s published proposal did not say “abolish the money-laundering rules.”

Its proposed two-year regulatory trial specifically retained the main anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing safeguards.

So when somebody says:

“Reform wants to deregulate cryptocurrency!”

The useful question isn’t:

“Is deregulation good or bad?”

It’s:

“Which rule does Reform want to remove, what does that rule currently do, and what protection would actually be lost by changing it?”

Once you ask that question, you’re having the real argument instead of arguing about a scary word.
