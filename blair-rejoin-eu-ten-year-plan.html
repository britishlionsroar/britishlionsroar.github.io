Blair Puts Rejoining the EU Back on the Table

FREE MOVEMENT. EU PAYMENTS. THE SINGLE MARKET. THE CUSTOMS UNION. BRITAIN’S OLD RED LINES — ALL BACK ON THE TABLE.
Steve English • 24 September 2026

Ten years after Britain voted to leave the European Union, Tony Blair’s institute has said something that until recently many politicians preferred to avoid saying openly:

Britain should set itself the objective of rejoining the EU.

Not tomorrow. Not necessarily under the EU as it exists today. But within the next decade, if the conditions are right.

The Tony Blair Institute for Global Change published Beyond Leave and Remain: A Path to Rejoining a Reformed Europe on 24 September 2026. It calls for a clear, public objective to rejoin the EU within a decade, subject to reform within the EU itself. The paper argues that Britain should approach negotiations without treating the old post-referendum red lines as untouchable.

That means reconsidering the single market, customs union, financial contributions and — perhaps most significantly — free movement of people.

That does not make Blair’s proposal government policy. But it puts a question firmly back into British politics that many voters may have thought had been settled in 2016.

If Britain followed Blair’s road back to Brussels, what would we actually be signing up to?

Rejoining Isn’t “Cancelling Brexit”

Britain cannot simply announce that Brexit has been reversed and resume its old membership on its old terms.

Article 50(5) of the Treaty on European Union provides that a state which has withdrawn and later asks to rejoin must go through the procedure in Article 49.

Under Article 49, an applicant submits its application to the Council. The Council acts unanimously after consulting the European Commission and obtaining the consent of the European Parliament. The conditions of admission and treaty adjustments are then set out in an agreement requiring ratification by all contracting states in accordance with their constitutional requirements.

In plain English: Britain would be applying to join again. Rejoining is not simply reversing the Brexit switch.

All EU member states would have a say. There would have to be an accession agreement. And Britain’s conditions of membership would have to be negotiated.

That matters because the Britain that left the EU was not an ordinary member.

Britain’s Old Special Deal Doesn’t Automatically Come Back

During its membership Britain accumulated a collection of exceptions and special arrangements. The most famous included keeping sterling rather than joining the euro and remaining outside the passport-free Schengen area.

The crucial point is that a returning Britain could not simply assume that every historical exemption would be restored.

Today’s accession framework requires applicants to take on the obligations of membership and implement the EU’s body of law — the acquis — subject to whatever terms are actually negotiated in an accession agreement.

Consider the euro. The European Commission states that all EU member states except Denmark are expected to adopt the euro once they fulfil the necessary conditions. There is no universal deadline, which is why several EU countries have remained outside the euro for many years.

Denmark possesses a treaty opt-out. Britain used to possess its own special arrangements, but a newly acceding Britain could not simply assume its former exemption still existed.

That does not mean Britain would automatically have to abandon sterling on the day it rejoined. It means that preserving a permanent British euro exemption would itself be a matter for negotiation rather than an inherited right.

And Then There Is Free Movement

Here the Blair Institute paper is unusually direct. Rather than pretending this question can be avoided, it argues that free movement should be back on the table.

Its case is that ending EU free movement did not deliver the reduction in overall immigration many Brexit supporters expected, while Britain lost economic benefits associated with the movement of workers.

Whether voters accept that argument is another matter. But the political significance is difficult to miss.

For millions of Leave voters, taking back control of immigration was not a minor technical feature of Brexit. It was one of its central issues.

A serious attempt to rejoin therefore brings the question straight back: would Britain accept free movement again as part of the price of membership?

Britain Is Already Moving Closer to the EU — Without Rejoining It

This is where the story becomes more complicated.

The current Government has not adopted Blair’s proposal to rejoin. But Britain and the EU have already been rebuilding parts of their relationship.

At the May 2025 UK-EU summit, the two sides agreed a new strategic partnership covering trade, food and agricultural standards, energy, emissions trading, security and defence, youth arrangements and other areas. A formal UK-EU Security and Defence Partnership was also signed.

In April 2026, Britain and the EU finalised the agreement for the UK to participate in Erasmus+ from 2027, with the Government saying it had negotiated a discount against the default contribution methodology.

The two sides have also been working towards agreements concerning electricity-market participation, emissions trading and sanitary and phytosanitary rules.

The May 2025 Common Understanding is particularly worth reading. It envisages an electricity agreement involving dynamic alignment with relevant EU rules and recognises the role of the Court of Justice of the European Union within the proposed framework.

That is not EU membership. Nor does it prove that these agreements form part of a secret plan to rejoin.

The Government argues that closer cooperation removes barriers, lowers costs and delivers practical benefits while Britain remains outside the EU.

But it demonstrates something important: the post-Brexit relationship is already changing. Blair now wants Britain to go considerably further.

First Erasmus. Then Energy. Then Carbon. Then What?

The official UK-EU programme includes work towards arrangements covering the internal electricity market, a Youth Experience Scheme, a common Sanitary and Phytosanitary area and linkage between the UK and EU Emissions Trading Systems.

Supporters argue that agreements of this kind solve practical problems created by Brexit.

Critics can ask how much regulatory independence Britain is willing to exchange for easier access to European markets.

That question becomes especially interesting because Blair’s paper also wants Britain to preserve the ability to move quickly in fields such as artificial intelligence, biotechnology and medicines.

The closer Britain integrates into EU markets, the more important the question becomes of whose rules govern those markets.

Full EU membership ultimately means participating in the EU legal and institutional order and accepting the obligations attached to membership. Whether that bargain is worthwhile is a political judgement for voters and governments. But the obligations themselves cannot simply be ignored.

Even Defence Cooperation Shows How Hard Negotiations Can Be

The UK-EU reset also opened negotiations over British participation in European defence initiatives, including access connected to the EU’s SAFE programme.

Those discussions illustrated a wider point: cooperation with Brussels is not simply a matter of Britain requesting access and receiving it on British terms.

Each arrangement involves negotiations over money, rules, eligibility and mutual obligations.

The same would be true on a vastly larger scale in a future accession negotiation. Article 49 requires unanimity. Twenty-seven governments would have interests of their own.

Blair’s Crucial Qualification: A “Reformed EU”

There is another enormous question inside the proposal.

Blair’s institute does not simply say Britain should rejoin the EU as it exists today. It proposes rejoining a significantly reformed European Union.

The paper envisages a Europe better able to compete with the United States and China, with stronger defence, energy and technological capabilities and changes intended to improve European competitiveness and sovereignty.

But Britain is no longer an EU member. It cannot redesign the European Union unilaterally from outside it.

Fundamental treaty reforms would require agreement among EU member states according to the applicable treaty procedures.

So Blair’s strategy effectively depends upon two major projects developing together:

THE EU MUST CHANGE.

AND BRITAIN MUST CHANGE ITS RELATIONSHIP WITH THE EU.

Neither process is entirely within the control of a British government.

And What About the Pound?

This deserves more attention than it normally receives.

The European Commission’s current position is that all EU members except Denmark are required to adopt the euro once they meet the convergence criteria.

There is no fixed timetable. Sweden and several Central and Eastern European members demonstrate how long a country can remain outside the euro in practice.

But Britain’s former permanent opt-out cannot simply be assumed to accompany a future application.

Any politician promising both EU re-entry and a permanent guarantee for sterling would eventually have to explain how that exemption would be secured in the accession negotiations.

Perhaps an agreement could be reached. Perhaps it could not. That uncertainty is precisely the point: the terms would have to be negotiated.

The Same Problem Applies to Every Old Red Line

The political debate about “rejoining” can make it sound like reversing a switch. It isn’t.

Would free movement return?

Would Britain contribute to the EU budget, and how much?

Would Britain regain its old opt-outs?

Would Britain secure a permanent exemption from the euro?

What relationship would Britain have with Schengen?

How much independent regulatory freedom would Britain retain?

What would happen to Britain’s independent trade policy?

Which EU laws would Britain have to adopt before accession?

What role would the European Court of Justice resume?

And what happens if one EU member state refuses Britain’s proposed terms?

Those are not details to be sorted out afterwards. They are part of the substance of membership.

And Then the British Public Would Have to Decide

There is another problem with treating “Rejoin” as a simple mirror image of the 2016 question.

Public support for the idea of rejoining is not necessarily the same as support for whatever terms might actually be negotiated.

A voter may favour closer relations with Europe while opposing free movement. Another may support single-market participation while refusing euro membership. Another may support membership only if particular British exemptions are restored. Another may oppose membership under any circumstances.

Eventually the question cannot simply be: “Do you regret Brexit?” It becomes: “Would you accept the actual terms negotiated for Britain’s return?”

Ten Years After the Referendum, the Argument Is Back

The 2016 referendum produced a clear result:

17,410,742 — LEAVE

16,141,241 — REMAIN

Britain subsequently left the European Union.

A decade later, Tony Blair’s institute is proposing that rejoining should become an explicit national objective.

Supporters are entitled to make their case for it. Opponents are entitled to challenge it. But voters are entitled to know exactly what the destination might involve.

Britain would not simply be walking back through the door it left in 2020 and picking up its old membership card.

It would be negotiating a new accession agreement.

And under the framework that exists today, Britain’s former privileges, exemptions and red lines cannot simply be assumed.

FREE MOVEMENT IS BACK ON THE TABLE.

FINANCIAL CONTRIBUTIONS ARE BACK ON THE TABLE.

THE SINGLE MARKET IS BACK ON THE TABLE.

THE CUSTOMS UNION IS BACK ON THE TABLE.

AND NOW EU MEMBERSHIP ITSELF IS BACK ON THE TABLE.

The argument Britain thought it had settled in 2016 has returned.
