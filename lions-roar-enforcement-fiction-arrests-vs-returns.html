The Enforcement Fiction — How Labour Publicised Arrests While Hiding the Return Rate

The government announced record immigration arrests. It did not announce that fewer than 1 in 8 arrests resulted in removal. It did not announce a 3% return rate on small boat arrivals. It did not announce that it had quietly stopped publishing the exit checks data that would have made all of this visible.

What This Article Establishes

  • In 2025, immigration enforcement made 8,971 arrests. 1,087 people were removed. 7 in every 8 people arrested were released back into the community.
  • Between July 2024 and December 2025, 12,322 arrests produced 1,726 total returns — a removal rate of 14%.
  • Between June 2024 and June 2025, 58,000 asylum claims were refused. Fewer than 11,000 people were removed — a 5:1 failure ratio.
  • Of the approximately 166,000 people who arrived by small boat between 2018 and 2024, around 4,995 have been returned — a 3% return rate.
  • The government stopped publishing exit checks data — the metric that would have made the true overstayer and return figures publicly visible. The House of Lords committee confirmed this on 23 June 2026 and demanded its immediate reinstatement.
  • Arrest statistics were publicised through press releases. Return statistics were buried in quarterly bulletins. This was not accidental.

This article does not require inference or editorial argument. The numbers are the story. Every figure below is drawn directly from official government publications. Readers are invited to read those publications in full.

The Two Numbers the Government Never Put in the Same Sentence

Throughout 2024 and 2025, the Home Office issued press releases announcing immigration enforcement operations. The releases carried headlines about raids conducted, about businesses visited, about the number of individuals encountered and arrested. The language was active, purposeful, and designed to convey a government in command of the situation.

There is a second number that never appeared in those press releases. It is the number of people who were actually removed from the United Kingdom as a result of all that enforcement activity. It appeared instead in the quarterly statistical releases published by the Home Office — documents that receive a fraction of the press coverage given to enforcement operation announcements, and which require active effort to locate and read.

The gap between those two numbers — 7,884 people arrested and not removed in a single year — is the operational reality of UK immigration enforcement. It is not a rounding error. It is not a processing lag that will correct itself in subsequent quarters. It is the structural outcome of an enforcement system that has the legal authority to arrest but lacks the detention capacity, the removal infrastructure, and the political will to follow arrest through to removal at any meaningful scale.

The Full Enforcement Funnel: What Happens After Arrest

The journey from arrest to removal in the UK immigration system passes through several stages, and attrition occurs at every one of them. The enforcement data for 2025 makes this visible in full.

8,971 Arrested by immigration enforcement in 2025. Press releases issued. Ministerial statements made.
2,251 Detained following arrest. The remainder — over 6,700 — were processed and released back into the community at the point of arrest or shortly after.
1,087 Removed from the United Kingdom. Of those detained, fewer than half were ultimately removed. Legal challenges, capacity constraints, and country agreement failures account for the gap.
7,884 Arrested but not removed. Still in the United Kingdom. Some will have subsequently absconded. Some are in legal proceedings. Most are simply back in the community.

The detention figure is instructive. 2,251 people were detained out of 8,971 arrested — meaning the large majority of those encountered by immigration enforcement were not even detained, let alone removed. Detention requires available spaces in immigration removal centres, and the UK's detention estate is finite and frequently at capacity. Arrest without detention almost never leads to removal.

Of the 2,251 detained, 1,087 were removed. The other 1,164 were released from detention — through bail applications, legal challenges, or the expiry of detention time limits — without removal. They returned to the community having been arrested, detained, and then freed, with the full knowledge of the Home Office.

The Wider Removal Failure: Asylum and Small Boats

The 2025 enforcement figures are not an anomaly. They reflect a consistent pattern across the full range of the government's removal activity.

Asylum claims refused: 58,000

People removed: fewer than 11,000

For every five people told by the British state that their asylum claim had failed and that they had no legal right to remain in the United Kingdom, fewer than one was actually removed. The other four remain. Where they are is, in many cases, unknown.

3% Return rate — small boat arrivals 2018 to 2024 (approx. 166,000 arrived; 4,995 returned)

The small boats figure requires no elaboration. Between 2018 and 2024, approximately 166,000 people crossed the Channel in small boats and arrived in the United Kingdom. Approximately 4,995 have been returned. That is a return rate of 3 per cent across six years of crossings.

The other 97 per cent remain. The government does not know, in most cases, precisely where. It knows they are here because there is no credible mechanism by which 161,000 people who crossed the world to reach Britain then quietly departed with no record. They are in British communities, using British services, and in the overwhelming majority of cases, entirely outside any form of immigration oversight.

The Data the Government Stopped Publishing

Exit checks data is the metric that would make the full scale of return failure visible. When a person leaves the United Kingdom through a controlled port — an airport, a ferry terminal, the Channel Tunnel — their departure is recorded. The aggregate of those records, set against the records of who entered, produces the overstayer figure: the number of people who entered legally and did not leave when their permission to remain expired.

The government stopped publishing exit checks data. The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee, reporting on 23 June 2026, confirmed this and demanded its immediate reinstatement. The Lords committee found that as a direct consequence, the government does not know the current number of people who have overstayed their leave to remain in the United Kingdom. It described the data situation as "woefully inadequate."

"Woefully inadequate data on migration." — House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee, Settlement, Citizenship and Integration, 23 June 2026

The timing of the decision to stop publishing exit checks data is not examined in this article — the committee's report does not pinpoint a date. What is established by the committee's findings is the consequence: the government cannot tell Parliament, or the public, how many people have overstayed. It cannot produce a figure. It has stopped collecting, or stopped publishing, the data that would generate that figure.

Read alongside the enforcement pipeline — 8,971 arrests, 1,087 removals, 7,884 not removed — the absence of exit checks data is not a bureaucratic gap. It is the completion of a picture in which every metric that would reveal the true scale of removal failure has either been withheld, buried in statistical bulletins, or quietly discontinued.

The Press Release Strategy: Activity vs Outcome

The government's communication approach to immigration enforcement has been consistent and deliberate: publicise activity, suppress outcome. The distinction matters because the two metrics measure entirely different things.

Activity metrics — arrests, raids, operations — measure the inputs of the enforcement system. They tell you how busy the system is. They convey dynamism. They imply effectiveness without demonstrating it. A government that has conducted 8,971 arrests can truthfully describe itself as having conducted record enforcement activity.

Outcome metrics — removals, return rates, departure confirmations — measure what the system actually produces. They tell you whether enforcement activity translates into the result it exists to deliver: the removal of people who have no legal right to be in the United Kingdom. A government with a 12% removal-to-arrest ratio and a 3% small boats return rate cannot truthfully describe its enforcement as effective.

What Keir Starmer Said on 22 June 2026

In his resignation statement delivered outside 10 Downing Street, Keir Starmer claimed "a decrease in undocumented migration" as part of his account of his government's record.

He did not cite a source. He did not reference the return rate. He did not reference the arrest-to-removal ratio. He did not reference the 53,298 absconders or the 736 convicted criminals among them. He did not reference the exit checks data that his government had stopped publishing.

The Home Office enforcement statistics covering the period of his government were updated four days before his resignation speech. They were publicly available. He chose not to engage with them.

The Public Accounts Committee's June 2026 report confirmed what the enforcement data shows: the asylum system has accumulated a caseload of 100,600 active claimants, a backlog of 80,333 pending appeals averaging 60 weeks each, and a total cost of £4.9 billion in 2024–25. The PAC Chair described control as having been "all but lost." None of this featured in the Prime Minister's resignation statement.

What Honest Enforcement Reporting Would Look Like

An honest government communication about immigration enforcement would lead with removals, not arrests. It would present the full pipeline — encounters, arrests, detentions, removals — in a single table, in every press release, so that the public could evaluate the system's actual performance rather than its activity level.

It would publish exit checks data quarterly, enabling Parliament and the public to track overstayer numbers alongside arrival figures. It would present the refusal-to-removal ratio alongside every asylum decision announcement. It would acknowledge the 3% small boats return rate and explain, specifically and in detail, what it was doing to change it.

None of this occurred. What occurred instead is documented above: arrest figures in the press releases, removal figures in the quarterly statistics, exit checks data discontinued, and a Prime Minister who resigned claiming a decrease in undocumented migration without citing a single metric that could be independently verified.

Reform UK Policy Position

Reform UK has called for mandatory quarterly publication of the full enforcement pipeline — encounters, arrests, detentions, and removals — alongside the refusal-to-removal ratio and cumulative small boats return rate, in a standardised format accessible to the public.

Reform UK has called for the immediate reinstatement of exit checks data publication, consistent with the House of Lords committee recommendation of 23 June 2026.

Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse current demographic trends.

That is the outcome Labour set in motion from 1997 onwards, under Tony Blair, when mass immigration was deliberately embraced as an economic strategy — and it is the outcome the Conservatives later chose to continue.
