THE BRITISH LIONS ROAR

The Purged and the Exiled vs The Principled Walkaways

Why the "Reform is just reheated Tory" line collapses the moment you ask who actually locked the door.

  • Your Party has two MPs — Jeremy Corbyn and Zarah Sultana — both former Labour MPs. Restore Britain has one MP — Rupert Lowe — a former Reform UK MP.
  • Corbyn, Sultana and Lowe were all suspended, blocked from standing, or told there was "no way back" by their former party leaderships before they left.
  • The former Conservative MPs now sitting for Reform UK — Lee Anderson, Robert Jenrick, Danny Kruger, Andrew Rosindell and Suella Braverman — made conscious, mid-term decisions to walk out while they still had the choice to stay.
  • Jenrick was sacked only after Kemi Badenoch's team obtained evidence he was already secretly preparing to defect.
  • The distinction is not spin. It is the difference between being pushed and choosing to jump.
PURPOSE / MISSION: Political commentary and opinion journalism examining the "reheated Tory" claim made against Reform UK, using publicly reported facts about party suspensions, expulsions and defections to test whether the comparison holds.

"But Reform is just a reheated Tory party full of ex-Tories!"

It's one of the most common lines thrown at Reform UK by its critics. So let's actually look at it.

Your Party consists of just two MPs: Jeremy Corbyn and Zarah Sultana — both former Labour MPs. Restore Britain consists of just one MP: Rupert Lowe — a former Reform UK MP. Yet the same people who insist Reform is "reheated Tory" don't call Your Party "reheated Labour," or Restore Britain "Reform with a different badge."

There is a much more important difference than party colour. In any other workplace, if you quit because your boss made your position impossible, or because you found somewhere better, you walked away. If your boss permanently bars you from the building, replaces you, and makes clear you will never be allowed back, you were fired. And if your boss discovers you've already written your resignation letter and shouts "you're fired!" five minutes before you hand it over, that's procedural theatre — they didn't make you leave, they merely discovered you were already leaving.

The Purged and Exiled

Jeremy Corbyn

Corbyn had the Labour whip removed in 2020 after the Equality and Human Rights Commission found the party had breached the Equality Act, and he was accused of failing to fully accept its findings on antisemitism. The suspension was never fully lifted at the parliamentary level, and despite "unanimous support" from his local constituency party, Corbyn was blocked from standing as a Labour candidate at the 2024 general election. Once he confirmed he would stand as an independent, he was formally expelled from the party. Labour didn't wait for him to walk; it locked the door first, then framed his departure as his own choice.

Zarah Sultana

Sultana technically resigned from Labour — but look at why. She was one of seven Labour MPs who had the whip withdrawn in July 2024 for voting for an SNP amendment to scrap the two-child benefit cap. When the initial six-month suspension period ended, four of the seven had the whip restored and rejoined the parliamentary party — but Sultana remained suspended after continuing to criticise government policy. She left Labour in July 2025 to co-found a new party with Corbyn. She wasn't handed a way back in. She was pushed out of the window and it got called a jump.

Rupert Lowe

Lowe didn't get a parliamentary timeout either. Reform UK suspended his whip in March 2025 following complaints of bullying, and he sat as an independent for a year before founding his own party, Restore Britain. Crucially, Nigel Farage told The Telegraph that Lowe "knows there's no way back," accusing him of wanting to "cause maximum destruction and damage" to the party. That is not a leadership leaving the door ajar. That is the leadership publicly bolting it shut.

Compare all of that with the former Conservatives who now sit for Reform.

The Principled Walkaways

These MPs weren't merely fleeing a damaged political brand. They were actively rejecting a Conservative leadership they believed had abandoned conservative principles — and choosing Reform because it represented policies their former party no longer had the courage or conviction to deliver.

Lee Anderson

When Anderson joined Reform, he didn't claim he had nowhere else to go. He opened his defection speech with a simple declaration: "I want my country back." He went further, telling reporters: "I cannot be a part of an organisation which stifles free speech," pointing to his own suspension for remarks about Sadiq Khan as proof the party had turned on its own members for speaking their minds. The Conservatives left the door open for his return. He chose Reform and slammed it shut himself.

Danny Kruger

Kruger wasn't some forgotten backbencher looking for attention — he was serving as Shadow Work and Pensions Secretary when he crossed the floor. His verdict on the party was blunt: "We have had a year of stasis and drift and the sham unity that comes from not doing anything bold or difficult or controversial." Then came the line that made headlines: "This is my tragic conclusion: the Conservative Party is over, over as a national party, over as the principal opposition to the left." Kruger didn't believe conservatism was dead. He believed the Conservative Party was no longer capable of defending it.

Andrew Rosindell

Rosindell had been a Conservative since he was fourteen and represented the party in Parliament for nearly twenty-five years. He did not walk away lightly. For him, the party's failure to hold the government to account over the surrender of the Chagos Islands was, in his own words, "a clear red line." Announcing his resignation, he said "the time has come to put country before party." Rosindell didn't leave because the Conservatives rejected him. He rejected a Conservative Party he believed would no longer defend Britain's sovereignty.

Suella Braverman

When Suella Braverman joined Reform, her first words said everything: "I feel like I've come home." She praised Nigel Farage as the only man in British politics who had been "courageously consistent" on the issues facing the country, then turned on her former party: "I'm calling time on Tory betrayal. I'm calling time on Tory lies. I'm calling time on a party that keeps making promises with zero intention of keeping them." Braverman didn't join Reform because the Conservatives had barred her from standing or left her politically homeless. She joined because, after thirty years in the party, she believed Reform was now the political home of the principles the Tories had abandoned.

Robert Jenrick

Jenrick's decision had already been made before the Conservatives discovered his planned defection. In his speech unveiling him as a Reform MP, he didn't pull punches: "The two main parties are rotten. They are no longer fit for purpose. They both broke Britain and neither can fix it." His charge wasn't merely that the Conservatives had failed — it was that they remained in denial, refusing to admit Britain was broken or accept responsibility for breaking it. The Tories found his defection speech before he could deliver it and sacked him first. But he had already made his choice. He had already rejected them.

That Is the Difference

Corbyn, Sultana and Lowe created new political homes after their former parties had cast them out, frozen them out or declared there was no way back.

Anderson, Kruger, Rosindell, Braverman and Jenrick chose Reform because they believed the Conservative Party had ceased to defend free speech, national sovereignty, controlled immigration, cultural confidence and conservative principles.

One group needed somewhere else to go. The other group decided Britain needed somewhere better to go.

They weren't fleeing a Conservative label. They were rejecting a Conservative Party that was no longer conservative — and choosing Reform UK as its replacement.
