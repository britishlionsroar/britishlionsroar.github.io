1.34 Million Households Waiting for Social Housing. Where Are the Homes?

The Government celebrates falling net migration as new figures reveal nearly 179,000 children living in temporary accommodation. Britain's housing shortage remains far from resolved.

By Steve English • Independent British writer and editor • 27 November 2026

Yesterday, the Government celebrated net migration falling to 77,000.

Yesterday also saw the publication of another set of government statistics.

In England alone, 136,280 households are living in temporary accommodation.

Among them are 178,690 children.

More than 1.34 million households are on social housing waiting lists.

These are the figures that need to be considered alongside Britain's immigration statistics.

Beyond the 77,000 headline

As reported yesterday in Labour: The Party of Spin, the headline net migration figure tells only one part of the story.

The latest annual immigration statistics reveal:

  • 629,000 foreign nationals arriving as long-term migrants.
  • 258,000 net migration from outside the EU+ area.
  • 545,000 grants of permanent immigration status, including EU settled status.
  • 265,000 grants of British citizenship.
  • More than 93,000 people receiving Home Office asylum support.

Annual net migration may have fallen, but the accumulated housing requirements arising from previous years of population growth have not disappeared.

Closing hotels does not end housing demand

The latest Home Office figures show that more than 72,000 asylum seekers are living in dispersed accommodation, principally residential houses and flats.

Government contractors actively source accommodation from private property owners.

Those subsequently granted refugee status can enter the mainstream housing market and, subject to eligibility, seek social housing assistance.

Closing an asylum hotel may reduce hotel expenditure. It does not necessarily remove the need to accommodate its former residents.

Britain's housing supply is struggling

England delivered 203,690 net additional homes in 2025/26, a reduction of 2% compared with the previous year.

Meanwhile, the UK's population has grown by nearly four million since 2016.

The number of homes available to rent has fallen by 26% over the same decade.

Whether the Government is planning immigration policy, reforming the private rental market or procuring accommodation through the asylum system, each decision needs to be considered against the country's existing housing capacity.

Housing policy and immigration policy cannot be treated as entirely separate issues.

The question for ministers is how they intend to reconcile their immigration, asylum and housing policies with the needs of the millions of people already struggling to find affordable accommodation.
