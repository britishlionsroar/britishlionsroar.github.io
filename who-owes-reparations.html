Who Actually Owes Reparations? The Slave Trade Facts They Don't Teach You

The reparations debate has been framed almost entirely around Britain and the transatlantic slave trade. The historical record — from Arab slave markets to African ruling elites who supplied the chains — tells a far more complicated story.
  • The Arab-Trans-Saharan slave trade operated for over 1,300 years — nine centuries longer than the entire transatlantic trade
  • Up to 18 million Africans were enslaved through Arab and Islamic networks, with African ruling elites serving as primary suppliers
  • Britain's share of transatlantic slave purchasing amounts to approximately 3% — meaning most Black Britons statistically descend from enslavers, not the enslaved
  • Britain not only abolished its own slave trade but enforced abolition globally, at a cost of hundreds of Royal Navy lives
  • British taxpayers paid compensation to end the legal slave trade — a debt not finally settled until 2015
  • Barbary raiders enslaved British coastal communities for over a century before Britain entered the transatlantic trade
This article is part of an ongoing programme of political commentary, news analysis, and opinion journalism — examining the evidence behind the reparations debate without institutional deference or selective history.

The Trade Nobody Mentions

When politicians and activists demand reparations from Britain for slavery, they invoke a specific, partial history. The transatlantic slave trade — brutal, well-documented, and relatively recent — becomes the entire frame. What gets omitted is a system of enslavement that dwarfed it in duration, in scale, and in the deliberate erasure of African lineage: the Arab and Trans-Saharan slave trade.

This system began around 652 CE and continued in various forms until mid-twentieth century legal abolitions in the Arabian Peninsula. Thirteen centuries of organised human trafficking. Historians estimate that up to 18 million people were enslaved through these networks over that period — compared to approximately 12.5 million transported in the entire transatlantic trade, across all European nations combined.

The Arab trade outlasted the transatlantic system by nine centuries. It is not a footnote. It is the longest-running organised slave trade in recorded human history.

What "Domestic Servitude" Actually Meant

Academic texts frequently classify the high volume of female captives in the Arab trade as "domestic servants." Critics — including a number of African historians — argue this terminology functions as a sustained euphemism for systematic sexual violence. Women and girls captured for harems or concubinage were subjected to forced sexual exploitation on a vast scale. This is not a matter of contested interpretation; it is documented in the primary sources of the societies that operated these systems.

The fate of male captives was equally extreme. To supply palace guards and prevent the enslaved population from producing descendants who might threaten ruling dynasties, millions of African men and boys were subjected to forced castration. Historians estimate the mortality rate from this procedure alone was between 50% and 90%. It did not just destroy individuals — it systematically eliminated potential generations, which is why there is no large diaspora of African descent in the Arab world today comparable to those in the Americas or Europe.

"Neither mass castration nor the systematic sexual enslavement of captives featured in the Western transatlantic trade. Comparing these systems as equivalent forms of bondage minimises what was done in both."

Who Actually Did the Enslaving?

The reparations narrative requires a straightforward moral ledger: white European buyers, Black African victims. The historical record is considerably more complicated.

West and East African elites — kings, chiefs, and rulers — captured and sold prisoners of war, criminals, and rivals to both European and Arab merchants. The transatlantic trade was not a European raid on a peaceful continent. It was, in large part, a commercial relationship between African suppliers and European purchasers. The demand drove it; the supply chains were African-operated.

When one accounts for the fact that African slaves had been enslaved and sold to the majority of non-Western countries long before Europe entered the transatlantic market, a further question arises: by the time British plantation owners needed labour, the only competitive source was the existing African slave supply network. The infrastructure of African enslavement pre-existed British participation by centuries.

This framing places responsibility on the purchasers rather than the African rulers and traders who operated the supply chains. It is a selective moral framework — one that has been adopted almost entirely for political convenience.

What Britain Actually Did

The British working class — through political agitation and moral pressure — forced their government not only to end Britain's participation in the slave trade, but to pursue global abolition and enforce it with naval power. This is not national mythology; it is a matter of parliamentary and military record. The Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron, operational from 1808 to 1860, intercepted slave ships and liberated an estimated 150,000 enslaved people. Approximately 1,600 Royal Navy personnel died in this campaign — from combat, disease, and the hazards of West African waters.

The British working class also recognised a practical reality: abolishing the trade overnight would destabilise major commercial enterprises and destroy employment. Their solution was to agree to a government compensation scheme — paying slave owners for the loss of what had been, under the law of the time, legal property. This was not a celebration of slavery. It was a pragmatic mechanism to achieve abolition without economic collapse.

The loan raised to fund that compensation — under the Slave Compensation Act 1837 — was so large that the final payment was not made until 2015. Within living memory. People who are alive today paid taxes that serviced a debt incurred to end a trade that Africans created, maintained, and profited from for over a millennium.

The 3% Question

Britain's purchasing of enslaved Africans accounts for approximately 3% of the entire transatlantic slave trade. The majority of transatlantic voyages were Portuguese, Brazilian, and Spanish. Britain entered the trade relatively late and exited it first, as a deliberate act of political will.

The statistical implication for Black Britons is significant. If Britain purchased roughly 3% of transatlantic slaves, then a Black British person of West African descent has approximately a 3% chance of having had a British-owned enslaved ancestor. They have a considerably higher statistical probability of descending from the African ruling class that operated the supply chains — the kings, chiefs, and merchants who sold human beings to Arab, European, and domestic buyers alike.

The reparations movement is not, therefore, asking the descendants of victims to compensate other descendants of victims. It is asking the descendants of people who ended the trade, at financial and human cost, to pay the descendants of people whose ancestors overwhelmingly profited from it — while directing zero demands toward the Arab nations whose ancestors ran a larger and longer system, or toward the African nations whose ruling classes built the supply infrastructure.

Before Britain Ever Traded a Slave

There is one further element of this history that rarely appears in the reparations debate. For over a century before Britain entered the transatlantic slave trade, Barbary pirates — operating from North Africa — conducted raids on British coastal communities. Villages in Cornwall, Devon, Ireland, and as far north as Iceland were targeted. The inhabitants were seized and sold into slavery in North African markets. Historian Robert C. Davis estimated that between one and one and a quarter million Europeans were enslaved by Barbary raiders between 1530 and 1780.

British people were enslaved. Not metaphorically, not through economic exploitation — physically seized from their homes and sold in slave markets. This chapter of history is almost entirely absent from the contemporary reparations debate, because it complicates a narrative that requires white Europeans to occupy only one role in the story of slavery.

"The British working class forced their government to end a trade created and perpetuated by African and Arab slave networks — then paid for it, in blood and in taxes, for nearly two hundred years."

So Who Owes Whom?

The reparations debate, as currently conducted in British politics, is not a serious engagement with history. It is a selective moral prosecution that applies one standard to European nations and none at all to the African and Arab states whose ruling classes operated the longer, larger, and — in many respects — more brutal systems.

Labour politicians supporting reparations claims against Britain are not reckoning with the past. They are ratifying a politically convenient fiction: that slavery was something done by Europeans to Africans, full stop. The record does not support that fiction. It never did.

The question of who owes whom — when examined honestly, using the sources that reparations advocates never cite — has an answer that does not flatter the reparations movement. Britain paid once, in lives and in money. The suggestion that it should pay again, to the descendants of those who created and ran the system it dismantled, is not justice. It is historical inversion.
