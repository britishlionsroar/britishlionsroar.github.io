A Christmas Miracle in Basingstoke: How Labour's Early Release Scheme Sent an ISIS Execution Guard Shopping in Windsor

Mohammed Yaseen broke into Britain on a small boat and got a hotel room, a two-year sentence and Labour's early release discount. The only thing that put him back inside was a newspaper photographer.

By Steve English · Independent British writer and editor · 4 November 2026
  • A convicted ISIS member, who joined the al-Qaeda cell known for beheading Ken Bigley, crossed the Channel in December 2025 under a false name.
  • He was put up in the Crowne Plaza in Basingstoke until his fingerprints caught up with him on Christmas Eve.
  • He got two years for illegal entry and was out in about nine and a half months under Labour's early release rules.
  • His ISIS and al-Qaeda record didn't count. Labour's rules screen out offences, not offenders.
  • He went back inside only after a newspaper photographed him near Windsor Castle. Deportation is stuck on human rights grounds.
PURPOSE: To show, step by step, how Britain's border, sentencing and human rights rules let a convicted terrorist in, let him out early, and still can't send him home, and who wrote those rules.

Mohammed Yaseen. Who is he?

Mohammed Yaseen has a lengthy history of Islamic extremist involvement. He is an Iraqi from Ar-Rutbah who joined al-Qaeda at just 16 and became a member of the cell known for kidnapping and beheading British engineer Ken Bigley in 2004. A German court heard he was involved in bomb attacks that killed American troops in Iraq. He then got a promotion to ISIS. He carried a Kalashnikov, kept the crowds in order at stonings and floggings, and served as a security guard at at least two mass executions where the victims included children. Quite the CV.

He arrived in Germany in 2014, made three asylum claims under different names, and in 2020 was jailed for four years and three months for ISIS membership. Germany banned him for 20 years. Then a German judge ruled he couldn't be sent back to Iraq in case he was mistreated. So he wasn't deported. He simply wandered off to France.

"The UK accepts everyone"

In December 2025, Yaseen crossed the English Channel on a small boat from France. He had seen a viral TikTok video claiming the UK "accepts everyone" and respects human rights, even, it turns out, those of terrorists. He was of course quite correct in his thinking. Successive Uniparty governments (Labour and Conservative) have welcomed anyone and everyone breaking into Britain, including, quite obviously, hard-working terrorists.

He of course used a false name, "Mohammed Ashimiri", and lied about his nationality, claiming to be Kuwaiti. He knew that is all it takes to break into Britain and be welcomed by the Labour Party authorities. He was handed clean clothes, asylum support and accommodation at the lovely Crowne Plaza Hotel in Basingstoke, all at British taxpayers' expense, of course.

On Christmas Eve 2025, a Christmas miracle occurred. His biometric fingerprint checks finally flagged his true identity, eleven days after he had checked in, and he was arrested at the Basingstoke hotel.

Two years, said the judge

It wasn't until July 2026 that he stood in the dock at Winchester Crown Court. The prosecutor told the court he was a danger to the whole of the UK. Judge Christopher Parker KC called him a "significant public risk", then handed him just two years for illegal entry. The judge even said he would very likely be deported. Hold that thought.

Two years, then. Except nobody in Labour's Britain serves two years for a two-year sentence.

Labour's early release discount

In September 2024, one of Labour's first acts in government was to cut the automatic release point for most prisoners from half their sentence to 40%. Terrorism offences were excluded, and you might find that reassuring. Don't.

Labour's prisons minister, Lord Timpson, told the House of Lords exactly how it worked:

"In each case, we have excluded specific offences rather than cohorts of offenders."

He called that a legal necessity, because the power Labour chose to use could only change release dates for types of sentence, not for types of people. So Labour picked an emergency shortcut that, by law, couldn't tell a shoplifter from an ISIS execution guard.

In plain English: it doesn't matter who you are, only what you're in for this time. His crime in this country was illegal entry, not terrorism. So his ISIS and al-Qaeda record didn't count in Britain. As far as Labour's release rules were concerned, he was just another bloke who came over on a boat. Tick the box, open the gate.

Then Labour went further. The Sentencing Act 2026, passed under Starmer and switched on under Andy Burnham, cuts the release point again, to one third. Prisoners already inside are being let out in monthly batches by sentence length. The second batch, sentences between 575 and 882 days, became eligible on 13 October 2026. Yaseen's two years is 730 days, right in the middle of the batch.

Whichever Labour scheme opened the door, it opened that week. Sure enough, the Mail on Sunday found him out and about the following weekend.

Two years, the headlines said. He served about nine and a half months. That is what a prison sentence means in Labour's Britain: a number for the newspapers on sentencing day, and a different number entirely when nobody's looking. The judge says two years. The Ministry of Justice says less than half of that will do. The public hears the first and never finds out about the second. Sentences don't mean anything any more except false headlines.

And it wasn't the "six months" the press reported either. That figure doesn't survive a calculator. It was less than 40% of the sentence the judge handed down, courtesy of the Labour Party.

A stroll to the King's Co-op

And after the headlines and media attention had faded away, he was quietly released on licence. He was housed at a bail hostel in Old Windsor, with an ankle tag and a 6pm to 6am curfew, free to do his shopping at the Co-op a stone's throw from Windsor Castle. Nothing says "significant public risk" like popping out for milk next door to the King.

Then the Mail on Sunday photographed him. Within hours of the Home Office being asked about him, he was back in prison for breaching a "good behaviour" condition. Another Christmas miracle, two months early. The system didn't move when a judge warned it. It moved when a photographer did.

Back on the streets in time for the Christmas crowds?

Under Labour's Sentencing Act, most recalled prisoners now serve 56 days and are then released again, except in exceptional circumstances. Count 56 days from his recall and you land in mid-December: Christmas markets, packed high streets, crowded stations. Whether a man a Crown Court called a danger to the whole of the UK counts as "exceptional" is now in the hands of the same system that let him out the first time. Nobody has said which way it will go. That should worry you more than any answer would.

The human rights wall

Meanwhile the authorities argue the finer points of the European Convention on Human Rights and debate whether or not he can be deported. He has applied to stay on human rights grounds, with legal aid paid for by you. The Foreign Office has reportedly fretted about his possible "mistreatment" in Iraq. That's the same Iraq where he stood guard while other people were mistreated to death.

The Conservative Shadow Home Secretary, Chris Philp, now says: "If Andy Burnham took this seriously, he would deport this man at once." Fair enough. But this is the party that ran the Home Office for fourteen years, watched the boats keep coming, and never left the convention now shielding him. Uniparty, remember.

Germany had him for six years. They convicted him, banned him, and still couldn't get rid of him. Britain is now running the same script, with Labour's early release discount thrown in for good measure. When his sentence ends, the question isn't whether the system will protect the public from Mohammed Yaseen. It's whether it will protect Mohammed Yaseen from the public's wish to see him gone.
