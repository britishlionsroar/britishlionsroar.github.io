The Boats Didn't Stop. They Accelerated.

Labour came to power in July 2024 promising to "smash the gangs" and fix the "broken" asylum system they inherited. Eighteen months on, the numbers are running in the opposite direction.

In 2024, 36,816 people crossed the Channel by small boat — a 25% increase on 2023, and the second highest annual total since records began. In the first six months of 2025 alone, nearly 20,000 people made the crossing — up 48% on the same period in 2024, and 75% higher than the first half of 2023. The full year 2025 came in at 41,472 — more than any year except the 2022 peak under the Conservatives. That brings the total since Labour took office to well over 78,000 by any reasonable accounting of the figures.

The government's response has been to announce legislation, create a new criminal offence of endangering lives at sea, and cite disruptions to smuggling networks. What it has not done is reduce the numbers arriving.

"In 2025, 99% of all those crossing either applied for asylum or were named as a dependant on an application." — Migration Observatory, University of Oxford

Of the 133,000 small boat cases decided by end of 2025, 60% resulted in a grant of protection. Only around 7,500 people — 4% of all arrivals since 2018 — had been returned from the UK by the end of 2025. The pipeline runs almost entirely one way.

Hotels Must Go. But Where?

In June 2025, Chancellor Rachel Reeves stood at the despatch box and pledged to end the "costly use of hotels" to house asylum seekers by 2029 — by the end of this parliament. The Home Office's most senior civil servant confirmed the aim was to get hotel use "down to zero."

By March 2026, the number in hotels had fallen to 20,885 — down sharply from a peak of over 56,000 under the Conservatives and from 36,273 in September 2025. The government will point to this as progress. The question it refuses to answer is the obvious one: where did they go? The answer is dispersal accommodation — ordinary housing stock across the country, funded by local councils, managed by contractors like Serco, increasingly drawn from the private rental market at the expense of British tenants.

The alternatives the government has floated — military sites, former care homes, warehouses, student accommodation — are either limited in scale or, in the case of former military bases, proven by the NAO to be significantly more expensive than hotel accommodation. There is no large-scale replacement ready to absorb 32,000 people, let alone the tens of thousands more who will arrive before the hotels close.

Which leaves ordinary housing.

Serco Comes Calling — and British Tenants Pay the Price

The mechanism is already in operation. Serco — awarded a 10-year, £1.9 billion Home Office contract in 2019 — has been recruiting private landlords across the North West, Midlands and East of England on five-year guaranteed-rent agreements, paying no void periods, covering all repairs, maintenance, utilities and council tax, with Serco acting as the legal tenant.

The offer is straightforward: guaranteed government-backed income for five years, no tenant vetting, no management hassle. The cost to the taxpayer is £14 per night per person — compared with £145 per night in hotels. The deal is structured to be more attractive than the private rental market, especially for HMO landlords operating in an increasingly regulated environment.

What Serco is offering landlords
  • Five-year guaranteed leases at full market rent, funded by the Home Office
  • No void periods — rent paid whether the property is occupied or not
  • All repairs and maintenance covered by Serco
  • Gas, electricity and council tax paid by Serco
  • No landlord fees or property management costs
  • Serco currently manages over 7,000 properties housing more than 30,000 asylum seekers

Members of the House of Lords raised concerns in 2025 that the scheme is structurally incompatible with the Renters' Rights Bill — which bans fixed-term tenancies — because the government is simultaneously offering landlords exactly that. The government is, in other words, removing Section 21 protections from British tenants while offering the same landlords five-year fixed deals to house asylum seekers instead.

The practical consequence: landlords are terminating existing tenancies and evicting British residents to take up the Serco contract. Others are converting properties to HMOs — knowing that council pressure to increase dispersal accommodation makes planning approval near-certain. British tenants lose their homes. The properties fill with asylum seekers on taxpayer-funded leases. The landlord profits. The government hits its dispersal targets.

The Green Belt — Renamed, Reclassified, Released

Simultaneously, in December 2024, the Labour government published its revised National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF). The centrepiece was a new land classification: "Grey Belt."

Grey Belt is defined as land within the existing Green Belt that either was previously developed, or that does not "strongly contribute" to the Green Belt's core purposes — preventing urban sprawl, stopping towns merging, preserving the character of historic towns. Angela Rayner wrote directly to local planning authorities instructing them to review their Green Belt boundaries to identify Grey Belt land and bring it forward for development.

Here is why this matters. The Green Belt covers approximately 1.633 million hectares — roughly 12.5% of England's land area. It has been one of the most politically defended planning designations in British history. The public understands it as protected land. Labour has created a mechanism — "Grey Belt" — which allows development on land that remains designated as Green Belt in the official statistics, because the formal boundary has not changed. It simply gets built on anyway, under a policy that says lower-performing Green Belt land is fair game if there is "demonstrable need."

The official figures show 650 hectares of Green Belt formally released in 2024-25. The Grey Belt loophole means the real figure — land approved for building while still technically designated — is substantially higher and not yet centrally counted.

Planning consultancy Lichfields reported appeal success rates of approximately 75–80% for major residential schemes on Grey Belt sites in the period following the December 2024 NPPF update. Councils that cannot meet their mandatory housing targets are now required to review Green Belt boundaries — and those targets are set centrally, at a level that means most councils cannot meet them without releasing protected land.

The government's target of 1.5 million new homes in five years is not achievable on brownfield land alone. There is not enough of it. The only land in sufficient quantity to meet the target is Green Belt. The "Grey Belt" reclassification is the mechanism. "Greywashing" the Green Belt is the tactic — change the name, not the boundary, and build anyway.

The Council Tax Bill Lands on Your Doormat

Every link in this chain terminates at the same place: the British taxpayer, specifically the council taxpayer.

Local authority social care spending on asylum seekers has doubled since 2019-20, reaching £744 million in 2024-25. Councils are legally required to care for unaccompanied asylum-seeking children the moment they present at a port or coastline. Government grants do not cover the full cost. The Isle of Wight Council alone faced a £548,000 shortfall in 2025-26, funded entirely from local council tax. Hillingdon Council projected a £17 million deficit driven largely by asylum-related costs.

In 2025-26, 103 out of 207 councils in England raised council tax by over £100 for Band D properties. Most raised bills by the maximum permitted 4.99%. Some — Bradford, Birmingham, Newham — were allowed by Angela Rayner to exceed that limit. Meanwhile, services are being cut: library hours, elderly care visits, youth centres, road maintenance.

The cost chain — what councils are picking up
  • Social care for asylum-seeking children — not fully reimbursed by central government
  • Education, language support, integration services — not covered by existing funding arrangements (NAO, Dec 2025)
  • Local authority asylum social care spending: £744 million in 2024-25, doubled since 2019
  • Total UK asylum cost to taxpayer: £3.9 billion in 2025-26, up 141% since 2019
  • NAO 10-year projection: £15.3 billion — revised up from original £4.5 billion estimate

The Question Nobody in Government Will Answer

Put the four facts together and ask the question.

Over 78,000 people have arrived illegally by small boat since Labour took office. The government is committed to closing all asylum hotels by 2029. Alternative large-scale sites are more expensive and insufficient in scale. Councils have been told to increase dispersal of asylum seekers into residential housing across all local authority areas. Serco is already converting private rental stock — displacing British tenants in the process. And Labour has rewritten planning law to make it easier to build on what the public calls the Green Belt.

Where do these people go? The government's answer — processed in silence, without a single minister saying it plainly — is: into the housing stock. Into the communities. Funded by the council taxpayer. On land the British public thought was protected.

Britons won't die out, but they'll become a minority in their own country, with no control over its future.

Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse current demographic trends.

That is the outcome Labour set in motion from 1997 onwards, under Tony Blair, when mass immigration was deliberately embraced as an economic strategy — and it is the outcome the Conservatives later chose to continue.
