FREE PERSONAL CARE — NOT A FREE CARE HOME

Andy Burnham has promised a National Care Service “free at the point of use”. But what exactly becomes free, what will pensioners still pay, and does it solve the care crisis we investigated three months ago?

30 September 2026
  • Free personal care does not mean a free residential care-home place: the Government says bed and board will remain chargeable and means-tested.
  • Property has not disappeared from the equation, and Deferred Payment Agreements remain part of the system.
  • Burnham says care charges will no longer come out of the basic State Pension — but what a council-supported care-home resident will still contribute towards accommodation remains unanswered.
  • The proposed Triple Lock change is a funding source, but the IFS says it will not fund universal social care in the next Parliament without tax rises or cuts elsewhere.
  • The NHS case for reform is stark: Age UK estimates delayed discharge cost £2.66 billion in 2025/26.
Our purpose is simple: to separate the conference promise from the policy underneath it. This fifth article returns to the ownership, workforce, funding and NHS questions examined in our original four-part investigation and tests what Burnham’s National Care Service would actually change — and what it would leave untouched.

Three months ago, The British Lions Roar published a four-part investigation into Britain’s care-home and social-care system. We followed the money, looked at ownership and private provision, examined the workforce, overseas recruitment and pay, and explored the cost to the NHS when patients cannot leave hospital because the care they need is not available.

In Article 4 we asked: if taxpayers are already spending billions supporting this system, is there a better way to build it?

Now Prime Minister Andy Burnham has given his answer. At Labour’s 2026 conference he promised a National Care Service, introduced during the next Parliament, providing free personal care for older people in England according to need rather than ability to pay.

He described it as “free at the point of use”, a service that “starts in the home”, “care that is about people not profit”, and promised “no care charges paid out of your basic state pension”. Those are substantial promises. They do not mean residential care-home bills disappear.

What “free personal care” actually means

The Government defines the proposed free personal care as help with everyday needs such as eating, bathing and using the toilet. It would be based on assessed need rather than ability to pay.

For somebody remaining in their own home, that can mean the personal assistance required to allow them to continue living there. In residential care, however, the bill contains both the cost of providing personal care and the accommodation and living costs of staying in the home. Burnham is proposing to make eligible personal care free; he is not proposing to make the entire residential placement free.

“The service will not cover bed and board.” — GOV.UK, 29 September 2026

The Government says existing means-tested council contributions towards those costs will remain. Free personal care may therefore make a substantial difference to people currently paying for eligible care, but “National Care Service” and “free at the point of use” should not be read as a promise that care-home bills disappear.

Your house has not disappeared from the equation

Burnham told conference that the National Care Service would protect people’s homes and savings. Under England’s 2026/27 charging rules, however, the upper capital limit remains £23,250 and the lower limit £14,250. Above the upper limit, a person requiring permanent residential care will generally be expected to meet the full cost, subject to the detailed charging rules and disregards.

The proposal does not abolish means testing for care-home accommodation or the relevance of property. Existing protection will continue where a partner or dependent relative remains in the home, while Deferred Payment Agreements will remain available for eligible people. That can avoid a forced sale during a person’s lifetime, but a deferred bill remains a bill.

The state-pension question

Burnham promised “no care charges paid out of your basic state pension”, saying older people with nothing more than the State Pension, or only a little more, should no longer face those charges.

For somebody receiving care at home, the existing rules help explain the promise. In 2026/27 a single person over Pension Credit age receiving council-arranged care outside a care home must ordinarily be left with a Minimum Income Guarantee of £241.45 a week. The full new State Pension is £241.30.

Residential care is different. A council-supported care-home resident must currently be left with a Personal Expenses Allowance of £31.80 a week. On a full new State Pension, that can leave roughly £209.50 a week available to contribute towards assessed care-home costs.

THE UNANSWERED QUESTION

Under the proposed National Care Service, will a council-supported care-home resident whose only income is the State Pension continue to contribute all but the Personal Expenses Allowance towards accommodation costs? If not, what will they contribute?

The Government has said personal-care charges will end but bed and board will remain means-tested. It has not yet answered that specific question.

Scotland shows why “free” needs explaining

Burnham’s proposal is frequently compared with Scotland. From April 2026, Scotland’s fixed contribution towards eligible personal care for a self-funding care-home resident is £260.30 a week, with a further £117.10 where nursing care is required. Published 2026 figures put average self-funder residential care in Scotland at around £1,539 a week.

England has not published the detailed mechanism it would use, so the Scottish figures are not a forecast of English payments. They demonstrate the principle: free personal care can coexist with a substantial residential-care bill.

Who pays for the National Care Service?

Burnham’s answer begins with the State Pension Triple Lock. The existing system guarantees annual increases by the highest of earnings growth, inflation or 2.5%. He has promised to retain it unchanged until April 2030.

After that, the pension would continue rising by at least inflation or 2.5%, with a mechanism intended to preserve its value relative to average earnings over the longer term. The Government estimates that the change could reduce State Pension spending by around £15 billion a year by the end of the 2030s, with savings contributing to the National Care Service.

The IFS says the revised Triple Lock will not save enough to fund universal social care during the next Parliament. Its reasoning is that cancelling an increase in pension spending which was itself unfunded does not release money already available elsewhere in the Budget. On that basis, tax rises or cuts to other public spending would still be required to finance the new commitment. The OBR’s analysis of a similar change found no significant savings before 2034.

The Health Foundation estimates that a Scotland-style system of free personal and nursing care for over-65s in England would cost about £6.5 billion extra in 2026/27, rising to around £7.5 billion by 2035/36.

Burnham says the service will be “fully funded, and not through borrowing”, but a complete final costing and funding settlement has not yet been published. Baroness Louise Casey’s commission is due to report in summer 2027 and recommend how the service should be built up.

The £2.66 billion hospital problem

Age UK’s July 2026 report The Longest Wait to Leave found that around 13,000 people on a typical day were medically fit to leave hospital but remained there, while 57% of people who could have been discharged on a typical day were still in hospital.

During 2025/26 at least 6.75 million hospital bed-days were lost to discharge delays of seven days or more. More than one in seven hospital bed-days were occupied by people medically fit to leave, and Age UK estimates delayed discharge cost the NHS £2.66 billion in 2025/26.

Burnham’s warning that broken social care can also break the NHS therefore has a substantial factual basis. But making eligible care free does not by itself create carers, care-home beds, community nursing teams or discharge packages. The Government itself says the service will expand as workforce and provider capacity are built up.

The workforce has changed — but the challenge remains

Skills for Care’s 2025/26 figures show the vacancy rate falling to 6.2%, its lowest level in a decade, while filled posts rose by 22,000 to around 1.59 million.

At the same time, posts filled by British nationals fell by another 40,000 in one year and by 130,000 since 2020/21. Direct international recruitment fell to around 30,000 in 2025/26, its lowest level in four years, while Skills for Care estimates another 410,000 posts will be needed by 2040.

The median independent-sector care-worker wage was £12.60 an hour in December 2025, up from £12.00 in March, while the National Living Wage rose to £12.71 in April 2026. Workers with five or more years’ experience earned only around 10p an hour more than new starters. Burnham’s promise of “proper recognition” for care workers can eventually be judged against those numbers.

“Care that is about people, not profit”

This phrase takes us directly back to Article 1. IFS analysis says around 95% of care-home beds are provided by the independent sector — private and voluntary providers.

Government provider-fee data for 2025/26, still provisional, shows English councils paying external providers an average of about £956 a week for residential care without nursing for over-65s and £1,089 with nursing, excluding NHS-funded nursing care. Published self-funder figures are higher on average, although the datasets cover different populations and should not be used to manufacture a precise like-for-like gap.

Net council spending on adult and children’s social care reached £42.8 billion in 2025/26, representing 71.9% of core spending; adult social care alone accounted for 44.5%. ADASS, reported by Community Care, also found that councils overspent their 2025/26 adult-social-care budgets by £715 million.

THE OWNERSHIP QUESTION

Burnham has explained how he wants to change who pays for personal care. He has said much less about changing who owns and provides the system receiving that public money.

That does not prove the eventual National Care Service will leave ownership untouched: Casey’s work is unfinished. It means the answer is not there yet.

What has actually been promised?

Burnham proposes a National Care Service providing free personal care for older people in England, introduced in phases during the next Parliament and based on assessed need rather than ability to pay. It will not cover care-home bed and board; accommodation costs will remain means-tested. Existing property protections and Deferred Payment Agreements will remain.

The current Triple Lock remains until April 2030 and is then replaced with a different uprating mechanism, with projected savings contributing towards the new service. The Government has not yet published a complete final funding settlement or detailed implementation model. The programme is politically conditional: it is intended for the next Parliament and therefore depends on Labour securing the mandate to implement it at the next general election, which must be held by 2029.

Political context

Reform UK has opposed Burnham’s proposed change to the Triple Lock and says it would retain the existing Triple Lock, pointing instead to welfare savings. Reform does not currently propose the same free-personal-care entitlement.

The Liberal Democrats have a published free-personal-care policy for England. Their 2024 manifesto proposed provision based on need rather than ability to pay and allocated an additional £2.7 billion by 2028/29 — the party’s own costing — funded as part of a wider package including reversing previous reductions in bank taxation.

This article is not a party-policy league table. Those positions provide context; the subject here is what Burnham’s proposal does and does not contain.

Three months later, the same questions remain

When Article 4 ended, our argument was not simply that Britain needed to spend more money on care. Hospitals were paying because patients could not leave, councils were paying, families and self-funders were paying, and care workers were carrying part of the cost through persistently low wages.

Burnham has put fundamental social-care reform back at the centre of national politics. His diagnosis that social care and the NHS are inseparable is supported by the evidence, and free personal care would materially change who bears some of today’s costs.

But the questions raised by our original investigation have become the test against which the National Care Service should be examined: can Britain recruit and retain enough carers, create enough capacity to get medically fit people out of hospital, support councils already carrying enormous costs, explain what happens to residential accommodation bills and pension income, and show where the remaining funding comes from?

And if the Prime Minister genuinely wants “care that is about people, not profit”, one final question remains unavoidable: WHO WILL OWN THE SYSTEM WE ARE ABOUT TO PAY FOR?
