FREE PERSONAL CARE — NOT A FREE CARE HOME

Andy Burnham has promised a National Care Service “free at the point of use”. But what exactly becomes free, what will pensioners still pay, and does it solve the care crisis we investigated three months ago?

By Steve English • 30 September 2026

Three months ago, The British Lions Roar published a four-part investigation into Britain’s care-home and social-care system. We followed the money, looked at ownership and private provision, examined the workforce, overseas recruitment and pay, and explored the extraordinary cost to the NHS when patients cannot leave hospital because the care they need is not available.

In Article 4 we asked the question governments have avoided for decades: if taxpayers are already spending billions supporting this system, is there a better way to build it?

Now Prime Minister Andy Burnham has given his answer. At Labour’s 2026 conference he promised a National Care Service, introduced during the next Parliament, providing free personal care for older people in England according to need rather than ability to pay.

He described it as “free at the point of use”, a service that “starts in the home”, “care that is about people not profit”, and promised “no care charges paid out of your basic state pension”.

Those are substantial promises. But they do not mean that residential care-home bills disappear.

What “free personal care” actually means

The Government defines the proposed free personal care as help with everyday needs such as eating, bathing and using the toilet. It would be based on assessed need rather than the person’s ability to pay.

For somebody remaining in their own home, that can mean the personal assistance required to allow them to continue living there. In residential care, however, the bill contains both the cost of providing personal care and the accommodation and living costs of staying in the home.

Burnham is proposing to make eligible personal care free. He is not proposing to make the entire residential placement free.

“The service will not cover bed and board.” — GOV.UK, 29 September 2026

The Government says existing means-tested council contributions towards those costs will remain. That does not make free personal care insignificant: for people currently paying for eligible care, removing that charge could make a substantial difference. But nobody should hear “National Care Service”, “free at the point of use” or comparisons with the NHS and assume that care-home bills are about to disappear.

Your house has not disappeared from the equation

Burnham told conference that the National Care Service would protect people’s homes and savings. Under England’s 2026/27 charging rules, however, the upper capital limit remains £23,250 and the lower limit £14,250. Above the upper limit, a person requiring permanent residential care will generally be expected to meet the full cost, subject to the detailed charging rules and disregards.

The new proposal does not abolish means testing for care-home accommodation, nor does it abolish the relevance of property. The Government says existing protection will continue where a partner or dependent relative remains in the home. Deferred Payment Agreements will also remain available for eligible people, allowing them to avoid selling their home during their lifetime to meet care costs.

That protection matters, but a deferred bill is still a bill. On the information currently published, the proposal does not guarantee that somebody’s property or estate can never contribute towards residential accommodation costs.

The state-pension question

Burnham also promised “no care charges paid out of your basic state pension”, saying older people with nothing more than the State Pension, or only a little more, should no longer face those charges.

For somebody receiving care at home, the existing rules help explain the promise. In 2026/27, a single person over Pension Credit age receiving council-arranged care outside a care home must ordinarily be left with a Minimum Income Guarantee of £241.45 a week, before any additional applicable allowances. The full new State Pension is around that level.

Residential care is different. A council-supported care-home resident must currently be left with a Personal Expenses Allowance of £31.80 a week. On a full new State Pension of £241.30, that can leave roughly £209.50 a week available to contribute towards the resident’s assessed care-home costs.

The unanswered question

Under the proposed National Care Service, will a council-supported care-home resident whose only income is the State Pension continue to contribute all but the Personal Expenses Allowance towards accommodation costs? If not, what will they contribute?

The Government has said personal-care charges will end but bed and board will remain means-tested. It has not yet answered that specific question.

Scotland shows why “free” needs explaining

Burnham’s proposal is frequently compared with Scotland, where free personal care has existed for years. The comparison is useful because it shows the difference between free personal care and a free care-home place.

From April 2026, Scotland’s fixed contribution towards eligible personal care for a self-funding care-home resident is £260.30 a week, with a further £117.10 where nursing care is required. By comparison, published 2026 figures put average self-funder residential care in Scotland at around £1,539 a week.

England has not yet published the detailed mechanism it would use, so those Scottish figures should not be treated as a forecast of English payments. They demonstrate the principle: free personal care can coexist with a substantial residential-care bill.

Who pays for the National Care Service?

Burnham’s answer begins with the State Pension Triple Lock. The existing system guarantees annual increases by the highest of earnings growth, inflation or 2.5%. He has promised to retain it unchanged until April 2030.

After that, the pension would continue rising by at least inflation or 2.5%, with a mechanism intended to preserve its value relative to average earnings over the longer term. The Government estimates that the change could reduce State Pension spending by around £15 billion a year by the end of the 2030s, with the savings used to build the National Care Service.

But those are long-term savings. The IFS says the revised Triple Lock will not save enough to fund universal social care during the next Parliament. Its underlying point is important: cancelling an increase in pension spending which was itself unfunded does not suddenly create money elsewhere in the Budget. On that analysis, tax rises or cuts to other public spending would still be required to finance the new social-care commitment.

The OBR’s analysis of a similar Triple Lock change found no significant savings before 2034, reinforcing the reason the proposed National Care Service is being presented as a phased programme rather than an immediate nationwide switch.

On the care side, the Health Foundation estimates that a Scotland-style system of free personal and nursing care for over-65s in England would cost about £6.5 billion extra in 2026/27, rising to around £7.5 billion by 2035/36.

Burnham says the service will be “fully funded, and not through borrowing”. But a complete final costing and funding settlement has not yet been published. Baroness Louise Casey’s commission is due to report in summer 2027 and recommend how the service should be built up.

The £2.66 billion hospital problem

There is a powerful argument for fixing social care which has little to do with party politics: the NHS is already paying heavily when the care system cannot take people out of hospital safely.

Age UK’s July 2026 report The Longest Wait to Leave found that around 13,000 people on a typical day were medically fit to leave hospital but remained there. More than half — 57% — of people who could have been discharged on a typical day were still in hospital.

During 2025/26, at least 6.75 million hospital bed-days were lost to discharge delays of seven days or more. More than one in seven hospital bed-days were occupied by people medically fit to leave. Age UK estimates the cost of delayed discharge to the NHS at £2.66 billion in 2025/26.

So Burnham’s warning that broken social care can also break the NHS has a substantial factual basis. The question is whether the proposed solution creates enough actual capacity.

Making eligible care free does not by itself create a carer, a care-home bed, a community nursing team or a suitable discharge package. Removing financial barriers may help people access care, and closer integration may make discharge more efficient, but somebody can leave hospital only when the support they need actually exists.

The Government acknowledges this directly: it says the National Care Service will expand as workforce and provider capacity are built up.

The workforce has changed — but the challenge remains

Skills for Care’s 2025/26 figures contain both good news and a warning. The adult-social-care vacancy rate fell to 6.2%, its lowest level in a decade, while filled posts rose by 22,000 to around 1.59 million.

At the same time, posts filled by British nationals fell by another 40,000 in one year and by 130,000 since 2020/21. Direct international recruitment fell to around 30,000 in 2025/26, its lowest level in four years, while the number of non-British, non-EU workers has risen sharply. Skills for Care estimates the sector will require another 410,000 posts by 2040.

Pay remains another pressure point. The median independent-sector care-worker wage was £12.60 an hour in December 2025, up from £12.00 in March. Yet the National Living Wage rose to £12.71 in April 2026. Skills for Care also found that workers with five or more years’ experience earned only around 10p an hour more than new starters.

Burnham has promised “proper recognition” for care workers. Those figures provide a clear benchmark against which the eventual workforce plan can be judged.

“Care that is about people, not profit”

This phrase takes us directly back to the first article in this series. Around 95% of care-home beds are provided by the independent sector — private and voluntary providers — according to IFS analysis.

Government provider-fee data for 2025/26, still provisional, shows English councils paying external providers an average of about £956 a week for residential care without nursing for over-65s and £1,089 with nursing, excluding NHS-funded nursing care. Published self-funder figures are higher on average, although the datasets cover different populations and should not be used to manufacture a precise like-for-like gap.

Councils themselves are under severe pressure. Net spending on adult and children’s social care reached £42.8 billion in 2025/26, representing 71.9% of core council spending; adult social care alone accounted for 44.5%. ADASS also reported that councils overspent their 2025/26 adult-social-care budgets by £715 million.

The ownership question

Burnham has explained how he wants to change who pays for personal care. He has said much less about changing who owns and provides the system receiving that public money.

That does not prove the eventual National Care Service will leave ownership untouched: Casey’s work is unfinished. It means the answer is not there yet. If the ambition is genuinely “care that is about people, not profit”, ownership and provision cannot remain outside the discussion.

What has actually been promised?

Strip the conference language back to the policy underneath it and the position is clearer. Burnham proposes a National Care Service providing free personal care for older people in England, introduced in phases during the next Parliament and based on assessed need rather than ability to pay.

It will not cover care-home bed and board. Those accommodation costs will remain means-tested. Existing property protections and Deferred Payment Agreements will remain. The current Triple Lock remains until April 2030 and is then replaced with a different uprating mechanism, with projected savings contributing towards the new service.

The Government has not yet published a complete final funding settlement or detailed implementation model. The programme is also politically conditional: it is intended for the next Parliament and therefore depends on Labour securing the mandate to implement it at the next general election, which must be held by 2029.

Political context — without losing sight of the proposal

This article is about Burnham’s plan, not a party-policy league table. But the reactions help place the proposal in context.

Reform UK has opposed Burnham’s proposed change to the Triple Lock and says it would retain the existing Triple Lock, pointing instead to welfare savings. Reform does not currently propose the same free-personal-care entitlement.

The Liberal Democrats do have a published free-personal-care policy for England. Their 2024 manifesto proposed provision based on need rather than ability to pay and allocated an additional £2.7 billion by 2028/29 — the party’s own costing — funded as part of a wider package including reversing previous reductions in bank taxation. That figure should not be confused with the Health Foundation’s independent estimate of the cost of a Scotland-style scheme.

The existence of alternatives does not answer the central questions about Burnham’s plan. It simply shows that the argument over who pays for personal care, and how, is now firmly back in national politics.

Three months later, the same questions remain

When Article 4 ended, our argument was not simply that Britain needed to spend more money on care. The evidence already showed that enormous sums were being spent because the system was failing elsewhere: hospitals were paying because patients could not leave, councils were paying, families and self-funders were paying, and care workers were carrying part of the cost through persistently low wages.

Burnham has put fundamental social-care reform back at the centre of national politics. His diagnosis that social care and the NHS are inseparable is supported by the evidence, and free personal care would materially change who bears some of today’s costs.

But the questions raised by our original investigation have not disappeared. They have become the test against which the National Care Service should be examined: can Britain recruit and retain enough carers, create enough capacity to get medically fit people out of hospital, support councils already carrying enormous costs, explain what happens to residential accommodation bills and pension income, and show where the remaining funding comes from?

And if the Prime Minister genuinely wants “care that is about people, not profit”, one final question remains unavoidable:

WHO WILL OWN THE SYSTEM WE ARE ABOUT TO PAY FOR?

That is where the next stage of Britain’s social-care debate begins.
