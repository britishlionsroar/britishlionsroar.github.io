How They Sold Our Elderly

The Conservatives started it. Labour continued it. And now Britain's most vulnerable people are being cared for by debt-laden corporations registered in offshore tax havens — while the bill lands on the taxpayer.

  • A single Act of Parliament in 1990 stripped councils of their duty to provide care and handed it to the private market
  • Private equity firms now own one in eight care home beds in England and extract profit through debt, rent and offshore structures
  • Over £256 million was extracted in profit from just three English regions in three years, while frontline workers were paid below the living wage
  • 98% of care homes closed by regulators between 2011 and 2023 were run for profit
  • Every government since Thatcher has allowed this to continue — and none has reversed it

Britain is an ageing country. Nearly 441,000 people currently live in residential care homes. The vast majority are elderly. Many have dementia. They cannot advocate for themselves. They cannot walk out. They cannot switch provider.

They are, in the language of financial markets, a captive customer base. And for three decades, governments of every stripe have allowed that captive customer base to be monetised, leveraged, and exploited by some of the most aggressive capital structures in the world.

This is not an accident. It is policy. And it started with one piece of legislation.

“The Griffiths proposals aimed to subject every aspect of community care services to competitive tenders. They would confine social services departments to the role of purchaser of continuing care. 80% of government money flowing to social services had to be spent in the independent sector.”
— Analysis of the NHS and Community Care Act 1990

The National Health Service and Community Care Act 1990 received Royal Assent on 29 June 1990. Its title made it sound bureaucratic. Its consequences were anything but. The Act legally forced local councils to stop being providers of care and become purchasers of care instead. From that point forward, councils could not run care homes. They could only buy spaces in homes run by someone else.

That someone else, by design, was the private sector.

The legislation required that 80% of government money flowing to social services be spent in what was called the “independent sector” — meaning private or voluntary organisations. The government dressed this up as efficiency and choice. What it actually created was a permanent, legally mandated transfer of public responsibility into private hands.

The immediate consequence was predictable. Council-run care homes, which had operated on a cost-recovery basis with no profit motive, were closed or sold off. In their place came a new market of private providers, competing for council contracts on the basis of price. The lowest bidder won. Quality was secondary.

This was not incompetence. It was the Thatcher government's explicit ideology applied to the care of the elderly.

What followed over the next three decades was the systematic financialisation of an industry built on human vulnerability. The private care market did not stay in the hands of small local businesses for long. As councils became increasingly cash-strapped and their funding per resident failed to keep pace with actual costs, the only operators who could survive were those with access to large-scale capital. That meant private equity.

Private equity firms do not buy businesses to run them. They buy businesses to extract value from them. The model is well understood in the financial world and almost entirely invisible to the general public. A private equity fund borrows a large sum of money, uses that money to buy a care home chain, and then transfers the debt onto the care homes themselves. The homes then spend years repaying that debt out of their operating income, while the fund collects management fees and, eventually, sells the chain on to another buyer at a profit.

The Numbers: Private Equity in UK Care
  • The 5 largest private equity-owned or backed care home providers have borrowed £35,072 per care home bed they own
  • This creates an interest cost of £102 per bed per week — 16% of the average weekly fee for residential care
  • By contrast, the 8 largest not-for-profit providers have interest costs of just £19 per bed per week
  • In 2019, around £1.5 billion was identified as “leaking” out of the care home industry annually in profits, debt repayments, management fees, and rental payments
  • Between 2011 and 2023, 98% of the 816 adult care homes closed by the Care Quality Commission were run for profit
Source: Centre for Health and the Public Interest (CHPI) — chpi.org.uk

The money that should pay for food, staffing, training, and the daily dignity of elderly residents is instead servicing the debt that private equity used to buy the building in the first place. This is not a market distortion. It is the market functioning exactly as designed.

And it gets worse. Investigators at the Reclaiming Our Regional Economies programme examined just three English regions — the North East, South Yorkshire, and the West Midlands — and found that private care providers extracted over £256 million in profit between 2021 and 2024. More than a third of those profits went to companies owned by private equity firms or with parent companies registered in offshore tax havens. Directors of those same companies were earning up to 60 times the average wage. Frontline care workers were frequently paid below the living wage.

That is the system that three decades of Conservative and Labour government built and maintained.

“Politicians and pundits tell us there simply isn't the money to invest in social care. The truth is that money exists in the care system but too much of it is leaking out.”
— Reclaiming Our Regional Economies report, November 2025

Labour's culpability in this is worth spelling out, because the party has spent years presenting itself as the natural guardian of public services.

When Labour came to power in 1997, the privatised care market created by the 1990 Act was already bedding in. Labour did not reverse it. Under Blair and Brown, the purchaser-provider split was not dismantled — it was extended to the NHS itself through further market reforms. The logic of outsourcing and contracting became the default setting for every public service Labour touched. By the time Labour left office in 2010, the private care sector was entrenched and expanding.

The Conservatives then continued the process under the Health and Social Care Act 2012, which opened up what remained of NHS commissioning to external competition. By this point, the distinction between a public health system and a private care market had been deliberately blurred by governments of both colours.

Labour returned to power in 2024 with warm words about a National Care Service and a Fair Pay Agreement. Before the election, Wes Streeting talked about stripping private equity-run care homes of public contracts if they failed to meet quality and value-for-money standards. None of that has materialised. The private equity operators are still there. The offshore structures are still there. The profit extraction is still there.

The elderly are still in beds that cost more to finance than to staff.

The mechanism that makes all of this possible is one that most people never think about, because it operates quietly at the intersection of council budgets and corporate balance sheets.

Local authorities, facing chronic central government underfunding, set a maximum weekly fee they will pay for a state-funded care home resident. That cap — in many areas around £800 per week — is lower than the true cost of providing quality care. Care home operators bridge the gap by charging self-funding residents substantially more. Those are the people who have savings above £23,250 or who own their own home: by law, they must pay their own way.

This cross-subsidy is not a minor accounting adjustment. It is structural. It means that wealthier elderly people are effectively subsidising the shortfall created by inadequate council funding. And it means that the private operators who receive both council fees and self-funder premiums have every financial incentive to keep their council contract costs — principally wages — as low as legally possible.

The Council Price Trap
  • Councils set a fixed cap on weekly fees for state-funded residents (typically around £800 per week in many areas)
  • This fee is routinely below the true cost of care, forcing providers to cross-subsidise via higher charges to self-funders
  • Because wages are the largest single operating cost, and income from councils cannot be raised, providers cut wages to protect margins
  • Care worker median pay in the independent sector was £12.00 per hour in March 2025 — below retail comparators and barely above the minimum wage floor
  • Between March and December 2025, care worker pay increases were 1.7 percentage points below the rise in the National Living Wage — the widest gap since 2019/20
Source: Skills for Care Annual Workforce Report 2025; The King's Fund Social Care 360

The result is a workforce crisis that has been building for years. British care workers — trained, experienced, committed people — look at their pay packet and then look at the wages being offered by Amazon, Aldi, or the local supermarket. They do the maths. They leave.

Since 2021, the number of adult social care posts filled by people of British nationality has fallen by 130,000. Those are not people who became unemployed. They are people who made a rational economic decision to stop doing one of the most physically and emotionally demanding jobs in the country for wages that cannot cover a decent life.

In their place, the care industry imported workers. Lots of them. From Nigeria, India, Zimbabwe, Ghana, the Philippines. On Health and Care Worker visas that tied their right to remain in the country to their employer. Workers who could not easily walk out. Workers who, because of their immigration status, were far more tolerant of poor conditions than any domestic employee would be.

By 2024, migrant workers filled 32% of all care worker roles and 29% of senior care worker roles. The industry had replaced a domestic workforce problem with an immigration dependency — and handed the long-term costs of that dependency to the British taxpayer.

That story — the full fiscal catastrophe of what the visa system has cost and continues to cost — is the subject of Article 2 in this series.

“Britons won't die out, but they'll become a minority in their own country, with no control over its future.”

What is happening in social care is a microcosm of a wider pattern. An industry that should be a public service has been turned into a financial product. The people it exists to serve — elderly British men and women who paid taxes and National Insurance their entire working lives — are an afterthought in a balance sheet managed in Luxembourg or the Cayman Islands.

Three governments have watched this happen and done nothing of substance to reverse it. The Conservatives built the architecture. Labour validated it. The current Labour government is tinkering at the edges while the structure rots.

The question of who could actually fix it — and how — is not complicated. The solution is not to negotiate better terms with the corporations currently extracting value from our elderly. It is to remove those corporations from the equation entirely.

That solution, and the remarkable economic case for it, is laid out in Articles 3 and 4 of this series.

Reform UK — The Policy Position

Reform UK is the only party in British politics currently proposing changes at sufficient scale to address the structural failure of the social care market. Their immigration reduction policies would, by removing the visa pipeline that allows care providers to bypass domestic wage pressures, force the sector to compete for British workers at competitive wages. The economic and policy implications of that position are explored fully in Articles 3 and 4 of this series.

Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse the current model of privatised, import-dependent social care.
