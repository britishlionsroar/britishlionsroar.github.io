The Data Blackout — The Government That Stopped Counting

Two parliamentary committees reported in the same week of June 2026. Both reached the same conclusion: the government does not know who is in this country. Exit checks data discontinued. No shared evidence base between departments. 45,272 asylum claimants vanished from the system without trace. These are not sophisticated failures. These are the basic things a functioning government should already have been doing.

What This Article Establishes

  • The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee, reporting 23 June 2026, found the government's migration data "woefully inadequate" and confirmed that exit checks data — which would reveal how many people have overstayed — has been discontinued.
  • The Public Accounts Committee, reporting 5 June 2026, found no single shared evidence base across the Home Office, Ministry of Justice, Department for Work and Pensions, and local councils.
  • Between 2019 and Q1 2025, 45,272 asylum cases were closed because claimants had withdrawn their application or disappeared from the process entirely with no return recorded.
  • The Lords committee called for a triennial migration plan — a basic instrument of governance that does not currently exist.
  • Two committees. Same week. Same conclusion. The government has been running an immigration system without the data infrastructure needed to manage it, and without the will to build one.

The bureaucratic language of parliamentary committees can obscure what their findings actually mean in plain English. This article translates that language. Every finding cited is drawn verbatim from the source documents linked below.

Two Committees. One Week. One Verdict.

In the first three weeks of June 2026, two separate parliamentary committees published reports that, taken together, amount to a comprehensive indictment of the British government's capacity to manage its own immigration system.

The Public Accounts Committee published its report on 5 June. The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee published on 23 June. The two committees examined different aspects of the same system — the PAC focused on the asylum pipeline and its costs; the Lords committee examined settlement, citizenship, and integration. They used different evidence, called different witnesses, and worked independently of each other.

They reached the same conclusion.

Public Accounts Committee 5 June 2026

No single shared evidence base across government departments dealing with asylum and immigration.

Control of the asylum system "all but lost" at the time of inquiry.

100,600 active asylum claimants. 80,333 appeals pending. £4.9bn annual cost.

Home Office cannot account for the whereabouts of 53,298 absconders.

House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee 23 June 2026

Migration data "woefully inadequate."

Government does not know the number of people who have overstayed leave to remain.

Exit checks data discontinued — Home Office must restart publishing urgently.

Inter-departmental data sharing between Home Office, MoJ, DWP and councils inadequate.

The convergence of two independent parliamentary inquiries on the same fundamental finding — the government does not know who is in this country — is not coincidental. It reflects a system that has been operationally overwhelmed for years and which has responded to that overwhelm not by building the data infrastructure needed to manage it, but by progressively reducing the visibility of its own failure.

Exit Checks: The Data the Government Stopped Publishing

Exit checks are the mechanism by which the United Kingdom records the departure of individuals from its territory through controlled ports. When a person leaves via an airport, a ferry terminal, or the Channel Tunnel, their departure is logged. The aggregate of those departure records, set against arrival records, produces the overstayer figure — the count of people who entered legally and did not leave when their permission to remain expired.

The government stopped publishing this data. The Lords committee confirmed this finding on 23 June 2026 and called for its "urgent" reinstatement. The committee stated plainly that without this data, the government cannot tell Parliament or the public how many people are currently overstaying their leave to remain in the United Kingdom.

"The Home Office must restart publishing exit checks data urgently." — House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee, Settlement, Citizenship and Integration, 23 June 2026

The consequences of this data gap are direct and practical. Without exit checks publication, there is no publicly verifiable overstayer count. Without an overstayer count, the true scale of illegal residence in the United Kingdom cannot be established from official sources. Estimates exist — researchers and think tanks have produced figures — but they are estimates, not confirmed counts, and the government's position is that it cannot verify them because it does not have the underlying data.

That position is itself the indictment. A government that cannot tell its citizens how many people are illegally present in the country — because it has discontinued the data collection that would produce that figure — is a government that has chosen not to know. The decision to stop publishing exit checks data was not forced upon the Home Office by circumstance. It was a decision. Decisions can be reversed. The Lords committee said it must be.

No Shared Evidence Base: The Departmental Silo Problem

The PAC's finding that no single shared evidence base exists across government departments handling asylum and immigration is, in the language of public administration, a severe indictment. In plain English, it means this: the Home Office, the Ministry of Justice, the Department for Work and Pensions, and local councils each hold data about the same population of people, and they do not systematically share it with each other.

The practical consequences are considerable.

  • ✗ The Home Office does not know what the MoJ knows. A person who has been convicted of a criminal offence, served a sentence, and been released may be in the MoJ's records as a convicted foreign national. That information is not automatically transmitted to the Home Office enforcement system in a way that triggers deportation proceedings in every applicable case.
  • ✗ The Home Office does not know what DWP knows. A person in receipt of benefits from the Department for Work and Pensions has a registered address and an active claim file. If that person is simultaneously an absconder from immigration conditions, the DWP data is not cross-referenced against the immigration absconder register in any systematic way that would produce a combined address.
  • ✗ The Home Office does not know what local councils know. Local authorities hold housing records, school registration data, and social services records. A family that has absconded from immigration reporting requirements may be registered with a local council for housing or children's services purposes. That information does not flow automatically to immigration enforcement.
  • ✗ There is no triennial migration plan. The Lords committee called for the establishment of a triennial migration plan — a basic planning instrument that would set out projections, targets, and departmental responsibilities across a rolling three-year period. No such plan exists. The government has been managing an immigration system of this scale without a formal cross-departmental plan for what it is trying to achieve or how it intends to achieve it.

Each of these gaps is individually significant. Together, they describe a system in which the left hand does not know what the right hand is doing — and in which the absence of joined-up data is not an oversight but a sustained institutional failure that no government in recent memory has made a serious attempt to correct.

The 45,272: Cases Closed Because the Claimants Disappeared

Between 2019 and Q1 2025, 45,272 asylum cases were closed on the grounds that the claimant had withdrawn their application or had ceased to engage with the process. No return was recorded for the overwhelming majority of these cases. The claimants did not leave the United Kingdom through any documented channel. They stopped engaging with the system and the system stopped looking for them.

45,272 Asylum cases closed — claimants withdrew or disappeared — 2019 to Q1 2025

An "implicit withdrawal" — the technical term for a case closed because the claimant ceased contact — is not a removal. It is not a grant of leave. It is a case file marked closed because the person it relates to can no longer be located. The system's response to losing track of a claimant is to close their file. The person remains in the country. The file does not.

The 45,272 implicit withdrawals and the 53,298 confirmed absconders are not the same population — there is overlap, but the categories are constructed differently and cover different time periods and eligibility criteria. What they share is the same underlying reality: people who entered the UK immigration system and then left it, not through removal or legal grant, but simply by stopping engagement, with no mechanism to locate them or account for their whereabouts.

The Lords committee's finding that the government does not know how many people are overstaying their leave to remain sits alongside these figures. The implicit withdrawal figure, the absconder figure, and the overstayer figure are three separate measurements of the same systemic failure: a state that has lost the ability to account for a significant and growing portion of the population present on its territory.

What "Woefully Inadequate" Means in Plain English

Parliamentary committees use measured language. They rarely reach for the strong word when a weaker one will do. "Woefully inadequate" is not measured language. It is the language of a committee that has examined the evidence, cross-examined the witnesses, and concluded that the failure is not marginal, not understandable given resource constraints, and not the kind of thing that can be addressed with minor operational adjustments.

The Lords committee used that phrase to describe the government's migration data. It did so in the context of a report that also found: no overstayer count, discontinued exit checks, inadequate inter-departmental data sharing, and the absence of a triennial migration plan.

"Woefully inadequate data on migration." — House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee, 23 June 2026. A cross-party committee of the upper chamber of the United Kingdom Parliament.

The PAC's language was, if anything, more direct. "All but lost" is the verdict of the Public Accounts Committee — not a journalist, not a think tank, not a political opponent — on the state of control of the asylum system at the time of their inquiry.

The significance of both findings appearing in the same week is that they cannot be dismissed as politically motivated. The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee is a cross-party body. The Public Accounts Committee is chaired by a member of the official opposition but operates on a cross-party basis and takes evidence from permanent civil servants, not ministers. Their findings reflect what the civil service told them under oath. They reflect the state of the system as the people running it described it.

The Triennial Plan That Doesn't Exist

The Lords committee called for a triennial migration plan — a formal, published, cross-departmental document setting out the government's migration projections and policy intentions across a rolling three-year period. The call implies the obvious: no such plan currently exists.

A triennial migration plan is not a sophisticated instrument. It is the kind of basic planning framework that any organisation managing a system of this scale and complexity would be expected to maintain. It would set out expected arrival numbers by category, planned processing capacity, removal targets, and the resource implications across departments. It would be updated every three years and would provide Parliament and the public with a basis for holding the government to account for its performance against stated intentions.

The government has been managing an immigration system that costs £4.9 billion per year, that has an active caseload of over 100,000 people, that generates a counter-terrorism caseload of tens of thousands of formally assessed subjects of interest, and that has 53,298 confirmed absconders on its books — without a triennial plan. Without a cross-departmental evidence base. Without published exit checks data. Without a reliable overstayer count.

These are not the failures of an overwhelmed department doing its best with limited resources. They are the failures of a system that has not been required to account for itself, and a series of governments that have not required it to do so.
