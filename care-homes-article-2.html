The Immigration Sticking Plaster

When private equity drove British workers out of the care sector, the government's solution was to import hundreds of thousands of replacements on tied visas. The rogue operators who exploited them are now gone. The bill for the rest falls on you.

This is the second in a four-part series on the destruction and possible rescue of Britain's adult social care sector. Article 1 traced how political decisions from 1990 onwards handed elderly care to private equity. This article examines what happened next: how the resulting workforce crisis was patched over with mass foreign recruitment, what that recruitment actually cost, and how a rogue employer scandal left tens of thousands of vulnerable workers stranded while the taxpayer picked up the consequences.
This article is written in plain English, following George Orwell's rule: if you can say something simply, do so. It combines investigative reporting with policy analysis and opinion. Satire is used where appropriate to test ideas by applying them consistently. Facts are sourced; opinions are labelled; institutions are not indulged.

By the early 2020s, the care home sector had a problem it could not ignore. Thirty years of privatisation and wage suppression had made frontline care work the most poorly paid demanding job in the country. British workers, as described in Article 1 of this series, had been voting with their feet for years. By 2021, with Brexit having removed the ready supply of EU labour that had previously plugged the gap, the vacancies were becoming impossible to hide.

The care industry went to government with a simple argument: we need workers, we cannot attract domestic workers at the wages our financial model supports, and you need to open the immigration tap. The government listened. In December 2021, care worker roles were added to the Shortage Occupation List, making them eligible for the Health and Care Worker visa at a reduced salary threshold. What followed was one of the largest and fastest visa surges the UK immigration system has ever processed.

Monthly applications rose from 4,100 in February 2022 to 18,300 by August 2023. In the single calendar year of 2023, 146,000 people arrived in the UK on Health and Care Worker visas, making it the most popular visa route under the entire Skilled Worker scheme. Combined with their dependants, a total of 348,000 visas for health and care workers and their families were issued in 2023 alone — described at the time as an historically high level. From the expansion of the route in 2022 to the end of 2024, a total of 648,100 people applied for Health and Care Worker visas, including the dependants of main applicants.

The top nationalities were India, Nigeria, and Zimbabwe. The workers came in good faith, following legal routes, to do jobs the system said needed doing. What many found when they arrived was something different.

“The Home Office moved quickly to expand the Skilled Worker route and did not consider the risks of non-compliance with visa rules.”
— House of Commons Public Accounts Committee report on skilled worker visas

The visa system created a structural incentive for exploitation that should have been obvious to anyone who thought about it for five minutes. A worker on a Health and Care visa cannot simply leave a bad employer and move on. Their right to remain in the United Kingdom is tied to the specific organisation that sponsored them. If that organisation behaves badly — underpays, overworks, charges illegal fees, confiscates passports, threatens dismissal as a control mechanism — the worker faces a stark choice: endure it, or lose their immigration status and have 60 days to either find a new sponsor or leave the country.

This is not a design flaw in the system. This is what the system does. It creates workers who are, by legal structure, far more tolerant of poor conditions than any domestic employee would be. For a care sector already built around minimum wage labour, it was an irresistible temptation for unscrupulous operators.

What Rogue Operators Did to Foreign Care Workers
  • Workers forced to pay illegal recruitment fees of thousands of pounds to secure their position
  • Sponsors applying for volumes of visas that exceeded their actual capacity to employ workers — meaning workers arrived to find no jobs existed
  • Excessive hours imposed under threat of visa cancellation
  • Passports confiscated by employers
  • Workers charged high accommodation fees while housed in unsuitable conditions
  • Pay withheld or workers paid below the legally agreed rate
  • Home Office compliance officers identified evidence of modern slavery on a daily basis but found it difficult to evidence for prosecution
  • The Home Office had just one sponsor compliance officer for every 1,600 licensed sponsors
Sources: Work Rights Centre; Gangmasters and Labour Abuse Authority; House of Commons Public Accounts Committee; Bureau of Investigative Journalism

The Home Office was slow to act. Between 2022 and 2023, the approval rate for Health and Care Worker visas ran at over 99%. The system was, in effect, a rubber stamp. It was only in 2023, as reports of exploitation became impossible to ignore, that the Home Office began strengthening its compliance activity. The approval rate fell to 81% by the end of 2024. Too late for tens of thousands of workers who had already arrived into circumstances they had not been warned about.

Between July 2022 and December 2024, 470 care agencies had their sponsor licences revoked for breaking the rules. By the government's own admission, this enforcement action left at least 39,000 migrant care workers without legal permission to work.

The government's response to 39,000 stranded workers was to announce a rematching scheme — a set of regional partnerships funded to help displaced workers find new sponsors. The scheme received £16 million in government funding in 2024-25, later reduced to £12.5 million.

Freedom of Information requests by the Work Rights Centre revealed the truth of what that money bought. Between May 2024 and May 2025, 28,621 emails were sent to displaced workers. Of those contacted, just 941 — 3.4% — were reported as having found alternative employment with a legitimate visa sponsor. One region, out of fifteen funded partnerships across England, matched precisely one single worker to a new role.

“Even if the rematching scheme boasted a 100% success rate rather than its current 3.4%, it would still fill less than a third of the estimated social care vacancies in England.”
— Open Democracy, April 2026

The workers who could not be rematched faced a clock. Sixty days to find a new sponsor, or leave the country. Many had families. Many had children in British schools. Some had been in the UK for years, doing exactly what they were sponsored to do, and found themselves suddenly stateless in all but name through no fault of their own.

Meanwhile, the Home Office — which had acknowledged it “could have done more to understand the way the care sector is structured and assess the potential risks” — identified just 80 potential victims of modern slavery on the Skilled Worker visa between 2022 and 2024. Eighty, against a backdrop of 39,000 displaced workers and a daily caseload of modern slavery evidence that its own caseworkers described.

This is what a system built on cheap imported labour, rather than properly paid domestic workers, looks like when it unravels.

The human cost of the rogue operator scandal is real and should be acknowledged. These were people who followed the legal rules, came in good faith, and were exploited by a system that had no adequate protections for them. That is a proper scandal and the government's failure to either prevent it or remedy it adequately is a genuine indictment.

But there is a second scandal running alongside it, less visible and considerably larger. It is the fiscal cost that the entire visa model — rogue operators aside — imposes on the British taxpayer over time. This is the part that the industry, the government, and the media have consistently refused to calculate honestly.

The House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee, drawing on Home Office and Migration Advisory Committee data, did the calculation. Their findings are stark.

The Lifetime Fiscal Cost: What the Lords Found
  • Individual worker: A foreign care worker represents an average lifetime net fiscal cost of £36,000 to the public purse. Because care wages are low, tax and National Insurance contributions are heavily outweighed by use of public services (NHS, schools, housing infrastructure)
  • With an adult dependant: The lifetime net fiscal cost of an adult dependant is £67,000. A worker who settles with a spouse or dependent adult costs the taxpayer a total of £103,000
  • The settlement pipeline: Between 2026 and 2030, an estimated 117,000 care workers are expected to receive settlement rights in the UK
  • Adult dependants in the same period: 79,000 adult dependants of care workers are expected to settle in the same period, at £67,000 each
  • Total projected liability: Multiplying 117,000 settling workers by the £103,000 household cost produces a direct long-term liability to the taxpayer of over £12 billion in avoided public service pressures
  • For context: The Office for Budget Responsibility calculates that the average UK resident born and raised here becomes a net fiscal cost only after the age of 80. A migrant care worker on low wages becomes a net fiscal cost from arrival
Source: House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee; Home Office; Migration Advisory Committee

These figures do not include children. Research from the Migration Observatory at the University of Oxford found that more than one in four foreign-born children arriving on visas did so as dependants of Health and Care workers. The Department for Education spends an average of £6,500 to £7,500 per year per pupil on state school places. Those costs are not calculated in the House of Lords headline figures. They are additional.

The care industry told government it could not afford to pay British workers properly. The government accepted that argument and opened a visa route that will cost the British public well over £12 billion in long-term infrastructure costs, plus annual education costs for the children who came with them, plus the cost of the rematching scheme for the 39,000 who were stranded, plus the cost of Home Office compliance operations that were so underfunded they had one officer for every 1,600 sponsors.

This is what “we can't afford it” actually cost.

There is a final dimension to the fiscal picture that compounds the damage. It is what economists call the remittance effect.

British workers spend their wages in Britain. They buy food in British supermarkets, pay rent to British landlords, drink in British pubs. For every pound paid to a low-income domestic worker, standard macroeconomic multipliers show that approximately £2.36 of economic activity is generated in their local community, producing VAT and business rate income for the public finances.

Migrant workers on low wages behave differently. A significant proportion of their income is sent home to support families in Nigeria, India, Zimbabwe, Ghana, or the Philippines. That money leaves the UK economy. It generates no domestic VAT. It supports no British high street. It benefits the economies of other countries while the British taxpayer funds the infrastructure those workers and their families use here.

The care industry, the government, and every commentator who presented mass foreign recruitment as a solution to the care workforce crisis omitted this from every cost-benefit calculation they ever published. They counted the workers. They did not count the leakage.

The full economic case for what a British workforce in a publicly owned care system would actually mean for the taxpayer — the tax yields, the welfare savings, the NHS bed-blocking clawback, the multiplier effect — is laid out in full in Article 3 of this series.

Reform UK — The Policy Position

The visa route that created this crisis has now been formally closed to new overseas applicants from July 2025. But the 39,000 displaced workers, the 117,000 expected to settle, and the £12 billion-plus in future public liabilities remain. Reform UK's position — that the immigration system has been used to suppress domestic wages and avoid the structural reforms the care sector needs — is borne out by the evidence in this article.

Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse the current model of privatised, import-dependent social care. The solution those policies point toward is the subject of Articles 3 and 4.
