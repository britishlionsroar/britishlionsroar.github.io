Tens of Thousands of Terrorism Suspects — Unmonitored, In Your Community, By Admission

Counter Terrorism Policing's own published factsheet confirms "tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest" living in Britain without active monitoring. Manchester Arena. Westminster Bridge. London Bridge. Every attacker was a closed subject of interest. This is not a leak. It is an admission.

What This Article Establishes

  • Counter Terrorism Policing's February 2025 factsheet — a public, official document — confirms "tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest" in the United Kingdom.
  • A Subject of Interest is not someone casually glanced at. It is a person who has been formally assessed as a terrorism risk and placed on the official register. "Closed" means deprioritised — not cleared, not innocent, not monitored.
  • At any one time, approximately 3,000 subjects of interest are under active investigation. The remainder — tens of thousands — receive no active monitoring whatsoever.
  • 24-hour surveillance of a single individual requires up to 20 officers. The 3,000 active cap is a hard resource ceiling, not a safety threshold.
  • The Manchester Arena, Westminster Bridge and London Bridge attackers were all closed subjects of interest at the time of their attacks. Official reviews confirmed the intelligence existed and was not acted upon.
  • The number of people in the system is a policy consequence. It is not an immutable fact of nature.

The information in this article comes from official publications, parliamentary records, and government-commissioned independent reviews. None of it has been disputed by the agencies involved. It has simply not been placed in front of the public in plain language.

What a Subject of Interest Actually Is

The phrase "subject of interest" is official Counter Terrorism Policing and MI5 terminology. It is not a casual label. It is not applied to someone who once attended a controversial meeting or expressed unpopular views online. It is a formal designation — a decision made by trained analysts, documented in official systems, reviewed by senior officers — that an individual represents a terrorism concern warranting formal assessment.

The process for becoming a Subject of Interest involves intelligence gathering, threat assessment, and formal recording within the counter-terrorism database. It requires a specific threshold of concern to be met and signed off. Subjects of Interest are not members of the general public who happen to be Muslim, or who have relatives with extremist sympathies, or who attended a mosque under investigation. They are individuals who have been independently assessed, by professional counter-terrorism analysts, as people about whom the state has specific terrorism-related concerns.

The distinction between "active" and "closed" in this context is critical and is consistently misunderstood in public discourse.

Active vs Closed: What the Terms Mean

Active Subject of Interest: an individual currently under formal investigation, subject to some level of monitoring, with an open case file being worked by an assigned team. Approximately 3,000 individuals fall into this category at any one time.

Closed Subject of Interest: an individual who was formally assessed as a terrorism concern, placed on the register, and subsequently deprioritised due to resource constraints. Their case file has been closed. They are not being monitored. They have not been assessed as no longer presenting a risk — they have been assessed as a lower priority than the cases currently receiving resources. There is a difference between "cleared" and "closed." A closed subject of interest has not been cleared.

This distinction is not a semantic quibble. It is the operational reality that produced three terrorist attacks in 2017 in which the perpetrators were known to counter-terrorism authorities, had been formally assessed, and had their cases closed before they acted.

The Official Admission: CTP's Own Words

Counter Terrorism Policing published its Mission Factsheet in February 2025. The document is publicly available on the Counter Terrorism Policing website. It is not a classified document. It is not a leak. It is the official self-description of the counter-terrorism apparatus, produced for public communication purposes.

The factsheet confirms the existence of "tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest." It uses that phrase. Those are the words of Counter Terrorism Policing, in a document they produced and published themselves.

"Tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest." — Counter Terrorism Policing, Our Mission Factsheet, February 2025. Public document, available at counterterrorism.police.uk.

The significance of this admission is considerable. Counter Terrorism Policing is not a body that reveals operational sensitivities casually. The decision to use this phrase in a public-facing document reflects a deliberate choice to be transparent about the scale of the problem — perhaps in the expectation that the figure would not be widely reported, would not be contextualised against the 3,000 active cap, and would not be read alongside the record of what closed subjects of interest have done.

~3,000 Active subjects of interest — under investigation now
40,000+ On wider terrorism radar since 2020 recheck
Tens of thousands Closed subjects of interest — assessed risks, unmonitored

The wider pool — those on the terrorism radar in a broader sense — was confirmed at over 40,000 following MI5's systematic recheck of its records in 2020. This figure was reported by The Times and corroborated by analysis in Small Wars Journal. The 40,000 are not all Subjects of Interest in the formal sense; they represent the population of individuals about whom some level of concern has been recorded at some point.

Within that wider population, the tens of thousands of closed Subjects of Interest represent the formally assessed tier — people who met the threshold for formal designation and whose cases were subsequently closed, not because the risk evaporated, but because the resources to maintain their active investigation did not exist.

The Resource Ceiling: Why Most Cases Are Closed

The 3,000 active cap is not a policy choice about the appropriate level of terrorism monitoring. It is a resource constraint presented as a policy choice. The arithmetic is stark.

Covert 24-hour surveillance of a single individual — the level of monitoring required to detect and prevent an imminent attack — requires between 15 and 20 trained officers when accounting for shift patterns, rest requirements, and the multiple surveillance disciplines involved. At 20 officers per subject, monitoring 3,000 active subjects requires 60,000 dedicated surveillance personnel. Counter Terrorism Policing employs approximately 6,500 officers across all functions nationally.

The resource mathematics mean that intensive surveillance of even a fraction of the active population is operationally impossible. For the tens of thousands of closed subjects, no surveillance takes place at all. Their cases are closed. They move through British communities, use British infrastructure, and — in the cases that have proceeded to attack — plan and execute acts of mass murder without any counter-terrorism officer being aware of their preparations.

15–20 Officers required for 24-hour surveillance of a single individual

Official reviews into the 2017 attacks acknowledged this constraint explicitly. The Brickcourt independent review — commissioned following the Manchester Arena, Westminster Bridge and London Bridge attacks — described the triage problem as "intractable" within existing resource and population parameters. The review noted that the intelligence had existed in each case. The problem was not intelligence failure. It was prioritisation failure driven by the gap between the number of people who required monitoring and the capacity available to monitor them.

The counter-argument — which this article advances — is that framing this as an "intractable problem" accepts the size of the monitored population as a given. It is not a given. It is the product of decades of immigration policy that has imported a population large enough to generate a counter-terrorism caseload that exceeds the system's monitoring capacity. The caseload is not a natural phenomenon. It is a policy consequence.

The Human Cost: Three Attacks, Three Closed Cases

The following three attacks are presented as a matter of factual record. The information is drawn from official independent reviews commissioned by the government and published in full. Nothing below is contested.

Manchester Arena — 22 May 2017 — 22 Killed, 1,017 Injured

Salman Abedi detonated a device in the foyer of the Manchester Arena following an Ariana Grande concert. The victims included children as young as eight years old.

Abedi was a closed Subject of Interest at the time of the attack. The Brickcourt independent review found that intelligence relating to Abedi had been received but "not fully appreciated." His case had been assessed and deprioritised. He was not under active monitoring in the period leading to the attack.

Westminster Bridge — 22 March 2017 — 6 Killed, 49 Injured

Khalid Masood drove a vehicle into pedestrians on Westminster Bridge before stabbing a police officer at the gates of Parliament.

Masood was a former Subject of Interest whose file had been closed. He had been assessed, formally recorded, and deprioritised. He was not under active monitoring at the time of the attack. The Brickcourt review confirmed his prior SOI status.

London Bridge — 3 June 2017 — 8 Killed, 48 Injured

Khuram Butt and two associates drove a vehicle into pedestrians on London Bridge before carrying out stabbings in Borough Market.

Butt was a closed Subject of Interest at the time of the attack. He had appeared in a Channel 4 documentary about British jihadists while simultaneously under counter-terrorism investigation — a fact that had been reported to authorities. His case was nonetheless closed. He was not under active monitoring when the attack was planned and executed.

Three attacks. Fifty-six people killed. Over a thousand injured. Three perpetrators who were known to counter-terrorism authorities, formally assessed as risks, and subsequently closed — not cleared — before they acted.

The official reviews described this as a systemic problem. They were correct. The system is structurally incapable of actively monitoring the population it has identified as presenting terrorism risks. That structural incapability is not a flaw in the counter-terrorism apparatus. It is the predictable consequence of operating a finite counter-terrorism resource against an expanding at-risk population.

The intelligence existed. The assessment existed. The case was closed because the resource to keep it open did not exist. The attack happened. — Summary of the pattern confirmed across all three 2017 attacks by the Brickcourt independent review and the MI5/CTP implementation stock-take.

The Political Argument the Data Produces

The counter-terrorism community's framing of this problem as "intractable" is understandable from an operational perspective. Counter-terrorism professionals work within the constraints they are given. They cannot control immigration policy. They cannot determine the size of the population they are asked to monitor. They can only prioritise within a triage system that will inevitably leave most known risks unmonitored.

The political argument produced by the data is different. If the counter-terrorism system cannot monitor the population of known risks it currently holds, and if the population of known risks is itself a function of who has entered and remained in the country, then the size of that population is a direct consequence of border and immigration policy decisions taken over decades.

This is not an argument that all immigrants are terrorism risks. The overwhelming majority of people who have come to the United Kingdom are not. The argument is narrower: that the absolute number of people requiring counter-terrorism assessment increases as the population grows, and that beyond a certain threshold the monitoring resource required to actively track all formally assessed risks exceeds what can plausibly be provided. Britain passed that threshold. The 2017 attacks were the consequence.

The tens of thousands of closed subjects of interest living in British communities today are not a temporary backlog to be cleared. They are the permanent operating condition of a counter-terrorism system that is structurally outpaced by the population it is asked to monitor. That condition will not improve under current immigration trajectories. It will worsen.
