Labour, the Working-Class Party? Not for Over 100 Years!

Angela Rayner told the TUC that “only Labour is the party of work.” Every Labour government since 1929 has left more people out of work than it found. The last one that didn't was in 1924.

By Steve English • Independent British writer and editor • November 2026
  • 1929–31: unemployment up 143%
  • 1945–51: up 121%
  • 1964–70: up 54%
  • 1974–79: up 107%
  • 1997–2010: up 25%
  • 2024–now: unemployment rate up from 4.2% to a peak of 5.2%, still 4.6% today
  • The OBR expects 76% of Labour's employer National Insurance hike to come out of workers' wages, from this year.
PURPOSE: To test Labour's claim to be “the party of work” against the only thing that counts: what happened to jobs every time Labour was in power, and who Labour governs for today.

On 14 September 2026, Housing Secretary Angela Rayner stood up at the TUC Congress in Brighton and gave the union delegates the line they came to hear: “Make no mistake. Only Labour is the party of work. The clue is in the name.”

It's a good line. Unions cheer it. Labour has been selling it for a century. There's just one problem with it: the record.

The record Labour never mentions

In 1991, the Department of Employment was asked in Parliament to back up a claim that every Labour government since 1929 had driven unemployment up. It answered with a table of official figures. Add the two Labour governments since, and this is what you get.

Five completed Labour governments. Every one of them left more people out of work than it found. Three of them more than doubled it.

So when was the last time a Labour government handed Britain fewer unemployed than it inherited? You have to go back to Ramsay MacDonald's first minority government, which lasted from January to November 1924. It lasted ten months, and unemployment fell on its watch. That was 102 years ago.

The party of work? Not for over 100 years.

This government: same story, new excuses

Labour took office in July 2024 with unemployment at 4.2%. By the end of 2025 it had hit 5.2%, with 1.88 million people out of work. It has since slipped back to 4.6%. That is still more than Labour inherited, and the OBR's own March forecast had it peaking at 5.3% this year.

That isn't bad luck. It's policy. In her first Budget, Rachel Reeves raised employer National Insurance from 13.8% to 15%. Labour called it a tax on business. The Office for Budget Responsibility, the government's own forecaster, told the truth about who pays. Firms would pass around 60% of the cost on to employees at first. Then, in the OBR's words, “76 per cent of the total cost is passed through lower real wages”. That's from this year, 2026–27.

Read that again. Labour put a tax on hiring people, and the government's own forecaster says three-quarters of it comes out of the pay packets of the people who were hired.

The OBR's March 2026 forecast describes the result. The labour market is loosening. The weakness is driven mainly by people entering the jobs market and struggling to find work because firms aren't hiring. Growth for 2026 was cut to 1.1%. That's what you get when you make every new job more expensive: fewer new jobs.

Rayner listed the new union rights, the new agencies and the new advisory boards. She didn't mention any of this.

So who is Labour the party of now?

If Labour isn't delivering for working people, it's fair to ask who it is delivering for. The answer is in the votes it is most afraid of losing.

At the 2024 general election, Labour won a landslide, and still lost five safe seats to pro-Gaza independents. Jonathan Ashworth, a shadow cabinet minister, lost Leicester South to Shockat Adam. Khalid Mahmood's 15,317 majority in Birmingham Perry Barr was wiped out by Ayoub Khan. Kate Hollern lost Blackburn to Adnan Hussain. Heather Iqbal lost Dewsbury and Batley to Iqbal Mohamed. Jeremy Corbyn kept Islington North as an independent. Muslims make up between roughly a third and almost half of voters in the four seats Labour lost to newcomers.

All five winners had been endorsed by The Muslim Vote, a campaign set up to organise Muslim voters behind chosen candidates. Before polling day, it issued Keir Starmer with a list of 18 demands.

That is a block vote. It was organised, it was published, and it worked. And Labour has spent the two years since trying to win it back.

In March 2026, the government adopted an official definition of “anti-Muslim hostility” and announced a tsar to champion it. In April 2026, it scrapped the two-child benefit limit.

Now look at who the two-child limit affected most. Census analysis carried out by the ONS shows that 55% of Muslim children live in families with three or more children. Across the whole population, the figure is 29%. In other words, a Muslim child is nearly twice as likely as the average child to be in a family hit by the cap.

That follows from the birth rate. Pew Research Center found that Muslim women in the UK and France average 2.9 children, a full child more than non-Muslim women. Across England and Wales as a whole, the fertility rate fell to 1.41 in 2024, the lowest on record for the third year running. The 2021 census puts the median age of Britain's Muslims at 27, against 40 for the population as a whole. A young community with bigger families gains the most from uncapped child benefits.

And it gains them on the back of the lowest employment rate of any religious group in the country. In the 2021 census, 51.4% of Muslims aged 16 to 64 were in work, against 70.9% of the population overall. Muslim unemployment was 6.7%, against 4.4% overall.

Put that together. A tax on hiring workers. A benefit rise aimed at the largest families. A new official definition protecting the community whose votes Labour lost. That isn't the party of work. It's the party of welfare and the block vote.

A century of the same result

Labour's name was chosen for working people. Its record has been written against them. Every Labour government since 1929 has ended with more people out of work than when it started. This one is on course to do the same, while taxing jobs, raising benefits and courting a block vote.

Judge Labour by outcomes, not by speeches to the TUC. The outcomes have been the same for 100 years.

There is a party with a plan to put work first, and its policies are published in full.

Cut employer National Insurance from 15% back to 13.8%, paid for by a new Employers' Migrant Labour Levy. It becomes cheaper to employ a British worker and more expensive to rely on low-wage imported labour.
