Cohesion Cash: Labour Pays Anti-Reform Media While Riot Recovery Money Funds Lessons in Beating Reform

The government has paid hundreds of thousands of pounds of taxpayers' money to left-wing, anti-Reform, anti-right-wing media, and handed millions to councils who then spent some of it on anti-Reform UK training.

By Steve English · 13 November 2026

  • £266,221 of public money has gone to Bylines Networks Ltd, a citizen-journalism network "allied with" Byline Times, whose titles run a steady line of attacks on Reform UK.
  • More than £630,000 of the first £6 million Local News Fund went to members of a single trade body.
  • Angela Rayner's £15 million Southport recovery fund handed millions to councils. At least £22,450 of it went to a left-wing organising school for training titled "Defeating Narratives of Division".
  • The training warned of working-class voters drifting to the "Tory-Right" and named Nigel Farage, Suella Braverman, Jacob Rees-Mogg and Boris Johnson.
  • The law bans councils from publishing material designed to affect support for a political party. The government says it can claw the money back. It has not said it will.
PURPOSE / MISSION: To follow taxpayers' money to where it actually ends up, and to ask why two government funds sold as "cohesion" keep landing with people whose idea of cohesion is stopping you voting Reform.

In the summer of 2024, after the Southport murders and the disorder that followed, the government set up a fund to help towns "rebuild". Two years later we know where some of that money went. It went on lessons in how to stop working-class voters drifting right.

Last week, a different department announced that one of the biggest winners from its new local news fund is a network of websites that spends a good deal of its time attacking Reform UK.

Two departments. Two pots of money. The same word on the label both times: cohesion.

A fund to heal Southport

The Community Recovery Fund was set up in September 2024 by the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, then run by Angela Rayner as Deputy Prime Minister. It was worth £15 million. Each eligible council got a flat £600,000, and up to £3 million more was set aside for Sefton, which covers Southport.

The government's own guidance made each council responsible for handing out the money locally and keeping an audit trail. The department spent none of it directly. That detail matters later, because it is the government's whole defence.

Where the money went

According to documents reported by the Telegraph, Birmingham City Council handed £13,450 to the Ella Baker School of Organising for a pilot called "Defeating Narratives of Division": an 11-part lecture series with its own manifesto. Blackpool Council paid £9,000 for a local version. Sessions were also run in Sheffield (including for council staff handling asylum and immigration), Walsall, Solihull and Sefton. The government has confirmed the Birmingham and Blackpool payments.

So what were council staff taught with this money? According to the material reported by the Telegraph:

  • Working-class communities were described as vulnerable to "far-Right, alt-Right and Tory-Right narratives of division and blame".
  • Nigel Farage was labelled an "authoritarian populist".
  • Suella Braverman, Jacob Rees-Mogg and Boris Johnson were named.
  • Tips included ways to steer the public away from concern about small boats, and role-play on calming discontent in small seaside towns.

A booking form for one of the school's sessions, in Glasgow, warned of "the impending disaster of a Reform UK government".

And who runs the school? According to the Telegraph, it is a network founded by former members of the left-wing group Hope Not Hate. The training material praises that group's "big focus on disrupting far-Right candidates at elections."

Hope Not Hate itself was reported to the Electoral Commission in June by Nigel Farage, over its campaigning in the Makerfield by-election. Hope Not Hate says its campaigning there was kept within the legal spending limits. Either way, this is the political tradition that councils hired, with riot recovery money, to teach their staff about "division".

That is not community cohesion. It is a political campaign with a council invoice attached.

What the law says

Section 2 of the Local Government Act 1986 says a council "shall not publish, or arrange for the publication of, any material which, in whole or in part, appears to be designed to affect public support for a political party."

When deciding whether material breaks that rule, the Act says regard must be had to "whether the material refers to a political party or to persons identified with a political party".

Training that names the leader of Reform UK, names senior Conservatives, and warns of the "disaster" of a Reform government refers to political parties and the people identified with them. Readers can judge for themselves how it gets past section 2.

"Nothing to do with us"

The government's answer came quickly. On the day the Telegraph published, the ministry put out a blog saying it was "not correct" that Rayner had funded partisan organisations directly. It said councils were responsible for making sure spending met the grant conditions, and that "we have powers in place to recoup money if it is found to have been mis-spent."

Notice what that statement does not say. It does not say the money will be clawed back. It does not say anyone is checking.

Parliament had already asked. In September 2025, more than a year before the Telegraph story, Conservative MP James Cleverly tabled a written question asking which bodies had received Community Recovery Fund money and what the department knew about how it was spent. The minister's answer named no recipients and gave no amounts. It said the money had gone to 20 councils and had paid for community cohesion work, including engagement events and community campaigns.

So Parliament asked where the money went, and the department did not say. A newspaper had to find out instead.

The second pot: £266,221 for Bylines

On 5 November 2026, the Department for Culture, Media and Sport announced the winners of its £6 million Local News Fund, with up to £6 million more to come next year. The fund's stated aim is to "strengthen communities and social cohesion, rebuild social trust, and support local growth."

Bylines Networks Ltd got two grants: £116,335 for a "sustainability programme" across its nine titles, including Yorkshire Bylines, North East Bylines and Bylines Cymru, and £149,886 to build a technology platform for small publishers. That makes £266,221. The Spectator's Andrew Gilligan reports that this makes Bylines the second-largest recipient in the whole fund.

Who are Bylines? Their own titles describe themselves as a trading brand of Bylines Networks Ltd, "separate to, but allied with, Byline Times." Gilligan reports that Byline Times founders Peter Jukes and Steve Colegrave are directors of both companies. The network's founder, Mike Galsworthy, is chair of the European Movement, the campaign to take Britain back into the EU.

And what do they publish? A sample:

  • North East Bylines ran an opinion piece saying Farage's "biggest rally in history" had "flopped".
  • Byline Times and the Bylines Network produced a joint analysis on flood risk in Reform UK's council areas, under the headline "Drowning in Denial".
  • Byline Times runs a regular column called ReformWatch.

They are entitled to publish all of that. What is in question is why taxpayers are paying for it under the heading of "trusted, high-quality local journalism".

Bylines are not alone. The Independent Media Association, the trade body for independent publishers, boasts that seven of its members won more than £630,000 between them in this first round, a tenth of the whole fund.

"An independent panel decided"

This is the same defence as before, from a different department. DCMS says bids were assessed by the Government Grants Managed Service and a steering board of "independent industry experts". But the gov.uk announcement is clear on one point: the final decision was made by DCMS.

The board is co-chaired by Polly Curtis, chief executive of the think tank Demos. Another member, Jaldeep Katwala, is a former non-executive director of the Bristol Cable, which received the maximum grant of £125,000. Taxpayers are entitled to know whether he stood aside when that bid was judged.

Media Minister Ian Murray says the government is "determined not to leave local journalism to collapse." Most people would agree with that. But the panel did not need to be rigged. When the fund's own goal is "social cohesion", and cohesion has come to mean countering "narratives of division", the money flows to whoever is loudest about the division. Right now, the people loudest about it are the ones who think the division is Reform.

What they are doing

Opinion

Put the two stories side by side.

One department hands millions to councils to "rebuild" after riots. Some of it ends up teaching council staff that the danger to their towns is working-class people listening to Reform and the "Tory-Right".

Another department hands hundreds of thousands to a news network allied with Byline Times, whose regional titles mock Reform rallies and track Reform councils. That network is now building the technology that other small publishers will run on.

Both times the label is "cohesion". Both times the government's answer is the same: it wasn't us. The councils chose. The panel chose. Ministers just signed the cheques.

The sums traced so far are not huge. That is not the point. The point is which way the money flows. It never seems to reach anyone who thinks small boats are a problem worth talking about, or that a Reform council might be worth a fair hearing. Under Labour, "cohesion" has come to mean agreeing with Labour, and you are paying for it.

What should happen now

  • MHCLG should publish every council's Community Recovery Fund spending, recipient by recipient, the list Parliament asked for in 2025 and never got.
  • MHCLG should say whether it will use its power to recover the money paid to the Ella Baker School of Organising, and test that training against section 2 of the Local Government Act 1986.
  • DCMS should publish the scoring and the steering board's declarations of interest for every Local News Fund award, starting with Bylines, before a single penny of next year's £6 million goes out.
