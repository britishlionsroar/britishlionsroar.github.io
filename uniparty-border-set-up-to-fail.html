Set Up to Fail: The Uniparty Border That Can't Say No

A judge has ruled that asking a family from Gaza for fingerprints before they come to Britain breaches their human rights. She admitted it raises the risk to national security. It's the sixth ruling of its kind since 2024. The judges, the law and the tribunal were all put in place, and kept in place, by Labour and the Conservatives. Smoke and mirrors.

By Steve English • Independent British writer and editor • November 2026
  • Upper Tribunal Judge Sarah Pinder has quashed two Home Office decisions that kept a mother and five children in Gaza out of Britain until they gave fingerprints and photographs.
  • She accepted that excusing biometrics means “a greater risk to border and national security”, then ruled against the Home Office under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights.
  • It is at least the sixth Upper Tribunal ruling since 2024 telling the Home Office it cannot insist on biometrics up front for families in Gaza.
  • The judges deciding these cases include a former writer for a pro-migrant website and a judge who spent six years as a trustee of a charity supporting migrants in northern France.
  • Labour created the system that appoints them. The Conservatives kept it for fourteen years, and promoted Judge Pinder. Labour now insists Britain stays in the Convention.
  • The Tories' Rwanda scheme cost £700 million and sent four volunteers. More than 200,000 people have crossed the Channel by small boat since 2018, under both parties.
PURPOSE: To show, through one ruling and the system behind it, how Labour and the Conservatives built a border that is set up to fail, and what it would take to fix it.

Fingerprints and a photograph. That is all the Home Office asked for. It is the basic check every visa applicant in the world goes through before being let into Britain. It is how officials confirm that a person is who they say they are, and check them against criminal and terrorist records.

Now a tribunal judge has decided that asking a family from Gaza for that basic check before they travel breaches their human rights.

It would be easy to blame one judge. But she is not the problem. She is the symptom. The law she used, the tribunal she sits on and the system that appointed her were all built and kept by the two parties that have run Britain for a century. Both say they want to control the border. Both have left in place the machinery that stops them.

The ruling

According to GB News, Upper Tribunal Judge Sarah Pinder has quashed two Home Office decisions refusing to excuse a mother and her five children, aged between seven and 17, from giving biometrics before their visa applications were decided. The father ran a medical clinic in Gaza and left in September 2023 to visit his brothers in the United States. He entered Britain in February 2025 and was granted asylum in August.

The Home Office insisted on fingerprints on national and border security grounds. Judge Pinder agreed with the principle. In her own words: “The very fact that an applicant may be excused from providing their biometric information does entail a greater risk to border and national security.”

Then she ruled against the Home Office anyway. She found the department had acted “irrationally” and concluded that “a fair balance has not been struck” and that the Home Secretary “has failed to comply with her positive obligation under Article 8 ECHR.” The Home Office must now reconsider whether to excuse the family from the standard checks altogether.

Read that again. The judge accepted that letting people skip fingerprinting is more dangerous. She acknowledged the concern that the family is coming from a place where terrorism is rife. And she still decided that a foreign family's “right to family life” outweighs the British public's right to know who is coming through the door.

Colonel Richard Kemp, the former commander of British forces in Afghanistan, told GB News: “It's shocking if they will not even undertake basic checks of fingerprints and biometrics. They are the minimum checks.”

She admitted it was riskier. She ruled for them anyway.

Not a one-off. A conveyor belt.

If this were a single eccentric decision, it would be bad enough. It isn't. The Upper Tribunal has ruled against the Home Office's biometrics checks for Gaza families again and again.

  • March 2024: Upper Tribunal Judge Kamara declared it “disproportionate, and thus in breach of Article 8 ECHR” to refuse to decide a Gaza family's visa applications before they gave biometrics.
  • April 2024: Judges Jackson and Gleeson found the Home Office had failed to consider two more Gaza families' situations “in a rational manner” when demanding biometric data.
  • April 2024: Upper Tribunal Judge Smith allowed a further challenge on Article 8 grounds by two families in Gaza.
  • June 2026: Upper Tribunal Judge Norton-Taylor quashed a refusal to excuse biometrics and declared it a breach of Article 8.
  • September 2026: Upper Tribunal Judge Lindsley quashed refusals for two more families and ordered visas issued within two working days if biometrics were excused.
  • November 2026: Judge Pinder.

Each ruling makes the next one easier. Each one is cited by the lawyers in the next case. And the queue is long: around 250 to 300 people in Gaza are estimated to be seeking to join family members in Britain who have refugee status.

The Home Office's own case was clear. In the September hearing, its lawyers argued that biometrics protect the public by fixing a person's identity and checking them against criminal and terrorist records, and that excusing them carries a greater national security risk. The tribunal heard the argument. The tribunal overruled it. Again.

Why the checks matter

This month, a Birmingham jury convicted Mohammed Alfatih, also known as Mohammed Naim, of a knife attack on a bailiff outside his home in Kings Heath. Alfatih is reported to have been a bodyguard to the leader of Hamas. He was granted asylum in Britain in 2015 and British citizenship in 2020. He is wanted in Germany over an alleged plot to murder members of the Jewish community there. He had been under surveillance by a counter-terrorism unit.

Labour's response was to blame the Tories, calling the case a “staggering example of the loss of border control” under the previous government. Labour is right about that. What it didn't say is that the system that let him in is the one Labour is running today.

That is what happens with the checks. Now the tribunal wants to start waiving them.

Who sits in judgment

Judge Pinder is a good example of the type of person deciding these cases.

Sarah Pinder was called to the Bar in 2006. She became a part-time First-tier immigration judge in 2019 and was promoted to the Upper Tribunal in September 2024. The appointment was made on the advice of the then Lord Chancellor, the Conservative Alex Chalk. Before that she jointly led the immigration and public law team at Goldsmith Chambers.

She also wrote for Free Movement, a website for immigration and asylum lawyers founded by the barrister Colin Yeo. Her articles there run from 2011 to 2022, so she was still writing for it three years after becoming a judge. In one, she called immigration detention centres “truly abhorrent”. GB News reports she also joined the Bail for Immigration Detainees rota, representing detainees seeking bail free of charge.

Her rulings since joining the Upper Tribunal fit the pattern. In January 2025 she blocked the deportation of a Zimbabwean man jailed for child sex offences, accepting that he would face “substantial hostility” in Zimbabwe. She also blocked the deportation of a Jamaican drug dealer, ruling it would be “unduly harsh” on his children. Richard Tice, Reform UK's deputy leader, has called on her to “resign or be fired”, saying: “She simply does not agree with the concept of detention, deterrence and strong borders.”

She is not alone. In October 2025, Robert Jenrick, then the Conservative shadow justice secretary, published research identifying 35 judges who, before becoming judges, had advised or acted for free for pro-migrant groups such as Bail for Immigration Detainees. He said he had reported 11 of them to the Judicial Conduct Investigations Office over “activist” content they had posted online.

Rebecca Chapman. Called to the Bar in 1990, she spent four years as a legal officer at a refugee charity before practising. She has been a deputy judge of the Upper Tribunal's immigration chamber since 2015, appointed under a Conservative government, and now also sits in the First-tier Tribunal. Her own chambers profile says she was a trustee of Refugee Legal Support for six years, all of them while she was an immigration judge. The charity tells the Charity Commission it works “in solidarity with people on the move” and supports “advocacy and strategic litigation”. The Daily Mail reported that its website described supporting people “blocked in northern France”: in other words, people waiting to cross the Channel. While a judge, she also wrote a practical guide for lawyers on health and medical claims against removal, and spoke at a seminar on using Article 3 of the Convention to fight removal on health grounds: claims that can end up in front of the same tribunal she sits on. On the bench, she sent back for a rehearing the case of an Iranian man whom a lower judge had found to be making an “opportunistic attempt to fabricate a claim”.

Greg Ó Ceallaigh KC. A deputy Upper Tribunal judge and, like Chapman, a barrister at Garden Court Chambers, and one of the names on Jenrick's list. After his appointment, he reposted on LinkedIn a message by the executive director of Asylum Aid calling for immigration laws passed by the Conservatives to be scrapped. Asylum Aid is the charity that helped lead the legal fight against the Rwanda scheme.

Satvinder Juss. A tribunal judge who was given formal advice for misconduct after standing as a candidate in a local election. GB News reports he stood for Labour.

The judges' own rulebook is plain. The Guide to Judicial Conduct says judges should “avoid extra-judicial activities that are likely to cause them to have to refrain from sitting” because of “a reasonable apprehension of bias”. Part-time judges are “expected to refrain from any political activity which would conflict with their judicial office”. The rule exists. It is rarely enforced, and when it is, the penalty is a letter of “formal advice”.

Smoke and mirrors

None of this happened by accident. Look at who built it.

Labour built the appointments machine. Tony Blair's government handed the job of choosing judges to the Judicial Appointments Commission, a quango no voter can remove. Judge Pinder, Judge Chapman and Mr Ó Ceallaigh were all chosen through it.

The Conservatives kept it for fourteen years. They kept the Judicial Appointments Commission, the Human Rights Act, the Convention and the tribunal. They promised to bring net migration down to “the tens of thousands”. It hit a record 906,000 in the year to June 2023. They promised to “stop the boats”. Their Rwanda scheme cost £700 million and, in more than two years, sent four volunteers and not a single asylum seeker. Now, in opposition, their shadow home secretary says they have “a plan” to leave the Convention and abolish the immigration tribunal. They had fourteen years to carry it out.

Labour is now running the same system. More than 200,000 people have crossed the Channel in small boats since 2018. Under Labour, more than 41,000 crossed in 2025 alone. Labour's own figures show around 76,500 people a year are granted permission to stay in Britain on Article 8 family and private life grounds. Its answer is a new bill that tinkers with the definition of “family” and replaces the immigration tribunal with a new body of adjudicators. But Shabana Mahmood insists Britain stays in the Convention, calling membership “firmly in our national interest”. Same law. Same Article 8. Same court in Strasbourg sitting above it all. New faces, same result.

One party creates the machine. The other keeps it running. Each blames the other when it fails. And it keeps failing in the same direction, every time. It is almost as if neither of them wants it to work.

Labour built it. The Tories kept it. Labour runs it. The boats keep coming.

The way out

The man who exposed the bench is no longer a Tory. Robert Jenrick was sacked from the Conservative front bench in January 2026 and joined Reform UK the same day. His plan, set out as shadow justice secretary, would:

  • return the power to appoint judges to the Lord Chancellor, an elected minister answerable to the public, backed by a vetting committee to check candidates' backgrounds for bias;
  • abolish the Judicial Appointments Commission;
  • give the conduct watchdog the power to sack junior judges, including those in the immigration tribunal, who engage in political activism;
  • create a register of judges' interests, like the one MPs have, and require every part-time judge to be named.

Reform UK: Operation Restoring Justice

Leave the European Convention on Human Rights and repeal the Human Rights Act, so that Article 8 can no longer be used to overrule the Home Office on who comes into Britain. Pass an Illegal Migration (Mass Deportation) Bill placing a legal duty on the Home Secretary to remove anyone without leave to remain.

Without the Convention, there is no Article 8 “positive obligation” for a judge to find. Without activists on the bench, there is no one to find it. Fingerprints would go back to being what they always should have been: the minimum price of entry to Britain, not a breach of anybody's human rights.

The uniparty has had decades. It built the system, kept the system and runs the system, and the system does exactly what it was built to do. Only one party is offering to take it apart.
