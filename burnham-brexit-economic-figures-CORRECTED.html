BREXIT BRITAIN OUTGROWS EU GIANTS — AS BURNHAM CLAIMS BREXIT DID MORE HARM THAN GOOD

UK economic output has grown by 14% in real terms since the 2016 referendum, according to research drawing on official statistics — with exports, business investment and employment also higher.

8 October 2026  |  British Lions Roar  |  Steve English

Prime Minister Andy Burnham's assertion that Brexit has done “more harm than good” is facing renewed scrutiny after an analysis published by Brexit Facts4EU, and reported by GB News, highlighted growth in Britain's economy and several other key measures since the 2016 referendum.

The figures cited in the analysis are striking:

+14%UK ECONOMYReal GDP growth, June 2016–June 2026
+18.4%EXPORTSInflation-adjusted growth, period examined in the report
+16.7%BUSINESS INVESTMENTReal growth by the end of 2025
+2.4MPEOPLE IN EMPLOYMENTIncrease by the end of 2025

The report also cites 5.9% growth in real GDP per head over its chosen comparison period.

UK real GDP growth since the referendum has exceeded that of Germany, France and Italy, according to the report's comparison.

BURNHAM'S CLAIM

Speaking at Labour's September conference, Burnham said:

“Brexit has done more harm than good.”

His comments come amid renewed discussion of Britain's future relationship with the European Union, including the possibility of closer arrangements involving the customs union or single market, and potentially full EU membership.

WHAT THE FIGURES SHOW

The figures present a clear challenge to claims that the UK economy has simply gone backwards since the referendum. Output has increased, exports have grown, business investment is higher and more people are in work.

The comparison with Germany, France and Italy is particularly relevant to the debate. Germany remains the European Union's largest economy, yet the UK recorded stronger cumulative real GDP growth over the period examined by the researchers.

Economic damage and economic disaster are not the same thing. An economy can grow while performing less strongly than it might have under a different set of policies. Equally, forecasts of foregone growth should not be mistaken for evidence that the economy actually shrank.

Those are different claims — and they deserve to be distinguished when political leaders discuss Brexit's record.

THE DEBATE IS FAR FROM OVER

The Office for Budget Responsibility continues to estimate that the UK's post-Brexit trading relationship will leave long-run productivity around 4% below the level it otherwise might have reached. That is a forecast comparison with a hypothetical alternative, not a measurement of a 4% fall in actual GDP.

Supporters of closer EU ties point to trade frictions and potential lost growth. Critics of that argument point to actual post-referendum growth, differences between European economies and the difficulty of proving what would have happened under an alternative political settlement.

What cannot be ignored is the economic record itself: Britain's economy has grown since the referendum, and the latest report says its cumulative growth has exceeded that of three of the EU's largest economies.

If Brexit has been the economic catastrophe so often portrayed, why has Britain's economy outgrown Germany, France and Italy?

That question belongs in the national debate — alongside the forecasts, the trade data and the choices Britain would face under any new agreement with Brussels.

The figures deserve scrutiny. So do the claims made about them.
