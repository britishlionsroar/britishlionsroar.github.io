3,111 Votes, 1.3 Million Followers: The Fake Army Behind Rupert Lowe

Bot-filled comment sections, fan clubs run from Pakistan and Bangladesh, and £112,000 from X. Restore Britain's "uprising" collapses the moment it meets a ballot box.

By Steve English, Independent British writer and editor · 5 November 2026

  • 39% of the accounts commenting on Lowe's X posts were rated fake, against a normal rate of 7–10%.
  • The day after Reform suspended him, fake accounts piled onto his post demanding Zia Yusuf be sacked.
  • "Rupert Lowe" Facebook fan groups with tens of thousands of members were run from Rawalpindi, Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad and Sylhet. One was rebranded "Downloader Codes 2026".
  • Lowe has taken more than £112,000 from X since 2024. In June 2026 he was paid £11,724.97 for five declared hours, which works out at £2,345 an hour.
  • At the Makerfield by-election, Restore won 3,111 votes. That is fewer than the BNP got in the same seat in 2010.
  • In six council by-elections since August, Reform lost by between 2 and 60 votes. Restore's vote was bigger than the gap every time.

PURPOSE: To set Restore Britain's online image against the hard evidence: independent analysis of its X activity, a published investigation into its Facebook fan base, Parliament's own register of Lowe's earnings, and the only election results that count.

On the night of 18 June 2026, Rupert Lowe's movement finally met real voters.

Lowe has 1.3 million followers on Facebook. He is the best-paid politician on X by a distance. His supporters spent the campaign telling each other that Restore Britain was inevitable and the establishment was finished.

Makerfield gave them 3,111 votes, or 6.8%. Andy Burnham took 24,927 and Reform 15,696. Sixteen years earlier, in the same seat, the British National Party scored 3,229. Restore Britain, the movement that was supposedly sweeping the country, could not match the BNP's 2010 total, and it had more votes available to win.

One of Restore's own policy writers, Connor Tomlinson, admitted it was possible the party had sent more canvassers to Makerfield than it won votes. Lowe called coming third "remarkable".

It was certainly revealing. The gap between Lowe's online army and his real one is not an accident. Look closely at that army, and a lot of it isn't there.

39%: The Comment Section That Wasn't Real

In March 2025, the digital-intelligence firm Cyabra analysed a month of activity on Lowe's official X account. It looked at 19,694 profiles that had commented on his posts.

It rated 7,798 of them fake. That is 39%, nearly four in every ten.

Cyabra puts the normal level of fake activity on X at 7–10%. Lowe's comment sections were running at four to five-and-a-half times that rate.

The fake accounts were not just present; they were busy. They commented at the same times as real users, in the same threads, in the same language. On 16 March 2025, they out-commented the real people. More than a third of them (36.4%) had been created within the previous twelve months. Cyabra calls that age profile the classic signature of throwaway accounts built for a campaign and then dumped.

They also stayed on message. Cyabra found three recurring scripts:

  • Praise for Lowe "speaking the truth".
  • Praise for his courage on immigration and crime.
  • A steady drumbeat calling for Lowe to take charge of Reform UK.

"Sack Zia Yusuf": The Day After the Suspension

On 7 March 2025, Reform UK suspended Lowe and referred him to the police. Two female staff had complained of bullying, and the party alleged he had threatened its chairman, Zia Yusuf. Lowe denied everything, and in May the Crown Prosecution Service decided to bring no charges.

The next day, 8 March, Lowe posted: "Nigel Farage needs to take back control of Reform, and sack Zia Yusuf." The post drew 1.5 million views and 40,000 likes.

Cyabra examined the 2,246 profiles that engaged with it. Of those, 869 were rated fake, which is 38.7%. Across wider conversations about Yusuf, 1,018 of 3,163 profiles were rated fake. They pushed near-identical slogans: "Get rid of Zia Yusuf" and "Sack Muhammad Zia Yusuf".

Here is the key finding. Cyabra found that the same network attacking Yusuf was also promoting Lowe. 36% of the fake accounts in the anti-Yusuf campaign mentioned Lowe positively, many proposing him as the man to replace Yusuf. Many of those posts were aimed straight at Reform UK's official account. The campaign generated more than 4,200 engagements and, by Cyabra's estimate, could have reached around 474,000 views.

One man was being built up while his rival was being torn down, by the same accounts, in the same week, in the middle of Lowe's own fight with Reform's leadership.

Cyabra did not identify who was running the network. Nobody has. That is precisely the question Lowe has never answered.

"But Cyabra Got Norway Wrong"

Lowe's defenders will reach for this, so let's deal with it.

In December 2025, the Norwegian fact-checker Faktisk.no tested a separate Cyabra report on Norway's election. Of the twelve example accounts Cyabra had labelled fake, Faktisk found at least four belonged to real people. Cyabra did not answer its questions. Cyabra's methods are its own, and its labels are not proof that every account is a bot.

Fine. Throw out Cyabra entirely if you like. Then explain the Facebook groups, because those come with names, cities and dates.

Made in Rawalpindi

On 30 June 2026, PoliticsHome and the Institute for Strategic Dialogue published an investigation into Facebook groups trading on Lowe's name.

The largest, "Rupert lowe fans", was set up in February 2026 and grew past 28,000 members. Its creators' profiles placed them in Bangladesh. A third admin, added two days later, gave her location as New York.

The content was engagement bait aimed squarely at British anger:

  • Photographs of women in burqas with the question of whether they should be banned.
  • A poll asking whether Muslims should be banned from all public office in the UK.
  • Woolworths nostalgia and calls to reopen local bank branches, mixed in to make it all feel homegrown.

A second group, "Restore Britain – Rupert lowe for PM", had more than 19,000 followers. Its listed admins were in Birmingham and Rawalpindi, Pakistan.

A third, set up on 4 March 2026 as "Restore Britain Rupert lowe for pm", turned out to be the most telling. It gathered over 4,000 members on a diet of border-control posts and anti-Muslim content. On 13 April, an admin based in Islamabad renamed it "Downloader Codes 2026". The political fan club became a shop for pirate TV downloads.

Its main admin was in Karachi. Others were listed in Lahore and New York.

ISD's analyst noted the obvious irony that groups pumping out anti-Muslim content were being run by Muslims. His verdict on the politics was blunter still: in these groups it was "incidental", and the real point was "a vehicle to monetisation". The charity What To Fix told PoliticsHome that groups like these are often "warmed up" on a hot political issue to build an audience of Brits, then sold on.

So tens of thousands of British people joined what they thought was a home-grown movement for their country. They were being harvested as a commercial audience by operators thousands of miles away, using the name of a man who wants to ban immigration from Pakistan.

The investigation does not show that Lowe or Restore ran these groups. But it records no response from either of them, and no sign that Restore has ever warned its supporters off.

Follow the Money

Outrage is not just popular on X. Until September 2026, it was paid.

Under X's Creator Revenue Sharing programme, the platform paid users according to how much engagement their posts drew from Premium subscribers. More replies, likes and reposts meant more money. Elon Musk, the platform's owner, has repeatedly amplified Lowe's posts himself.

Lowe declares every X payment in Parliament's register of interests, with the hours he says he spent earning it. The trend is unmistakable:

Across 2026 to mid-September, Lowe declared £63,764.55 for 75 hours on X. In 2025, his average rate was £377 an hour. From June to early August 2026, it was £1,476. The Guardian calculated he was on course to earn as much from X in 2026 as his £98,599 MP's salary.

Compare that with Nigel Farage, who has made £19,866 from X in total, Richard Tice at £6,953 and Lee Anderson at £6,415.

Lowe says he gives his net MP's salary to charity, and that this is the most expensive job he has ever had. Perhaps so. But the business model is plain. The more anger a post generates, the more X paid for it. That is the same economy that pays a man in Karachi to run a burqa poll under Lowe's name.

X closed the programme on 7 September 2026. Lowe's last registered X payment arrived on 16 September. Nothing has been declared since.

The £2.6 Million Question

When Restore Britain registered with the Electoral Commission on 20 March 2026, its filings showed registered assets of £2,597,825.88. Parties only have to report donations made after registration. So the backers who built that war chest beforehand did not have to be named. For a party that demands transparency from everyone else, that is a convenient blind spot.

  4. Why has Restore never publicly disowned groups pumping out anti-Muslim bait under its banner?
  5. Who funded the £2.6 million Restore held before it had to declare a single donor?

Followers Don't Vote

Rupert Lowe built his brand on authenticity: the plain-speaking outsider, the voice of ordinary Britain, the movement the establishment couldn't stop.

The evidence tells a different story. His comment sections ran at up to five times the normal level of fake activity, and his fan clubs were managed from Pakistan and Bangladesh and flipped into Firestick shops. He has six figures in earnings from the outrage machine. And when real British voters finally had their say, he got fewer votes than the BNP.

A movement is measured in votes, not views. On that measure, Restore Britain's uprising is mostly smoke and mirrors, and some of the mirrors are in Karachi.

Three Motives, One Result

That leaves the obvious question. Why would people in Karachi, Rawalpindi and Sylhet put so much effort into promoting an anti-immigration British party?

They wouldn't, unless it paid. ISD found the politics was "incidental" and the groups were "a vehicle to monetisation". Put that alongside everything else in this article, and Restore Britain looks less like a movement and more like a collection of separate agendas pointing the same way.

The leader. When Reform suspended him, Lowe didn't just walk away. Within weeks he was declaring that "Nigel Farage must never be prime minister" and urging Reform supporters to withhold "your support, your time, your energy, your money or your vote". Months earlier, Elon Musk had touted him as Reform's next leader. That job never came. In my view, what came instead was a grudge with a party attached, plus a social media income that grows with every row.

The online army. These are overseas operators farming British anger for clicks and cash, and fake accounts inflating Lowe's comment sections to four or five times the normal level. Real British supporters are swept along by a following that is partly manufactured.

The old guard. Former BNP figures are turning up in Restore's ranks. Searchlight reported that Eddy Butler, who ran the BNP's East London election campaigns and once challenged Nick Griffin for the BNP leadership, attended the founding meeting of Restore's Epping Forest branch. Julian Leppert, a former BNP councillor, has publicly endorsed the party. More on that in my next article.

Each group wants something different. The result is the same. Restore cannot win, and Makerfield proved it. But it doesn't need to win to decide who does. It only needs a handful of votes where Reform is close:

Six seats, and in every one Restore's vote was bigger than the gap. In Dudley it was nearly 80 times bigger. On the night Labour held Peterlee in County Durham by 18 votes, with Restore on 14.3%, Lowe's response was: "Superb news."

Now scale it up. A September MRP poll reported by The Times suggested that if Restore stood in every parliamentary constituency, Reform's projected seats would fall from 108 to 59, without Restore winning a single seat itself.

Labour and the Greens could not have asked for a better gift. Restore may not win a single seat, but on that projection it could cost Reform forty-nine. So the question for every Restore voter is simple: who are you really voting for?
