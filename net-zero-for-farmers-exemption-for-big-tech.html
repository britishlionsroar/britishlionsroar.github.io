More Electricity Than Britain's Winter Peak.
More Water Than 235,000 Homes.

Labour's proposed data centres — in the Government's own numbers. British farmers are told to shrink their herds to save the planet. Big Tech is fast-tracked instead.

The British Lions Roar  ·  Investigative Opinion Journalism  ·  Editor: Steve English

PURPOSE: To lay out, using the Government's own published regulations, Ofgem's own connection-queue data, and the water industry's own submitted evidence to Parliament, the gap between what Net Zero demands of British farmers and families and what it quietly permits for the data centre industry. Every figure below is sourced. The conclusion is ours.

British dairy farmers are being told, by the Government's own climate advisers, to shrink their herds. British families are being told to cut back on meat and milk. Meanwhile, a few miles down the road, Labour has spent the past year building the fastest planning and grid-access route this country has ever offered to a single industry — and pointing it squarely at data centres.

This isn't a comparison of acreage or a guess about carbon. It's what's actually written into law, into Ofgem's own reform documents, and into the water industry's own evidence to Parliament.

What farmers are being asked to give up

The Climate Change Committee's Seventh Carbon Budget — the statutory advice Labour has committed to adopting — recommends a 20% reduction in average UK dairy consumption by 2035, and a 27% reduction in cattle and sheep numbers by 2040. These are framed as the sacrifice needed to hit Net Zero. Farmers, and the families who eat what they produce, are the ones being asked to make it.

What Big Tech is being handed in return

In the same period, the Government has:

  • Designated data centres Critical National Infrastructure (2024) — the same legal category as the energy grid, the water network, and emergency services.
  • Brought data centres into the fast-track NSIP consenting regime (January 2026), letting developers request that their applications be decided by the Secretary of State rather than the local council.
  • Created dedicated "AI Growth Zones" with central government intervention specifically to unblock planning decisions for data centre developers.
  • Directed Ofgem to reform the grid connections queue with data centres named specifically — Ofgem's own words are that reform "applies to data centres specifically, given their outsized presence in the current demand queue."
  • Set out, in a National Planning Policy Framework consultation, plans to let data centres and their on-site gas or battery generation be approved together, faster, through the same regime.

None of this is speculative. It's in the regulations, the consultation documents, and Ofgem's own published reform plans.

The power numbers

Ofgem's connection-queue data shows roughly 140 proposed data centre projects across Great Britain are currently seeking grid connections, with a combined demand of about 50 gigawatts of power — more than the UK's entire current peak electricity demand of approximately 45GW. Separately, the whole demand-side connection queue, data centres included, tripled from 41GW to 125GW in seven months between late 2024 and mid-2025.

Government's own more conservative planning estimate puts realistic UK data centre capacity at 3.3 to 6.3 gigawatts by 2030. Run at a realistic utilisation rate, that alone is roughly equivalent to the whole of London's current annual electricity consumption — and more than three times the entire electricity use of Wales.

The worst-case figure is not a scare number — it is Ofgem's own connection-queue arithmetic. If every project currently in the queue for a grid connection were built and run flat out, it would consume more electricity in a year than the entire United Kingdom does today.

"Britain's proposed data centres have applied for more electricity than the whole country currently uses at its winter peak."

The gas-powered irony

Here is the detail that should carry the most weight of all. Grid connections in the UK now typically take between five and ten years to secure — but a gas connection can be arranged in as little as six months. Faced with that gap, data centre developers are doing the obvious thing: going around the grid entirely.

More than 100 proposed UK data centres have requested gas connections in the past two years, according to industry figures presented at the All-Energy conference — some of them for permanent, not temporary, on-site generation. Future Energy Networks says those requests alone amount to more than 15 terawatt-hours of energy a year: enough to power London for around four and a half months.

This is not a short bridge to a clean grid. Key network upgrades that would free up capacity in major data centre hubs — Slough and Docklands among them — are themselves not deliverable until at least 2029, and some developers have been offered grid connection dates of 2037 or later. Nor is gas itself a quick fix: the turbines and rotors needed to build new gas power stations now carry lead times of five to seven years, meaning even the "temporary" workaround cannot realistically be delivered inside this decade. Ofgem's own Director for cyber regulation and AI, Stuart Okin, has said as much publicly — noting that data centres running on unabated gas "raises an interesting question about what that means for the Clean Power 2030 target." That is Ofgem's own regulator questioning whether the Government's flagship clean power target survives contact with the industry it is fast-tracking.

Put that next to the methane argument made earlier in this piece. British livestock produce methane that cycles out of the atmosphere within roughly twelve years, as part of a biogenic loop that adds no new permanent carbon. The gas turbines now being built beside data centres to dodge the grid queue produce fossil CO2 that can persist in the atmosphere for centuries, on infrastructure some operators intend to run permanently, not as a bridge to anything. Farmers are told to cut the twelve-year gas. The Government's own AI Growth Zones are, by its own regulator's admission, driving the construction of new fossil-fuelled power stations that call the 2030 clean power target itself into question.

The water numbers — and why this matters right now

This is the sharper story, given the hosepipe bans currently in force across the south and east of England. Data centres in England currently use approximately 6.6 million litres of drinking water a day — equivalent to 20,000 average homes, according to evidence submitted to Parliament. With the Government's ambition to treble data centre capacity by 2030, that could rise to 19.8 million litres a day, equivalent to 60,000 homes. On the water industry's own worst-case modelling — if new data centres perform no better than the least efficient facilities already operating — peak daily use could reach 77 million litres, equivalent to the daily usage of more than 235,000 British homes.

Individual sites are already asking for far more than the average. In the Affinity Water region around Slough, some proposed data centres have requested up to 3 million litres of water a day — roughly the daily consumption of 3,500 homes. Around 125 data centres are proposed or under construction in that region alone, much of it under an active hosepipe ban this summer.

More than three-quarters of the UK's data centres, and 84% of those now proposed, sit in the water-stressed south and east of England — the same regions facing restrictions on households right now. England already faces a forecast shortfall of five billion litres of public water supply a day by 2055, before data centre growth is even factored into the calculation. Data centres are not currently required to disclose how much water they use.

And here is the part that should make every household under a hosepipe ban furious: because data centres were classed Critical National Infrastructure in 2024, water companies say they have little power to restrict their supply during shortages. During this summer's hosepipe bans, restrictions fell on households — not on the data centres, which continue to draw drinking-quality water under the same rules that protect the water network itself.

The regulator's own verdict: the government didn't do the maths

This isn't campaigners' speculation. In July 2026, Water UK — the trade body representing every water company in Britain and Northern Ireland — issued a written briefing to MPs stating plainly that the Government's water forecasts are "fatally flawed" and "explicitly exclude" data centres. The briefing names the specific documents at fault: the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology's "Delivering AI Growth Zones" plan, its AI for Science Strategy, and its AI Opportunities Action Plan — all three, Water UK says, make no mention of water whatsoever. The Environment Agency's own most recent National Framework for Water Resources, published in June 2025, was found to have the same omission.

Water UK's own words to Parliament: "There appears to be an assumption that the country will always have enough water for its economic needs. Nothing could be further from the truth."

The trade body's proposed fix makes the priorities explicit. Rather than pausing data centre growth in water-stressed regions, Water UK's briefing proposes temporarily suspending water abstraction limits in those regions — specifically so data centres can keep drawing supply through heatwaves and hosepipe bans that restrict everyone else.

So the sequence is this: Labour classed data centres Critical National Infrastructure in 2024, built an entire growth strategy around them without a single mention of the water they need, and is now being told by the water industry's own trade body — after the fact — that the numbers don't add up. The industry that stands to profit from data centre growth is the one flagging the hole in the Government's plan. Labour never noticed it itself.

The carbon comparison — and why not all CO2e is equal

The emissions case is separate from the power and water case, and it deserves its own reckoning rather than a straight "tonnes versus tonnes" headline.

On a whole-product footprinting basis, UK milk carries a footprint of roughly 1.25kg CO2e per litre, and Defra's own figures put average annual production at approximately 8,278 litres per dairy cow — around 10.3 tonnes of CO2e per cow, per year. A 600-to-900-cow dairy operation, the range a 600-acre farm typically supports, therefore produces somewhere between 6,200 and 9,300 tonnes of CO2e annually.

A single 50-megawatt data centre, run continuously on the current average UK grid electricity mix, is associated with in the region of 57,000 tonnes of CO2e a year from grid electricity alone — before construction, servers, or backup diesel generation are counted. That is roughly 74 to 111 times the annual footprint of a 600-to-900-cow dairy farm, and many of the campuses now in planning are several times that size.

But the more important distinction isn't the tonnage — it's what happens to the gas afterwards.

Methane from a cow breaks down in roughly twelve years. The carbon dioxide from a fossil-fuelled data centre can remain in the atmosphere for centuries.

Cattle methane is part of what scientists call the biogenic carbon cycle. The carbon in it was drawn out of the atmosphere by grass through photosynthesis in the first place; when the methane breaks down after around twelve years, it converts back into water and CO2 that re-enters the same cycle it came from. Provided the national herd isn't growing, that methane is not adding new, permanent carbon to the atmosphere — it is carbon on loan, cycling in and out.

Fossil CO2 from grid electricity is different in kind, not just degree. It was locked underground for millions of years before being burned and released. Once emitted, a meaningful share of it — estimates put it at 15% to 40% — remains in the atmosphere for up to 2,000 years. It is new carbon added permanently to the system, not carbon cycling through it.

This doesn't mean livestock methane is harmless — it is a powerful greenhouse gas while present, and farms also produce nitrous oxide and use fossil-derived fertiliser, fuel and feed, all of which count against them fairly. But treating a twelve-year, recycled biogenic gas as scientifically equivalent to a permanent addition of fossil carbon is a comparison that doesn't hold up, and it is precisely the comparison implied whenever dairy herds and data centres are placed on the same footing.

Farming isn't standing still on this, either

While Labour asks farmers to shrink their herds, UK dairy farmers are already actively cutting and offsetting even that twelve-year methane, largely at their own initiative:

  • Rotational and mob grazing — moving cattle through small paddock sections in short, intensive bursts followed by weeks of rest, which stimulates deep root growth and pumps carbon into the soil rather than letting it degrade.
  • Herbal leys — sowing diverse, deep-rooted pasture mixes (chicory, clover, burnet) instead of monoculture ryegrass, driving carbon storage far deeper into the subsoil than standard grass can reach.
  • No-till, direct-drilling — reseeding fields without ploughing, so stored soil carbon isn't exposed to oxygen and released back as CO2.
  • Multi-species swards and nitrogen fixation — clover-based nitrogen fixing cuts reliance on synthetic fertiliser, which otherwise damages the fungal networks responsible for binding carbon to soil.
  • Agroforestry (silvopasture) — planting trees directly into grazing pasture, capturing an additional, separate layer of carbon underground alongside the grass.

None of this appears in the Climate Change Committee's headline recommendation to cut herd numbers by 27%. It is happening anyway, on farmers' own land, at farmers' own initiative — the same farmers being told their sector is the problem, while a fossil-reliant industry with a permanent carbon footprint is fast-tracked past them.

Even the government's own economics warn this could be existential

This isn't only a campaigners' claim. The Resolution Foundation — an independent think tank whose analysis draws directly on Climate Change Committee data — puts the direct cost of decarbonising UK food production at less than £1 billion a year, peaking at £720 million in 2032. Modest, on paper. But because farming already operates on razor-thin margins, the Foundation's own modelling shows that if those costs land on farmers rather than being passed up the supply chain, average farm income would fall by around a fifth — from £43,000 to £35,000 — and one in twenty farms would be pushed into the red. The report's own language for this is "an existential threat" and "existential risk" to the farmers affected.

To be fair to the report, it isn't arguing that farmers should simply absorb this. Its central recommendation is that government should force the cost up the supply chain — mandating supermarkets to source a growing share of low-carbon produce, similar to the mandate already used for electric vehicle sales — specifically so this existential outcome doesn't fall on farmers directly. That is a reasonable, costed solution.

But there is no equivalent mechanism in place, and no sign the supermarket mandate has been adopted. Farmers are left carrying a risk that the Government's own commissioned economics describe as existential, while data centres — designated Critical National Infrastructure, fast-tracked through a bespoke NSIP consenting regime, and prioritised by name in the grid connections queue — face no comparable exposure at all.

Labour's own MPs say the numbers don't add up

This isn't only the water industry raising the alarm. Labour MP Toby Perkins, who chairs the Environmental Audit Committee, wrote to Energy Secretary Ed Miliband warning it was concerning that the Government's Seventh Carbon Budget — the statutory plan setting out how Britain hits net zero — "made no allowance for the impact of data centres" at all.

Miliband's own written reply admitted that the climate impact of data centre expansion on the UK's net zero pathway is "inherently uncertain." The minister responsible for delivering net zero, in writing, could not say what this boom does to the numbers he is legally required to hit.

The scale of that uncertainty has since become clearer, and it isn't reassuring. The Government's own estimate of data centre carbon emissions was revised from 0.142 million tonnes to 123 million tonnes of CO2 over the next decade — a more than hundredfold underestimate, corrected only after the fact.

So the pattern holds across power, water, and carbon alike: Labour built the fast lane, then discovered — or was told, by its own MPs, its own minister, and the water industry's own trade body — that it hadn't done the sums first.

The Verdict

This is not a case of Labour having no climate policy. It has one — and it is being applied with real force to British farmers and British families, who are told to shrink their herds, cut their consumption, and live with less, carrying a financial risk the Government's own commissioned economics call existential.

It is being applied with the opposite force to the data centre industry, which has been designated Critical National Infrastructure, handed a dedicated fast-track planning regime, prioritised in the grid connections queue by name, and left largely unaccountable for the water it draws from a network already short of supply — all while Labour's own MPs and its own Energy Secretary admit they never worked out what the industry does to the climate numbers in the first place.

And here is the detail that should end the argument: farmers are told to cut methane that clears the atmosphere in twelve years, while the same AI boom Labour fast-tracked is now driving the construction of new, permanent, fossil-fuelled gas power stations — built specifically to dodge a clean grid connection queue that Ofgem's own director admits calls the 2030 Clean Power target itself into question. There is no fixed date this ends. Net Zero was supposed to be phasing gas generation out. Labour's own AI policy is multiplying it, with no credible timeline to stop.

Net Zero austerity for farmers. Net Zero exemptions, fast lanes, drought-proof water rights and brand new gas turbines for Big Tech — approved before the sums were even done. That is not a consistent climate policy. It is a policy that bends whenever enough investment is placed on the table.
