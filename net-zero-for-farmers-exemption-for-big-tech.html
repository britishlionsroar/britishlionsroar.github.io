More Electricity Than Britain's Winter Peak.
More Water Than 235,000 Homes.

Labour's proposed data centres — in the Government's own numbers. British farmers are told to shrink their herds to save the planet. Big Tech is fast-tracked instead.

The British Lions Roar  ·  Investigative Opinion Journalism  ·  Editor: Steve English
  • 50 gigawatts. Ofgem's own connection-queue data shows UK data centre developers have applied for a combined 50GW of grid capacity — more than the UK's entire current peak electricity demand of 45GW.
  • January 2026. New regulations let data centre developers bypass local councils entirely and apply directly to the Secretary of State through the fast-track Nationally Significant Infrastructure Projects regime.
  • 3 million litres a day. A single proposed data centre in the Slough area has requested that much drinking water daily — the equivalent of 3,500 homes — while nearby towns sit under hosepipe bans.
  • Up to 235,000 homes' worth. If Labour's ambition to treble UK data centre capacity by 2030 is realised, national data centre water use could rise from 6.6 million litres a day to 19.8 million — or as high as 77 million litres a day on industry's own worst-case modelling.
  • Same legal status as the water itself. Data centres were classed Critical National Infrastructure in 2024 — the same footing as energy, water and emergency services — which is precisely why restrictions fall on households during a drought, and not on the server halls next door.
  • 27% and 20%. The Climate Change Committee's recommended cuts to UK cattle and sheep numbers by 2040, and to average dairy consumption by 2035 — the sacrifice asked of British farmers and families, in the same period this pipeline is being built.
PURPOSE: To lay out, using the Government's own published regulations, Ofgem's own connection-queue data, and the water industry's own submitted evidence to Parliament, the gap between what Net Zero demands of British farmers and families and what it quietly permits for the data centre industry. Every figure below is sourced. The conclusion is ours.

British dairy farmers are being told, by the Government's own climate advisers, to shrink their herds. British families are being told to cut back on meat and milk. Meanwhile, a few miles down the road, Labour has spent the past year building the fastest planning and grid-access route this country has ever offered to a single industry — and pointing it squarely at data centres.

This isn't a comparison of acreage or a guess about carbon. It's what's actually written into law, into Ofgem's own reform documents, and into the water industry's own evidence to Parliament.

What farmers are being asked to give up

The Climate Change Committee's Seventh Carbon Budget — the statutory advice Labour has committed to adopting — recommends a 20% reduction in average UK dairy consumption by 2035, and a 27% reduction in cattle and sheep numbers by 2040. These are framed as the sacrifice needed to hit Net Zero. Farmers, and the families who eat what they produce, are the ones being asked to make it.

What Big Tech is being handed in return

In the same period, the Government has:

  • Designated data centres Critical National Infrastructure (2024) — the same legal category as the energy grid, the water network, and emergency services.
  • Brought data centres into the fast-track NSIP consenting regime (January 2026), letting developers request that their applications be decided by the Secretary of State rather than the local council.
  • Created dedicated "AI Growth Zones" with central government intervention specifically to unblock planning decisions for data centre developers.
  • Directed Ofgem to reform the grid connections queue with data centres named specifically — Ofgem's own words are that reform "applies to data centres specifically, given their outsized presence in the current demand queue."
  • Set out, in a National Planning Policy Framework consultation, plans to let data centres and their on-site gas or battery generation be approved together, faster, through the same regime.

None of this is speculative. It's in the regulations, the consultation documents, and Ofgem's own published reform plans.

The power numbers

Ofgem's connection-queue data shows roughly 140 proposed data centre projects across Great Britain are currently seeking grid connections, with a combined demand of about 50 gigawatts of power — more than the UK's entire current peak electricity demand of approximately 45GW. Separately, the whole demand-side connection queue, data centres included, tripled from 41GW to 125GW in seven months between late 2024 and mid-2025.

Government's own more conservative planning estimate puts realistic UK data centre capacity at 3.3 to 6.3 gigawatts by 2030. Run at a realistic utilisation rate, that alone is roughly equivalent to the whole of London's current annual electricity consumption — and more than three times the entire electricity use of Wales.

The worst-case figure is not a scare number — it is Ofgem's own connection-queue arithmetic. If every project currently in the queue for a grid connection were built and run flat out, it would consume more electricity in a year than the entire United Kingdom does today.

"Britain's proposed data centres have applied for more electricity than the whole country currently uses at its winter peak."

The water numbers — and why this matters right now

This is the sharper story, given the hosepipe bans currently in force across the south and east of England. Data centres in England currently use approximately 6.6 million litres of drinking water a day — equivalent to 20,000 average homes, according to evidence submitted to Parliament. With the Government's ambition to treble data centre capacity by 2030, that could rise to 19.8 million litres a day, equivalent to 60,000 homes. On the water industry's own worst-case modelling — if new data centres perform no better than the least efficient facilities already operating — peak daily use could reach 77 million litres, equivalent to the daily usage of more than 235,000 British homes.

Individual sites are already asking for far more than the average. In the Affinity Water region around Slough, some proposed data centres have requested up to 3 million litres of water a day — roughly the daily consumption of 3,500 homes. Around 125 data centres are proposed or under construction in that region alone, much of it under an active hosepipe ban this summer.

More than three-quarters of the UK's data centres, and 84% of those now proposed, sit in the water-stressed south and east of England — the same regions facing restrictions on households right now. England already faces a forecast shortfall of five billion litres of public water supply a day by 2055, before data centre growth is even factored into the calculation. Data centres are not currently required to disclose how much water they use.

And here is the part that should make every household under a hosepipe ban furious: because data centres were classed Critical National Infrastructure in 2024, water companies say they have little power to restrict their supply during shortages. During this summer's hosepipe bans, restrictions fell on households — not on the data centres, which continue to draw drinking-quality water under the same rules that protect the water network itself.

The Verdict

This is not a case of Labour having no climate policy. It has one — and it is being applied with real force to British farmers and British families, who are told to shrink their herds, cut their consumption, and live with less.

It is being applied with the opposite force to the data centre industry, which has been designated Critical National Infrastructure, handed a dedicated fast-track planning regime, prioritised in the grid connections queue by name, and left largely unaccountable for the water it draws from a network already short of supply.

Net Zero austerity for farmers. Net Zero exemptions, fast lanes and drought-proof water rights for Big Tech. That is not a consistent climate policy — it is a policy that bends whenever enough investment is placed on the table.

50GW Applied For.
45GW Is All Britain Has.
