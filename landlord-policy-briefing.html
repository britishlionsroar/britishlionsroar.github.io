Private Landlords: Reform UK Policies and Current Pressures

Updated policy briefing | Taxation, regulation, rental supply and published commitments

By Steve English · 28 November 2026

At a glance

The private rented sector faces overlapping tax, compliance and tenancy changes. Reform UK's October 2026 economic programme identifies three concrete deregulatory proposals; its 2024 contract also contained significant tax commitments. This update separates explicit recent commitments, historical pledges for which no specific withdrawal has been identified, and matters still awaiting published detail. A proposal is not current law.

1. Section 24: finance costs and mortgage interest

The Conservative government's 2015 legislation restricted income-tax relief on residential property finance costs for individual landlords. From 2017/18 the restriction was phased in; from 2020/21 the finance-cost deduction was replaced by a basic-rate tax reduction. Companies are not subject to the same restriction. The tax reduction has statutory limits and unused costs may be carried forward.

Reform's June 2024 contract, printed page 15, explicitly pledged to scrap Section 24 and restore finance-cost deductibility. It placed this in its first-100-days housing programme. The October 2026 programme was announced as additional to other economic policies, not as a complete replacement manifesto. No specific withdrawal of the Section 24 pledge has been identified in the materials reviewed, but neither has a fresh implementation timetable or costing. The historical commitment should not be described as a verified new pledge or a confirmed reversal.

For a higher-rate landlord with £20,000 rent and £10,000 interest, ignoring other costs and assuming full relief, the tax bill rises from £4,000 under pre-2017 treatment to £6,000 now and an illustrative £6,400 from April 2027. The remaining profit falls from £6,000 to £3,600. Our dedicated Section 24 investigation explains the calculations and their limitations.

Sources: 2015 legislation; HMRC worked examples; 2024 Contract, p.15; October 2026 programme; 2027 finance-cost relief explanation.

2. Property income taxation from April 2027

Two successive Budgets announced increases totalling four percentage points. The October 2026 proposal sets property basic, higher and additional rates at 24%, 44% and 49% from 6 April 2027, subject to legislation. The earlier 22%, 42% and 47% rates were set in Finance Act 2026; the additional increase is proposed in the subsequent Finance Bill. A four-point rate increase is not necessarily a four-percent increase in any individual's bill. Scotland and Wales have relevant devolved powers.

On 16 November 2026, Robert Jenrick listed the increases in rental income tax and additional-home stamp duty among Labour's tax changes. This establishes criticism, not a commitment to reverse either measure. The original post link and a reader-supplied screenshot were obtained; direct automated access to X was unavailable. No specific reversal pledge has been located in the official programme.

Sources: HMRC October 2026 policy paper; Finance Act 2026, section 7; Jenrick’s X statement.

3. Stamp Duty: standard rates and additional-property surcharge

In 2024 Reform proposed standard residential rates of 0% below £750,000, 2% from £750,000 to £1.5 million and 4% above that level. The contract did not expressly set out how the separate additional-property surcharge would operate. The current higher rates in England and Northern Ireland include a five-percentage-point surcharge. Jenrick criticised its rise from three to five points on 16 November 2026, but no explicit pledge to remove it has been located.

For a £300,000 additional dwelling, assuming the higher rates apply:

Portion Rate Liability
First £125,000 5% £6,250
Next £125,000 7% £8,750
Remaining £50,000 10% £5,000
Total £20,000

Under the historical proposed zero standard rate, retaining a flat five-point surcharge would yield £15,000; abolishing both would yield £0. These are illustrations, not confirmed policy outcomes. Scotland and Wales operate separate property transaction taxes.

Sources: 2024 Contract, printed p.5; HMRC higher rates; Jenrick’s November statement.

4. EPC C and mandatory improvements

Measure 18 of the October programme proposes ending compulsory EPC upgrades. The government's energy-performance response sets out its plans for higher minimum standards in the private rented sector. The short Reform pledge does not establish whether existing minimum standards, EPC certificates, exemptions or other energy-related duties would remain, nor how any transition would work. Landlords should continue to follow current rules.

Sources: Measure 18; Government response on private rented energy performance.

5. Making Tax Digital

Measure 53 proposes optional quarterly reporting. Under the government's rollout, qualifying gross self-employment and property income above £50,000 triggers MTD from April 2026; above £30,000 from April 2027; the planned next threshold is above £20,000 from April 2028. These thresholds concern income before expenses, subject to eligibility rules and exemptions.

The Reform announcement is not restricted on its face to landlords, but does not specify whether software, digital records or annual digital returns would remain compulsory. Voluntary quarterly reporting is not equivalent to repeal of the whole system.

Sources: Measure 53; HMRC MTD eligibility and timetable.

6. Renters’ Rights Act and tenant protection

Measure 20 explicitly proposes repeal. The current English regime began changing on 1 May 2026: most assured tenancies became periodic, section 21 no-fault possession ended for the new regime, and landlords must rely on applicable possession grounds. Rent increases, advance rent and other practices are subject to new rules. Grounds remain available for specified situations including sale, occupation, rent arrears and antisocial behaviour, subject to conditions.

Reform's 2024 contract opposed the predecessor Bill while acknowledging inadequate enforcement under existing legislation and proposing stronger monitoring, appeals and redress. This historical position is relevant, but no detailed replacement framework has been identified. Repeal alone does not establish restoration of section 21, fixed terms, changes to section 8, court timetables, rent rules, pet provisions, discrimination protections, registration, ombudsman arrangements or transition for existing tenants. Until any new legislation takes effect, the current law applies.

Sources: Measure 20; 2024 Contract, p.15; Government overview of current English tenancy rules.

7. New supply: brownfield incentives and Industrial Strongholds

In September 2026 Richard Tice announced consultation on tax incentives for newly built brownfield homes, including a ten-year exemption from rental income tax, capital gains tax and stamp duty. The proposal targets new supply rather than the taxation of the existing rental stock. Its eligibility and fiscal design are not yet established in the reviewed material.

On 13 November 2026 Reform announced a proposed Industrial Stronghold covering Wolverhampton, Dudley, Sandwell, Walsall and Cannock Chase. Its published package includes 100% first-year tax relief for new brownfield residential buildings, extensions and substantial improvements. The detailed mechanism and interaction with the September proposals remain unspecified.

Sources: Chartered Institute of Taxation, conference analysis; Official Industrial Stronghold announcement.

8. Rental supply, rents and the independent economic evidence

The OBR's October 2026 outlook, paragraph 3.46, assesses the combined property-tax increases as reducing landlord returns. It anticipates weaker incentives for new entrants, potential exits and greater long-run passing of costs to tenants. It regards demand exceeding supply as more likely than not, creating a risk of sustained rent increases. This is a forecast of possible market effects, not proof of rent increases already caused by the future measure.

The English Private Landlord Survey 2024 found that 31% planned to shrink or sell their portfolios, up from 22% in 2021 and 16% in 2018. Among those planning to reduce their holdings, 66% cited recent tax and legislative changes. The survey predated the July 2024 change of government and cannot measure the effect of later Budgets. Intentions do not necessarily translate into sales; a sale to another landlord does not remove a rental dwelling from the sector.

The policy trade-off matters: lower investment returns can discourage provision, while supporters of stronger tenancy and energy standards seek housing security and better conditions. Both supply and enforcement deserve consideration when evaluating reforms.

Sources: OBR October 2026, paragraph 3.46; English Private Landlord Survey 2024.

9. Research conclusion

The six original questions have now been investigated rather than left as a generic list. Three recent deregulation proposals are explicit. The earlier Section 24 and standard SDLT pledges are documented, with no specific withdrawal identified in this review. The exact treatment of property tax rates and the surcharge remains unpublished in the sources examined, as do important implementation details for the three regulatory proposals. The two brownfield announcements add a distinct new-supply dimension.

This is an independent account of publicly available evidence, not a statement of official party policy beyond the sources cited. The absence of a new announcement is neither proof of abandonment nor a guarantee that an earlier commitment will be delivered.
