While Britain Watches — How Every Other Country Is Doing What Labour Says Is Impossible

Germany flies convicted Afghans to Kabul on charter flights. Pakistan has expelled hundreds of thousands. Iran deports annually at the same scale. Twenty European nations are negotiating directly with the Taliban in Brussels. The EU has designated Bangladesh — one of the UK's top asylum source countries — a safe country of origin. Britain removed 1,087 people in a full year. The constraint is not legal. It is not logistical. It is political.

What This Article Establishes

  • Germany is operating active charter flight deportations of convicted Afghan criminals to Kabul, under the same international legal framework that British ministers cite as a barrier to removal.
  • Pakistan expelled hundreds of thousands of Afghan migrants in 2025 — including registered refugees — demonstrating that mass removal at scale is operationally achievable.
  • Iran deports hundreds of thousands of Afghans annually. Tajikistan is returning registered refugees to Taliban-governed Afghanistan.
  • Twenty European nations formally invited Taliban officials to Brussels in May 2026 to negotiate a mass returns framework — a diplomatic step Britain has not taken.
  • The EU designated Bangladesh, India, Egypt, Morocco, Colombia, Kosovo and Tunisia as safe countries of origin in December 2025, enabling accelerated removal. Bangladesh is among the UK's top asylum source countries. The UK has not made the same designation.
  • Every constraint cited by British ministers to explain the failure to remove — legal, logistical, diplomatic — has been navigated by multiple other nations in the same period.

This article makes one argument: Britain is not uniquely constrained. Every mechanism its government has described as impossible or legally unavailable has been used, in the same period, by comparable nations operating under comparable legal frameworks. The evidence is sourced. The sources are linked.

The Standard Defence and Why It Doesn't Hold

When the British government is pressed on its removal record — 1,087 people removed in the whole of 2025, a 3% return rate on small boat arrivals since 2018 — its ministers reach for a standard set of defences. Deportation is legally complex. International human rights obligations constrain what can be done. Removal to certain countries is not possible under current diplomatic agreements. The courts intervene. The process takes time.

Each of these defences contains a grain of truth. Deportation is legally complex. Human rights law does impose constraints. Diplomatic agreements matter. Courts do intervene. The process does take time.

None of these defences explains what other countries — operating under comparable legal frameworks, subject to the same international conventions, and facing the same diplomatic realities — are managing to do. If the constraint were truly legal, Germany could not fly Afghans to Kabul. If the constraint were truly diplomatic, twenty European nations could not be sitting across a table from Taliban representatives in Brussels. If the constraint were truly logistical, Pakistan could not have expelled hundreds of thousands in a single year.

The constraint is political. This article sets out the evidence.

Germany: Charter Flights to Kabul

Germany resumed deportation flights to Afghanistan in August 2024 — the first such flights since the Taliban takeover in 2021. The flights carry convicted Afghan criminals: foreign nationals who have been tried in German courts, sentenced, and placed on deportation orders. They land in Kabul. Germany operates this programme under the same European Convention on Human Rights framework that applies to Britain. It operates under the same EU legal architecture. It faces the same domestic legal challenges from the same categories of human rights litigants.

Germany has not resolved those legal challenges by finding a way around them. It has resolved them by making the political decision to pursue removal to completion through the courts rather than abandoning the attempt at the first legal obstacle. The flights operate. Convicted criminals are on them. They land in Kabul.

The Legal Parallel

Germany is a signatory to the European Convention on Human Rights. It is bound by the same Article 3 (prohibition of torture and inhuman treatment) and Article 8 (right to private and family life) provisions that British courts apply to deportation cases.

German courts have ruled that deportation of convicted criminals to Afghanistan is lawful in applicable cases. British courts have not been given the opportunity to reach a settled position because the British government has not pursued a sustained programme of Afghan deportations through the legal system to conclusion.

The legal framework is the same. The outcome is different. The variable is political will.

Pakistan: Hundreds of Thousands Expelled

In late 2023 and throughout 2024 and 2025, Pakistan conducted a mass expulsion of Afghan migrants from its territory. The programme was not confined to convicted criminals or to people who had recently crossed the border. It encompassed undocumented migrants, long-term residents, and — critically — individuals who held UNHCR refugee registration cards. Registered refugees were expelled alongside those without documentation.

Pakistan is not a signatory to the 1951 Refugee Convention. It has no domestic asylum law. Its legal constraints on deportation are therefore different from Britain's. This distinction is sometimes used to argue that the Pakistan comparison is inapplicable.

The distinction has limited force as an argument for British inaction. Pakistan's programme demonstrates two things that are directly relevant regardless of the legal differences: first, that removal at scale — hundreds of thousands of people — is logistically achievable when the political decision to achieve it is made; and second, that international pressure, diplomatic protest, and legal challenge from human rights organisations did not prevent the programme from proceeding. The Amnesty International report documenting the Pakistan expulsions is one of the sources cited below. Amnesty condemned the expulsions. They happened anyway.

Iran, Tajikistan, and the Scale of What Is Being Done Elsewhere

Iran deports Afghan nationals annually in the hundreds of thousands. The Iranian programme is long-established, large-scale, and continuous. Like Pakistan, Iran is not a party to the Refugee Convention. Like Pakistan, it operates without the legal constraints that British ministers cite. The comparison is nonetheless instructive on the question of logistics: the physical infrastructure of large-scale removal — transport, processing, border handover — is not beyond the capacity of states that choose to build it.

Tajikistan presents a more pointed comparison for those who argue that removal to Taliban-governed Afghanistan is categorically impossible under international human rights law. Tajikistan is returning registered Afghan refugees — people formally recognised as refugees under the UNHCR framework — to Afghanistan. If refugee status does not prevent return in Tajikistan, the claim that removal to Afghanistan is universally legally impossible requires more careful qualification than British ministers have offered.

United Kingdom — The Contrast

1,087 people removed in the full year 2025. A 3% return rate on small boat arrivals across six years. No charter flight programme to Afghanistan. No designation of Bangladesh as a safe country of origin. No formal diplomatic engagement with the Taliban on returns.

The Home Office's most recent enforcement statistics were published on 18 June 2026. They cover a period during which Germany was flying planes to Kabul, Pakistan was expelling hundreds of thousands, and twenty European nations were in formal negotiations with Taliban officials in Brussels.

Twenty Nations and the Taliban: The Brussels Talks

In May 2026, twenty European nations formally invited Taliban officials to Brussels to negotiate a framework for mass deportation of Afghan nationals from European territory. The nations involved include EU member states operating under the full architecture of European human rights law — the same legal framework routinely cited by British ministers as the primary constraint on Afghan deportations.

The Brussels talks are significant for what they represent diplomatically as much as what they may produce operationally. Engaging the Taliban as a formal negotiating partner on returns does not imply recognition of the Taliban government. It implies a political decision that the mass deportation of Afghan nationals from European territory is a priority sufficient to warrant direct diplomatic engagement with the regime governing the territory to which those nationals would be returned.

Twenty European nations sat across a table from Taliban representatives in Brussels to negotiate the return of Afghan nationals. Britain was not among them. — InfoMigrants, May 2026, reporting on the EU-Taliban Brussels deportation framework talks.

Britain was not among the twenty nations. No British representative attended the Brussels talks. No equivalent bilateral engagement with the Taliban on returns has been publicly announced by the British government. The diplomatic step that twenty comparable nations judged achievable and worthwhile has not been taken by the United Kingdom.

The Safe Countries List: What the EU Did That Britain Has Not

In December 2025, the EU Council and European Parliament agreed a regulation designating seven countries as safe countries of origin: Bangladesh, Colombia, Egypt, India, Kosovo, Morocco, and Tunisia. The regulation entered into force in June 2026. It enables EU member states to process asylum claims from nationals of those countries on an accelerated basis and to remove refused claimants more rapidly, on the basis that the designated countries are generally safe for their own nationals.

Bangladesh is one of the United Kingdom's top source countries for asylum claims. People from Bangladesh arriving in Britain, making asylum claims, having those claims refused, and then remaining — because the removal infrastructure cannot process them — are a significant component of the removal failure documented in this series. Bangladesh is now designated a safe country of origin by the European Union. The UK has not made the equivalent designation.

Safe country designation does not override individual rights to claim asylum or to challenge removal. It shifts the burden and the speed of processing. It enables a state to say, in effect: nationals of this country are presumed not to need international protection; their claims will be processed on an accelerated timetable; and if refused, removal will follow promptly. The EU has made this determination for seven countries, including Bangladesh. Britain has not.

The Argument Britain's Government Will Not Make

There is an argument available to the British government that would, at minimum, be honest. It would go something like this: we have chosen not to pursue aggressive removal at the scale other nations are pursuing it, because we judge the legal and diplomatic costs to be too high and the public benefit to be insufficient to justify them. We acknowledge that other nations are making different judgements. We believe our judgement is correct.

That argument has not been made. Instead, the government has claimed that what other nations are doing is beyond what Britain can do — that the legal constraints are uniquely binding, that the diplomatic relationships uniquely preclude it, that the logistical challenges are uniquely severe. The evidence in this article contradicts every element of that claim.

Germany has the same legal framework. It flies planes to Kabul. Twenty EU nations have the same diplomatic constraints. They negotiate with the Taliban. The EU has the same human rights obligations. It designates Bangladesh a safe country. Pakistan has expelled more people in a year than Britain has removed in a decade.

The constraint is not the law. The constraint is not diplomacy. The constraint is not logistics. The constraint is the political decision, maintained across multiple governments of both major parties, not to pursue removal at the scale the situation requires.
