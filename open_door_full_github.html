The Centre for Social Justice found that 27 young non-EU migrants were hired for every single young British worker since 2020. Over the same period, young British people not in education, employment or training surged by nearly 200,000, bringing the NEET total to almost one million.

At least 450 Islamic charities operate in the UK with a combined known income of over £750 million. Reporting constraints mean the true figure for both number and income is likely substantially higher. This is the sector Labour is now empowering to sponsor refugees.

A February 2026 investigation by The National exposed a sanctioned Hamas fundraising network operating through a matrix of UK-registered charities at a single Wembley address, including an education charity running youth trips with a group named in Parliament as a Muslim Brotherhood affiliate.

The Muslim Brotherhood’s own 1991 internal document — the “Explanatory Memorandum” — describes a strategy of “civilizational jihad” to establish Islam in the West by infiltrating institutions. What the Scheme Actually Does

On 27 June 2026, Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood announced three new refugee sponsorship routes — community, university and employer — to be enshrined in a forthcoming Immigration and Asylum Bill. Under the community route, organisations, charities and civic groups will be able to sign up as approved sponsors, committing to house refugees, support their financial needs, and find them employment during their first year. The scheme is modelled on Canada’s long-running programme and the Homes for Ukraine arrangement that brought 270,000 Ukrainians to Britain after 2022.

Refugees will be identified in partnership with the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees and subjected to security checks before arrival. The scheme will initially operate on a “capped basis” before expanding, with the long-term ambition described as “supporting thousands of refugees each year.” Reports suggest more than 10,000 could arrive by 2030. Sudanese and Eritrean nationals are among those expected to be prioritised.

Mahmood has framed this as a controlled, fair system that reduces dangerous Channel crossings and takes pressure off taxpayer-funded hotel accommodation. On the surface, it has a coherent logic. But the logic depends entirely on the integrity of the “approved community organisations” that will be handed the power to determine who comes, where they live, and who employs them. And that is where the scheme falls apart.

This is the same government whose Public Accounts Committee found a “disturbing picture” of never-ending asylum crisis and “shocking oversights.” The same Home Secretary who offered failed asylum seeker families up to £40,000 to leave voluntarily, then refused to disclose how many accepted. There were still 94,000 asylum claims in the year to March 2026 — more than double pre-pandemic levels — despite a £660 million deal with France to patrol the Channel. 9,852 migrants arrived on small boats in the first half of 2026 alone.

The hotels are expensive and politically visible. The boats are photographically damaging. Community sponsorship makes both problems disappear from the headlines. It does not make the underlying problem disappear. It disperses it — into British homes, British streets, British waiting rooms — funded by organisations the government admits it cannot fully account for.

The Centre for Social Justice published analysis in 2025 using HMRC payroll data that should have ended the political debate on this question. Between 2020 and late 2025, payrolled jobs held by young non-EU migrants rose from 81,500 to 370,900 — a net increase of 289,400, a 355 percent surge. Over the same period, payrolled jobs held by young UK nationals rose from 3,841,500 to just 3,852,300 — a net increase of 10,800, or 0.3 percent. That is 27 non-EU migrants hired for every single young British worker. In the most recent twelve months of that dataset, non-EU under-25s gained 33,200 payrolled jobs while UK-national under-25s lost 32,200. The jobs went one way. The unemployment went the other.

In retail and hospitality — the entry-level sectors that form the first rung of the employment ladder for young Britons — non-EU workers increased by 473,000 over the period, nearly doubling. UK nationals employed in those same sectors decreased by 252,000. A quarter of a million entry-level British jobs, gone to foreign workers, while nearly a million young Britons sit on the NEET register going nowhere.

The housing picture is equally stark. There are 1.34 million households on social housing waiting lists in England as of March 2025 — the highest figure since 2014, rising every year. England has seen a net loss of 24,000 social homes every year since 1991. Local authority housing stock has fallen 67 percent since 1981. In London, some boroughs have seen their waiting lists grow by nearly 880 percent in a decade. The community sponsorship scheme will place refugees directly into this housing market, competing for the same scarce private rental properties and the same oversubscribed social housing that British families have been waiting years to access.

The NHS is running at levels that would have been considered a national emergency a decade ago. The four-hour A&E target — that 95 percent of patients should be seen within four hours — has been missed every single month since July 2015. Not in winter. Not during pandemics. Every month. In January 2026, over 71,500 patients waited more than twelve hours for emergency admission — a figure that is 121 times higher than before the pandemic. In May 2026, approximately 1.56 million people had waited more than four hours in A&E over the previous twelve months alone. The NHS elective waiting list stood at 7.1 million in March 2026. The government’s own target of 78 percent of patients seen within four hours remains unmet.

These are not abstract statistics. They are the lived reality of the British people into whose communities, housing stock, job markets and health services this scheme will place thousands more people annually. Labour does not mention them when it talks about community sponsorship. It talks about Canada’s success rate and integration outcomes. It does not talk about what happens to a housing waiting list or an A&E department when demand is increased without any corresponding increase in supply.

A Sector the Government Cannot Account For

There are estimated to be over 450 Islamic charities operating in the UK, with a combined known annual income of over £750 million. These range from small grassroots organisations to large internationally-connected NGOs. But that figure almost certainly understates the true scale. The Charity Commission for England and Wales does not categorise organisations by religion, only by charitable activity and objective. Many Islamic organisations are registered under broad purposes such as “advancement of religion” or “relief of poverty.” Local mosque trusts, community centres and Islamic education groups frequently operate under these general classifications. The real number of organisations with Islamic governance structures is considerably higher than the 450 publicly identified, and their combined financial flows are correspondingly larger.

This is the sector Labour is now proposing to empower as the gatekeepers of refugee arrivals into Britain.

Britain’s charitable sector is large, lightly overseen, and historically permissive on foreign funding. The Charity Commission requires organisations with income above £25,000 to report overseas donations above that threshold — a declaration, not a prohibition. Unincorporated associations, one of the most common legal forms for community and campaign groups, face no mandatory permissibility checks on their donors at all. As the Electoral Commission has noted, anyone — including foreign nationals and foreign state-linked bodies — can donate to an unincorporated association, which can then pass funds into the wider civic system.

In 2018, the Henry Jackson Society published a detailed report, “How Islamist Extremists Exploit the UK Charitable Sector,” documenting precisely how extremist networks use charitable status to boost credibility, access institutional funding and exploit the mechanisms of religious giving. The report identified the use of charitable registration as a strategic tool for organisations whose true purpose extended well beyond the stated charitable objects. Eight years later, none of the structural vulnerabilities it identified have been closed by legislation.

The British government has known about Muslim Brotherhood penetration of this sector for over a decade. The 2015 Cameron review, conducted by Sir John Jenkins and published under David Cameron, found that Brotherhood-linked groups had established “public-facing and apparently national organisations” in which “membership of the Muslim Brotherhood remained — and still remains — a secret.” The review documented MB-associated groups influencing student networks, charities, mosques and government advisory bodies. Cameron told MPs that the review’s findings supported the conclusion that “membership of, association with, or influence by the Muslim Brotherhood should be considered as a possible indicator of extremism.”

The government’s response was a summary document and a list of intentions including a pledge to “ensure charities that have links to the Muslim Brotherhood are not misused.” Eleven years later, a House of Lords debate in January 2026 saw a peer ask the government point-blank whether it was confident it had “a complete and accurate picture” of MB infiltration into religious, educational and community organisations in Britain. The minister’s answer was non-committal. The Charity Commission, it was confirmed, was “monitoring.”

The Brotherhood’s Stated Plan

This is not speculative. In 1991, a senior Muslim Brotherhood operative in the United States produced an internal strategic document that came to be known as the “Explanatory Memorandum on the General Strategic Goal for the Brotherhood in North America.” It was entered into evidence during the 2007 Holy Land Foundation terrorism financing trial in the United States — the largest such prosecution in American history. The document describes a process of “civilizational jihad” designed to “destroy” Western civilisation “from within” and “eliminating and destroying the Western civilisation from within and sabotaging its miserable house.” The vehicles for this process are listed explicitly: Islamic organisations, charitable bodies, educational institutions, community groups and political engagement structures — all operating within the law while serving a purpose beyond it.

The document is not a relic. It is the operational description of a strategy that European governments, intelligence services and think tanks have spent a decade tracing through exactly the organisational structures the document describes. France found 139 Brotherhood-controlled mosques and 280 affiliated associations. A European Parliament report commissioned by the ECR Group in December 2025 documented how Brotherhood-affiliated organisations had received tens of millions of euros in EU funding through grant systems designed for civil society organisations. The same report identified Islamic Relief Worldwide — a UK-registered charity held up for years as a mainstream humanitarian organisation — as one of the main pan-European structures with Brotherhood connections.

Suite 702, Crown House, Wembley

In February 2026, The National published an investigation into a single office block in north London that makes the abstract concrete. Suite 702, Crown House, near Wembley Stadium, is home to a matrix of UK-registered companies, charities and non-profits — some active, others dormant or dissolved — placed under sanctions by the United States and other countries for links to the Muslim Brotherhood and Hamas.

The key figure is Majid Al Zeer, a dual UK-Jordanian national who founded the Palestinian Return Centre, described on its own website as a non-partisan advocacy organisation. In October 2024, the US Treasury sanctioned Al Zeer as part of a network it described as a “significant international Hamas fund-raising” operation. The Treasury stated that the case “highlights the abuse of the non-profit organisation sector by terrorist financiers through the use of sham charities to generate revenue.” Al Zeer was identified as the senior Hamas representative in Germany and a central figure in its European fundraising.

Also at Suite 702: Zaher Birawi, sanctioned in January 2026 for his role in the Popular Conference for Palestinians Abroad, which the US Treasury describes as a Hamas front organisation whose strategic direction is “controlled by Hamas through the placement of key Hamas-linked figures in major positions.”

Then there is Ahmed Al Nuaimi, owner of six businesses at Crown House including the Cambridge Education and Training Centre — a registered UK charity running Quran-themed outdoor activities for boys aged twelve to sixteen, and trips to Turkey in partnership with the Muslim Association of Britain, named in Parliament by Michael Gove as a Muslim Brotherhood affiliate and described in the 2015 government review as “dominated by the Muslim Brotherhood.”

Lorenzo Vidino, director of the programme on extremism at George Washington University, told The National that the company-matrix structure found at Crown House is a deliberate Brotherhood operational strategy. Organisations are created and dissolved, directors rotate between them, bank accounts are opened and closed. “That allows them to fund activities in a way that allows them two or three degrees of separation and so keep their hands clean.”

Sir John Jenkins, who conducted the Cameron-era review, recognised the same pattern. He described the Brotherhood as an organisation that “relies upon the dissemination of an ideology rather than the dissemination of a set of organisational structures” — quoting Lloyd George on Eamon de Valera: “Like trying to pick up mercury with a fork.”

Islamic Relief and the Limits of Charity Status

The Crown House network is not an isolated case. Islamic Relief Worldwide, registered as a charity in the UK and long presented as a legitimate mainstream humanitarian body, has been sanctioned by both Israel and the UAE. Its American affiliate, IRUSA, announced in October 2025 it was severing all ties with the UK parent, citing “multiple and significant concerns” raised by federal bodies about Islamic Relief Worldwide’s practices. IRUSA warned that its own tax-exempt status was at risk if the relationship continued. In 2026, it sued the UK body over what it described as an illegal campaign to solicit American donors directly.

The December 2025 ECR European Parliament report identified Islamic Relief Worldwide among the main pan-European structures with Brotherhood connections, linked through “overlapping leadership, shared ideology, familial ties” to the Brotherhood’s wider European network. This is an organisation that has operated freely in the UK, raised hundreds of millions of pounds from British donors, and has faced no proscription or equivalent regulatory action from the British state.

Europe Is Acting. Britain Is Not.

The contrast between British inaction and European responses has become impossible to ignore.

The French Interior Ministry’s May 2025 report — seventy-three pages — concluded that the Brotherhood’s primary threat is not terrorism but subversion: a patient, coordinated campaign to infiltrate civic life, charities, schools, mosques and local political structures. It identified Sweden and the United Kingdom as the two European countries where Brotherhood entrenchment is “most advanced and least scrutinised.” France moved: over twenty organisations dissolved, more than €10 million in assets frozen, five hundred imams retrained, a 30 percent reduction in MB-linked events within twelve months.

In January 2026, the French National Assembly passed a resolution calling on the EU to assess a terrorist designation for the Brotherhood. In March 2026, a majority in the Dutch parliament backed a motion calling for a national ban. Germany redirected €2.3 million in federal funds away from MB-linked NGOs and put fifty education centres under surveillance. As of 2026, the US, Germany, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Egypt, Russia, Argentina, Kenya and Bahrain have all designated the Brotherhood or specific chapters as a terrorist organisation.

In the House of Lords, Britain’s government responded to questions about whether it would follow the American designation with its standard formulation: proscription is “kept under review.” The Charity Commission is “monitoring.” The independent reviewer of terrorism Jonathan Hall KC admitted there is a “genuine legitimate concern” about the Brotherhood but warned that the road to a legal definition of extremism “is littered with wreckage.”

The Funding Loophole No One Is Closing

Now consider the mechanics of what Mahmood’s scheme creates.

Community organisations wishing to become approved refugee sponsors will not be required to demonstrate where their funding originates beyond existing charity reporting thresholds. The foreign funding loophole that allows unincorporated associations to receive money without mandatory permissibility checks remains open. The Government’s Representation of the People Bill is working to cap overseas donations to political parties. It is doing nothing equivalent for charitable and community organisations.

An organisation receiving financial backing from Qatar, Turkey or any other state sympathetic to Muslim Brotherhood networks can in principle apply to become an approved refugee sponsor. Its international funding — unless it exceeds reporting thresholds and is voluntarily disclosed — will not be a disqualifying factor. An organisation controlling who is sponsored, where they are housed, who employs them and which community they are embedded in holds extraordinary power over the character and composition of local British communities. That is the explicit purpose of the scheme. And the decision about which organisations receive that power is being made by a government that cannot tell Parliament it has a complete picture of how its own charitable sector has been infiltrated.

We have previously reported on how the Brotherhood uses layers of charitable fronts, shell companies, exchange offices and Islamic social service organisations to launder money and obscure funding chains. We have documented the convoluted routes through which foreign state money reaches ostensibly British organisations. The community sponsorship scheme adds a new dimension: it is not just money flowing through these networks. It is people.

Conclusion: The State Is Opening the Door

George Orwell wrote that the most dangerous lies are not the ones told in bad faith but the ones told with good intentions by people who will not look at what they are doing. The language of “safe and legal routes,” “community integration” and “genuine refugees” is not dishonest in itself. The people who designed this scheme may sincerely believe it will work. But sincerity is not scrutiny, and a scheme designed without scrutiny of the sector it empowers is a scheme that can be exploited. The British public deserves to know that before the doors open.

Britons will not die out, but they will become a minority in their own country, with no control over its future, if the institutions meant to protect that future continue to be captured without consequence. That is the outcome Labour set in motion from 1997 onwards, under Tony Blair, when mass immigration was deliberately embraced as an economic strategy — and it is the outcome the Conservatives later chose to continue.

Based on published policies, Reform UK is the only party proposing changes of sufficient scale to plausibly interrupt or reverse current demographic trends.

The community sponsorship scheme is not a new beginning. It is the next chapter of the same story, wearing a fresher face. The boats are bad optics. The hotels cost too much. So Labour has found a way to move the numbers off the front pages and into British streets — channelled through organisations it hasn’t audited, funded by sources it hasn’t traced, into housing lists that are already breaking, jobs that British young people have already lost, and an NHS that is already on its knees.

The state is not merely failing to guard the door. It is opening it wider, and handing the key to the organisations it has not had the courage to investigate.
