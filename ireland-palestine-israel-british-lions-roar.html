Ireland, Palestine and Israel: When a Foreign Conflict Becomes Part of National Identity

The Ireland–Israel football controversy is only the latest expression of an unusually deep Irish identification with the Palestinian cause. The facts are more revealing than the slogans.

When solidarity with a foreign cause becomes part of national identity, where does political protest end and collective responsibility begin?

A football match that became something else

On 27 September 2026, the Republic of Ireland beat Israel 3–0 in a UEFA Nations League match in Debrecen, Hungary. On the pitch, the result was emphatic. Around the match, almost nothing was ordinary.

For months, campaigners had demanded that Ireland refuse to play. More than 160 Irish sportspeople signed an open letter calling on the senior men's team to boycott the fixtures. Ireland goalkeeper Gavin Bazunu eventually withdrew on moral grounds. The remaining squad held a lengthy meeting and voted to fulfil the fixture.

The Irish players wore black armbands, officially described by the Football Association of Ireland as being in recognition of all lives lost in the conflict. They bowed their heads during the Israeli national anthem. There were no customary handshakes between the teams. Ireland's second scheduled game against Israel had already been moved away from Dublin.

Outside the stadium, the symbolism went further. A campaign led by pub owner Aidan Brown compiled a list of roughly 140 pubs and bars in Ireland and elsewhere that said they would not screen the match. Whatever one thinks of that protest, it demonstrated something difficult to dispute: in Ireland, Israel–Palestine has moved far beyond the normal boundaries of foreign policy.

Ireland really is an outlier

It would be easy to dismiss all this as the work of a noisy activist minority. The polling suggests otherwise.

Research published in 2026 by Miceal Canavan of Ulster University and Liam Kneafsey of Trinity College Dublin found that 76 per cent of Irish respondents said the Israel–Palestine conflict mattered to them either “a fair amount” or “a great deal”. That was substantially higher than the comparison countries in their study.

More strikingly, 53 per cent expressed greater sympathy with the Palestinian side, compared with 11 per cent who expressed greater sympathy with Israel. The researchers describe Ireland's position as exceptional within Western Europe.

That matters because it changes the question. Ireland's attachment to Palestine cannot sensibly be explained simply by a few student activists, celebrities or politicians. It has a broad cultural and historical foundation.

Why Palestine resonates in Ireland

The most persuasive explanation is Ireland's own national story.

For many Irish people, Palestine is interpreted through familiar themes: colonial rule, dispossession, partition, national self-determination and resistance to a stronger power. That analogy is contested and the histories are plainly not identical, but political analogies do not have to be exact to become powerful. They provide a framework through which people understand events.

Canavan and Kneafsey argue that historical narratives rooted in Ireland's anti-colonial experience help explain why the issue carries unusual emotional weight. In other words, Palestine is not viewed only as another international conflict. For many people it echoes Ireland's understanding of itself.

That helps explain why support for Palestinian statehood has become deeply embedded in Irish politics rather than confined to one ideological faction.

From sympathy to state policy

Ireland formally recognised the State of Palestine in May 2024, alongside Spain and Norway. The Irish government said recognition was intended to support a two-state solution based on Israel and Palestine living in peace and security.

Ireland has also pursued legislation concerning trade with Israeli settlements in occupied Palestinian territory. By 2026 the Occupied Palestinian Territory legislation had become one of the clearest examples of Dublin attempting to turn its position into concrete policy.

The relationship with Israel deteriorated sharply. In December 2024 the Israeli government announced that it would close its embassy in Dublin, accusing Ireland of pursuing extreme anti-Israel policies. The Irish government rejected the accusation. Then-Taoiseach Simon Harris said Ireland was not anti-Israel but “pro-peace, pro-human rights and pro-international law”. Ireland kept its embassy in Israel open.

That disagreement is important. Criticism of an Israeli government, support for Palestinian statehood, opposition to settlements and demands for compliance with international humanitarian law are political positions. None is automatically evidence of hostility towards Jewish people.

The distinction that matters

This is where the argument becomes more difficult — and more important.

A democratic society must leave room for fierce criticism of Israel's government and military conduct. It must equally leave room for criticism of Hamas, for discussion of the 7 October 2023 attacks, for concern about Israeli hostages, and for disagreement over words such as “genocide” without treating disagreement itself as moral contamination.

The danger begins when responsibility migrates from governments and armed organisations to people on the basis of nationality or Jewish identity: when an Israeli athlete, an Israeli student or an Irish Jew is treated as though he or she personally embodies the decisions of the Israeli state.

That is why the football episode deserves examination beyond sport. A national team is inevitably a symbol of a country, and sporting boycotts have a long history. But the players on a pitch are not a cabinet, a military command or a legislature. The argument over whether refusing normal sporting courtesies is legitimate protest or misdirected collective blame cannot simply be wished away.

What the antisemitism evidence does — and does not — show

There is evidence of genuine concern within Ireland's small Jewish community. In March 2026, the Jewish Representative Council of Ireland reported 143 incidents it considered antisemitic over a six-month reporting period. The categories included verbal abuse, vandalism or graffiti, threats or intimidation, exclusion or discrimination and direct digital targeting.

Those figures require an important qualification: the incidents were reports to the representative body and had not been independently investigated or adjudicated. Ireland also lacks an official state mechanism dedicated to recording antisemitic incidents. They therefore should not be presented as 143 proven offences.

But dismissing the reports altogether would be equally unjustified. They describe members of a community of only around a few thousand people reporting hostility in workplaces, schools, universities, public spaces and online.

At the same time, the wider survey evidence complicates any claim that Irish support for Palestine is simply disguised antisemitism. Canavan and Kneafsey found very high reported levels of comfort with having a Jewish neighbour. Their conclusion was that the data did not support antisemitism as a general explanation for Ireland's unusually pro-Palestinian outlook.

Both facts can be true. A country can contain a large majority motivated by humanitarian concern, historical identification or political conviction while a smaller number cross the boundary into hostility towards Jews. Serious analysis requires distinguishing the two.

The problem with turning identity into a loyalty test

The more a foreign cause becomes part of national identity, the harder nuance becomes.

If solidarity with Palestine becomes understood as a marker of authentic Irishness, people who disagree can begin to look not merely mistaken but somehow less Irish. That is an unhealthy direction for any pluralist society. National identity should be broad enough to accommodate an Irish citizen who marches for Palestine, an Irish citizen who strongly supports Israel, an Irish Jew worried about antisemitism, and somebody who thinks both Israeli and Palestinian civilians have been disastrously failed by their political leaders.

The same principle works in reverse. Concern about antisemitism should not be used to delegitimise every criticism of Israel. Nor should Palestinian suffering be used to imply collective guilt on Jews or Israelis.

Once those distinctions disappear, politics becomes a contest in moral identity rather than an argument about governments, laws, wars and policies.

The football match was a useful mirror

Ireland's match against Israel did not create these tensions. It exposed them.

The FAI itself had already wrestled with the issue. In July, delegates voted 75 to 32, with three abstentions, to endorse fulfilling Ireland's Nations League fixtures. The organisation acknowledged the strength of feeling over Palestine while maintaining that Ireland should meet its sporting obligations.

By September, the pressure had reached the players themselves. One withdrew. Others debated whether to play. The team eventually took the field while making visible gestures of protest.

People can reasonably disagree about each of those decisions. What is harder to dispute is how extraordinary it is for a single international conflict to penetrate Irish public life so deeply that footballers, publicans, broadcasters, politicians, universities, cultural figures and ordinary supporters repeatedly find themselves asked to declare a position.

Ireland does not need less concern. It needs sharper distinctions.

There is nothing inherently strange about Irish people caring about Palestinian civilians. Gaza has produced immense human suffering, and governments are properly subject to scrutiny over war, humanitarian law and the treatment of civilians.

Nor is there anything improper about remembering the Israeli civilians murdered and abducted on 7 October 2023, condemning Hamas, worrying about antisemitism, or challenging claims made about Israel. None of those positions requires indifference to Palestinians.

The revealing feature of the Irish debate is therefore not simply that Ireland is “pro-Palestinian”. The evidence shows that it is. The more consequential question is whether an international cause has become so entwined with national self-understanding that dissent, complexity and individual responsibility become harder to maintain.

Ireland's historical experience can explain its empathy with Palestinians. It cannot decide every contemporary argument in advance. History is a lens, not a verdict.

That is the distinction worth protecting: criticise governments, challenge policies, protest wars and argue passionately about international law — while refusing to turn nationality, ethnicity or religion into collective guilt.

History is a lens, not a verdict. Political responsibility belongs to governments, organisations and individuals — not entire nationalities or religions.
