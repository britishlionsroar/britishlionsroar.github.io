More Than Half a Million EU Nationals Have Become British Since the Brexit Vote

AND THEY ARE ONLY ONE PART OF AN ELECTORATE THAT LOOKS VERY DIFFERENT FROM THE ONE THAT VOTED LEAVE IN 2016.

On 23 June 2016, Britain voted to leave the European Union.

Ten years later, if Britain were ever asked whether it wanted to rejoin the EU, there is an extraordinary fact that tends to disappear beneath the argument:

It would not be the same electorate making the decision.

Millions of people have reached voting age. Millions of members of the 2016 electorate have died. British citizens living overseas now have voting rights that many did not possess in 2016. Large numbers of people have acquired British citizenship.

And more than half a million citizenship grants have gone to EU nationals across the post-referendum period.

That last figure deserves context.

EU Citizenship Grants Surged After the Referendum

Before Brexit, many EU nationals living in Britain had less practical incentive to become British because EU free-movement rights already gave them extensive rights to live and work here.

The referendum and Britain's subsequent departure changed that calculation.

The House of Commons Library has documented the sharp rise in citizenship acquisition by EU nationals after the referendum. Around 75,000 EU citizens acquired British citizenship in the two years immediately following the vote, roughly three times the previous annual rate.

Home Office figures show that the elevated rate continued. In 2019, 48,467 EU nationals were granted British citizenship. In 2020 the figure was 40,637. In 2021 it rose to 67,317.

There were 45,539 grants to EU nationals in 2022, 47,259 in 2023 and 60,632 in 2024. The Home Office recorded 53,004 in 2025.

The latest reporting periods continue to show EU citizenship grants running far above the pre-referendum level. In the year ending March 2026, the Home Office recorded 58,838 grants to EU nationals.

Across the post-referendum period, the cumulative total has passed half a million citizenship grants to EU nationals.

That does not mean half a million automatic new voters appeared overnight. Age, residence, registration and the rules of the relevant poll still matter.

But British citizenship changes something fundamental: it can bring eligibility for Britain's national electoral franchise.

British Citizenship Means National Voting Rights

The Home Office notes that British citizenship brings rights including the ability, subject to the applicable electoral rules, to register to vote in elections and referendums.

So an Italian, Polish, Romanian, French, German or Spanish national who lived in Britain in June 2016 but was excluded from that referendum could subsequently acquire British citizenship and become eligible for a future national referendum, subject to the normal requirements.

That does not tell us how that person would vote.

Being formerly Italian or Polish does not make somebody a guaranteed Rejoin voter. Nationality cannot responsibly be converted into an imaginary ballot paper.

But it establishes something that is measurable:

Hundreds of thousands of people recorded as EU nationals have acquired British citizenship since Britain voted to leave.

And EU nationals are only part of the citizenship story.

The Bigger Number Is Around 1.8 Million Citizenship Grants

British citizenship grants across all nationalities have been running at historically high levels.

There were 159,380 grants in 2019, 130,568 in 2020, a corrected 189,803 in 2021, 175,972 in 2022, 202,041 in 2023, 269,621 in 2024 and 235,782 in 2025.

The Home Office recorded 245,520 citizenship grants in the year ending June 2026, comprising 171,435 naturalisations and 74,085 registrations.

Combined with the earlier post-referendum years, the scale across the decade is around 1.8 million British citizenship grants.

That figure needs an important qualification.

Not every citizenship grant represents an adult immigrant becoming a first-time voter. Home Office citizenship statistics include both naturalisation and registration. Registration includes children and people acquiring citizenship through various statutory routes and connections to Britain or its overseas territories.

So it would be wrong to turn “1.8 million citizenship grants” into “1.8 million immigrant voters”.

The accurate point remains striking: Britain has granted citizenship on a very large scale since the referendum, and British citizenship can confer eligibility for national voting once the other requirements are met.

But Here Comes the Part Many People Don't Know

You do not necessarily have to be a British citizen to vote in a British general election.

The UK Parliamentary franchise includes British citizens, Irish citizens and qualifying Commonwealth citizens.

The Commonwealth category includes citizens of countries such as India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, South Africa, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Malaysia and Sri Lanka, among many others.

A qualifying Commonwealth citizen must satisfy the relevant immigration-status requirements, but British citizenship is not required.

And this is not a new post-Brexit rule. Qualifying Commonwealth citizens were included in the 2016 referendum franchise too.

The official Parliamentary account of the 2016 franchise lists British and Irish citizens resident in the UK, qualifying Commonwealth citizens, eligible overseas British citizens, members of the House of Lords and qualifying Gibraltar electors.

A qualifying Canadian, Australian, Indian or Pakistani citizen living in Britain may satisfy the nationality requirement for a Westminster vote without becoming British. A French or German citizen ordinarily does not qualify for Westminster elections merely by living here.

Cypriot and Maltese citizens can fall within the Commonwealth category because Cyprus and Malta are Commonwealth countries.

Parliament Deliberately Refused to Give the 2016 Vote to All EU Citizens

This question was not overlooked in 2015.

MPs debated amendments to the European Union Referendum Bill that would have broadened the franchise to include EU citizens living in the UK and Gibraltar.

Hansard records the proposal explicitly. Parliament rejected it.

The Government's position was that a decision about Britain's membership of the European Union should use the national Parliamentary-style franchise rather than automatically extending the vote to citizens of other EU member states.

A subsequent Parliamentary answer stated that amendments seeking to include EU citizens had been considered and rejected by both Houses.

So most EU nationals living here did not vote in 2016.

But many who have subsequently become British citizens could qualify to vote in a future national referendum.

There Is No Permanent “EU Referendum Electorate”

This may be the most important constitutional fact in the whole argument.

Britain does not maintain a permanent electoral register labelled “people allowed to vote in EU referendums”.

Parliament legislated for the 2016 referendum and defined its franchise.

If Parliament authorised another referendum, Parliament would again have to determine who was entitled to vote.

It could choose something broadly resembling the Parliamentary franchise.

Or it could legislate for something different.

Britain already operates different franchises for different elections. In Wales, for example, some foreign citizens can satisfy nationality requirements for Senedd and local elections even though that does not give them a Westminster vote.

A future Parliament could therefore propose a broader referendum franchise. That would require legislation. It is not the current law for a referendum that does not yet exist.

There is no evidence that millions of EU nationals have secretly been granted a vote in a future Rejoin referendum. No such referendum franchise currently exists.

But constitutionally, Parliament would decide the rules.

And the 2015 debates prove that the question of whether resident EU citizens should participate has already been fought over once.

Another Change Since 2016: “Votes for Life”

In 2016, many British citizens who had lived overseas for more than 15 years could no longer register as overseas Parliamentary electors.

That restriction has gone.

The Elections Act 2022 removed the 15-year limit and expanded eligibility for British citizens overseas who were previously registered or resident in the UK, subject to the current registration requirements.

So even if another referendum copied today's Westminster franchise, its potential electorate would not be identical to the one used in 2016.

Some British expatriates excluded then can register now.

Demographic and legal change therefore cuts in more than one political direction.

The Electorate Is Enormous — and Constantly Changing

The UK's electoral registers contain tens of millions of Parliamentary electors.

Against an electorate of that scale, half a million people can sound small.

But compare the figure with the result that decided the referendum:

That is why changes in the composition and legal boundaries of the electorate attract attention.

Not because we know how any particular new voter will vote. We do not.

But because the original decision was close enough that the electorate ten years later is politically and constitutionally relevant.

Then Come Ten Years of Young Voters

People who were children on referendum day have since entered the electorate in their millions.

Polling and academic analysis found strong age differences in attitudes towards Brexit, but caution is essential.

A 20-year-old in 2026 is not automatically a Rejoin voter. An older voter is not automatically a Leave voter. And somebody who voted Remain in 2016 does not necessarily support rejoining under whatever terms might be available years later.

Claims that demographic replacement has mathematically “reversed Brexit” therefore go beyond what demographic data alone can establish.

What can be established is much simpler:

An entire decade of young adults has entered the electorate since the referendum.

And Ten Years of Voters Have Died

The reverse is equally obvious.

Millions of people who were alive and eligible in June 2016 have died during the decade since.

Again, assigning their ballots retrospectively would be speculation. Age was associated with referendum preference, but population turnover is not the same thing as counting cancelled Leave or Remain votes.

The relevant point is that both ends of the electorate have moved enormously.

The electorate of 2026 cannot be the electorate of 2016.

Immigration Makes the Picture More Complicated — Not Simpler

One of the most common mistakes in this debate is to take immigration or net-migration figures and treat every migrant as a new voter.

That is wrong.

A French worker does not obtain a Westminster vote simply by moving to Britain. Neither does a Chinese student. Indefinite leave to remain by itself does not automatically create national voting rights.

Nationality and immigration status matter.

But Britain's Commonwealth franchise means some migrants can satisfy the nationality requirement for Parliamentary voting without first becoming British citizens. Other migrants subsequently naturalise and acquire British citizenship.

So the useful question is not “How many immigrants have arrived?” It is “How many people who could not vote in 2016 could satisfy the franchise of a future referendum?”

That number is much harder to calculate. But it is also the number that actually matters.

The EU Settlement Scheme Created a Large Citizenship Pipeline

Millions of EU citizens and their family members have received status under the EU Settlement Scheme.

Settled status itself does not give an EU national a Westminster vote.

That distinction is crucial.

But settled status can provide the immigration position from which an eligible person later applies for British citizenship.

The Home Office has itself linked the post-Brexit rise in citizenship grants to EU nationals with Britain's departure from the EU and the EU Settlement Scheme.

So the half-million-plus citizenship figure need not mark the end of the process. Further eligible EU residents may acquire British citizenship in the years ahead.

Who Are the EU Nationals Becoming British?

The latest Home Office figures provide a useful snapshot.

In the year ending June 2026, the leading EU nationalities receiving British citizenship included:

12,698 — ITALIAN

8,285 — ROMANIAN

8,092 — POLISH

Among non-EU nationalities, the largest groups included 24,851 Indian nationals and 18,489 Pakistani nationals.

Again, citizenship tells us something about legal status and potential electoral eligibility. It does not tell us politics.

A newly British Italian may support Brexit. A British-born voter may passionately support Rejoin. A Commonwealth voter may favour either side.

There is no legitimate shortcut from nationality to ballot choice.

So Who Would Vote in Another EU Referendum?

The only accurate answer today is:

We don't yet know — because Parliament has not legislated for another referendum or its franchise.

If Parliament reproduced something close to the national franchise used in 2016, newly naturalised British citizens could participate subject to the normal rules. Qualifying Irish and Commonwealth citizens could also be included. And considerably more overseas British citizens may be eligible than under the old 15-year rule.

Most non-British EU citizens would still not automatically receive a national vote merely because they lived in the UK.

If Parliament chose a broader franchise, the numbers could change substantially.

That would be a political and legislative decision that Parliament would have to make.

The Question Before the Next Question

Tony Blair's institute has now proposed that Britain set an objective of rejoining a reformed European Union within ten years.

That guarantees renewed argument over free movement, sovereignty, trade, EU law, Britain's former opt-outs and whether the public would support returning.

But before Britain could answer:

SHOULD BRITAIN REJOIN THE EU?

there is another question that deserves an answer:

WHO GETS TO DECIDE?

In 2016, Parliament answered that question deliberately.

Most resident EU citizens were excluded. Qualifying Commonwealth and Irish citizens were included. British overseas voting was more restricted. Hundreds of thousands of today's British citizens were still foreign nationals. Millions of today's adult voters were children. And millions of people eligible then are no longer alive.

Britain may never hold another referendum on EU membership.

But if it does, politicians should not be allowed to treat the franchise as a footnote.

The rules determining who gets a ballot could matter almost as much as the question printed on it.

Ten years after 17.4 million people voted Leave, Britain has changed.

And so has the electorate.
