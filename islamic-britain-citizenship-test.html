Islamic Britain: Don't Have a Working Knowledge of Islamic Customs and Traditions? You Might Fail the British Citizenship Test!

Would-be Britons must have a working knowledge of Islamic traditions. The Tories and Labour both promised to update the test. Thirteen years later, neither has.

By Steve English • Independent British writer and editor • November 2026
  • The official handbook for the British citizenship test teaches applicants about Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr and Eid ul Adha, and lists them under the UK's “customs and traditions”.
  • Every question in the test is drawn from that handbook. If it is in the book, it can be on the screen.
  • The handbook was rewritten by the Conservative-led government in 2013 and sold as putting “British culture and history” at the heart of the test.
  • In 2022 a Tory minister promised a major update within 12 months. In 2024 Labour promised a review by the end of 2025. Neither happened.
  • The Home Office is now lining up a test contract worth £132 million, while still unable to say what the new test will contain.
  • Seven prime ministers have governed under this handbook. Not one has fixed it.
PURPOSE: To set out, from the Home Office's own handbook and Parliament's own records, what the British citizenship test teaches about Islam, who put it there, and how both the Conservatives and Labour promised to change the test and then did nothing.

Want to become British? Then you had better know when Ramadan ends.

Every year, people applying to settle here permanently or become British citizens sit the Life in the UK test: 24 multiple-choice questions, 45 minutes, and a pass mark of 18. Every question comes from one book, the Home Office's official handbook, Life in the United Kingdom: A Guide for New Residents. Whatever is in that book, an applicant can be tested on.

And in that book, under the heading of Britain's “customs and traditions”, sits Islam.

This isn't a rumour or a doctored screenshot. It is the official study material, printed by the Stationery Office and sold to every applicant in the land.

What would-be Britons must learn

The handbook tells applicants that Eid al-Fitr “celebrates the end of Ramadan”, after Muslims have fasted for a month, and that they attend special services and meals to thank Allah for giving them the strength to complete the fast.

It then teaches Eid ul Adha: the festival remembering the prophet Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son when God ordered him to. It explains that many Muslims sacrifice an animal to eat, and helpfully adds that in Britain this must be done in a slaughterhouse.

So the questions write themselves, and the practice-test industry has written them. Which festival marks the end of Ramadan? How long do Muslims fast? Which prophet was willing to sacrifice his son? Get them wrong and you may not get your passport.

Islam is not alone. The same section teaches Diwali, the five-day Hindu and Sikh festival of lights, with a special mention for the celebrations in Leicester. It teaches Hanukkah, the eight-day Jewish festival, and Vaisakhi, the Sikh festival held every 14 April.

All of them are filed under the same heading as Christmas, Easter, Bonfire Night and Remembrance Day, as if a festival that arrived with migration in the last few decades belonged in the same breath as the poppy and the two-minute silence. That is the message the British state gives every person it is about to make British: these are your customs now.

Water meters out. British values in. Such as Eid.

None of this was an accident of the Blair years left lying around. The current handbook is the third edition, rewritten and published by the Conservative-led coalition in January 2013.

The Home Office announced it under the headline “British culture and history at heart of new Life in the UK test”. The Conservative immigration minister, Mark Harper, boasted that he had “stripped out mundane information about water meters, how to find train timetables, and using the internet”, so the new book could focus on “values and principles at the heart of being British”.

Out went the water meters. Out went the train timetables. And in the book the Tories sent to the printers, in the section on British customs and traditions, sat Eid.

That is the Conservative Party's idea of British values: a book that cut how to read your water meter, but kept which prophet was willing to sacrifice his son.

Promise after promise, and nothing

Everyone in Westminster knows the handbook is out of date. They have said so, on the record, for years. Here is what they promised, and what they delivered.

17 May 2022. Tory immigration minister Kevin Foster told the House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee that the handbook had last been significantly updated “nearly 10 years ago”. He promised: “We intend to set our plans for a major update in the next 12 months.” Twelve months came and went. No major update came.

30 June 2022. The same Lords committee demanded an urgent review and said “an end date for its completion should be set by the Government immediately”. No end date was set.

12 November 2024. Labour now in power, Burnley MP Oliver Ryan asked for a timetable. The Home Office replied: “We expect to finalise the next periodic review of the handbook towards the end of 2025.” The end of 2025 came and went. Same book.

12 May 2025. Labour's immigration White Paper, Restoring Control over the Immigration System, promised a “refresh” of the Life in the UK test. What would be refreshed, and why, it didn't say.

6 January 2026. The Home Office opened market engagement for a new Life in the UK test and online learning service, valued at £132 million including VAT, running from May 2027 to May 2032. A nine-figure contract to deliver a test nobody in government can yet describe.

12 November 2026. Labour's response to its earned settlement consultation confirmed the test stays, and said: “The government is also reviewing the test to ensure it better reflects life in modern Britain.” No detail. No timetable. No definition of “modern Britain”. So how many more questions can we expect on Islamic traditions? And if you pass, will a free prayer mat be presented?

Four promises. Two parties. Not one new page.

Both the Tories and Labour own this

Both the Tories and Labour have promised to update the test. Both have refused to say why or how. And both have failed to do it.

Since the Conservatives took power in 2010, Britain has had seven prime ministers: David Cameron, Theresa May, Boris Johnson, Liz Truss, Rishi Sunak, Keir Starmer and now Andy Burnham. Every one of them has governed under this handbook. Not one has changed what it teaches.

The Tories wrote it and called it British values. Labour inherited it, promised a review and missed its own deadline. Now Labour says it wants the test to reflect “modern Britain”, and won't say what that means. Given that Labour already treats Eid as a British tradition worth examining migrants on, readers can draw their own conclusions about which way “modern” points.

The handbook is not a side issue. It is the one document in which the British state sets out, in its own words, what it expects a new citizen to know about this country. What it chooses to teach is what it thinks Britain is.

The only party taking citizenship seriously

While the Tories and Labour have spent thirteen years promising to look at the test, Reform UK has set out what it would actually do about who gets to be British.

Reform would abolish indefinite leave to remain and replace it with a five-year renewable visa, with higher salary, English language and good-character tests and no access to welfare. It would raise the qualifying period for British citizenship to seven years. On 2 November 2026, after Labour's U-turn on settlement, Reform pledged to review every grant of British citizenship that Labour makes to Boriswave migrants as a result of it.

And in October 2026, Reform UK's Zia Yusuf stood outside HMP Maidstone, a prison holding foreign national offenders, and exposed it for stocking Life in the UK test books bought with taxpayers' money. “Behind these gates foreign criminals are being trained on how to become British citizens,” he said. Reform councillors wrote to the governor demanding the books be removed. The Ministry of Justice said it was “urgently investigating”.

One party treats British citizenship as something to be earned and protected. The other two have spent thirteen years teaching new Britons about Eid, and can't even agree among themselves what the test is for.

If you want British citizenship to mean Britain, there is only one party that will make it so.
